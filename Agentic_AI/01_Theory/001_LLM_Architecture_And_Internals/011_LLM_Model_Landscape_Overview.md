# LLM Model Landscape Overview

## Why This Matters for an Interview, Not Just Trivia

It's tempting to treat "which models exist and what are their specs" as pure trivia — the kind of thing that goes stale within a quarter and isn't worth deep study. That's a mistake for two reasons. First, the specific architectural choices different labs made — RoPE versus learned positional embeddings, dense versus mixture-of-experts, standard multi-head attention versus grouped-query or latent attention, RLHF versus RL-with-verifiable-rewards — are not arbitrary; each one is a response to a concrete engineering or capability problem, and understanding *why* a lab made a choice teaches you the underlying trade-off space far better than reading about the trade-off in the abstract. Second, in a real interview, being able to reason fluently about "GPT-4o versus Llama 3 70B versus a fine-tuned open model" for a specific product requirement is a direct proxy for whether you've actually shipped LLM-backed systems versus only read about them. This chapter is deliberately structured to build both: a tour of what differentiates the major model families conceptually, followed by a practical framework for reasoning about model selection that you can actually deploy in an interview answer.

One important discipline to carry through this whole chapter: the closed, proprietary model families (GPT, Claude, Gemini) do not publish full architectural details, and a meaningful fraction of what circulates about them is informed speculation, leaked information of uncertain reliability, or extrapolation from behavior. This chapter flags speculation explicitly wherever it appears rather than presenting it as fact — that distinction itself is a marker of seniority that's worth demonstrating directly in an interview rather than reciting confident-sounding "facts" that may not hold up.

## The GPT Series

OpenAI's GPT line is the best reference point for tracing how the field's training recipe evolved over time, because the progression from GPT-2 to GPT-4 and beyond tracks the field's growing realization that pretraining alone does not produce a good assistant.

### Pure Pretraining, Then RLHF

GPT-2 (2019, 1.5B parameters at its largest public release) is the model most worth crediting with establishing the now-ubiquitous **decoder-only scaling recipe** that every model discussed in this chapter still follows in its bones: a stack of identical decoder-only transformer blocks (causal self-attention plus a feedforward sublayer, each wrapped in a residual connection), trained purely on next-token prediction over a large, diverse text corpus, with no task-specific architecture or head bolted on for different downstream uses. Before GPT-2, a meaningful share of NLP research still relied on encoder-decoder architectures (as in the original Transformer, aimed at translation) or bidirectional encoder-only models (BERT-style, aimed at classification/understanding tasks via fine-tuned heads); GPT-2's central bet, validated further by GPT-3, was that a single decoder-only architecture, scaled up and trained purely generatively, could be steered to perform an enormous range of tasks purely through the prompt, with no architecture change and no task-specific fine-tuning required. That bet is the foundational assumption underlying essentially every model covered in this chapter, closed or open.

GPT-3 (2020, 175B parameters) scaled that same recipe by roughly two orders of magnitude in parameter count and validated the emerging scaling-law relationship between model size, data, and loss (see file 005 for the Kaplan/Chinchilla scaling-law discussion in depth) at a scale nobody had previously demonstrated. GPT-3 is also the model most associated with popularizing in-context learning at scale — the empirical finding that a sufficiently large pretrained model can perform new tasks from a handful of examples in the prompt alone, without any gradient updates, which was a genuinely surprising capability that motivated much of the subsequent scaling-focused research direction and directly foreshadowed the prompting-centric way essentially all LLMs are used today. Both GPT-2 and GPT-3 were pure next-token-prediction models trained on large web-scale text corpora with no additional alignment step; you queried them by continuing a document, and their "chat" behavior, to the extent it existed at all, was an emergent side effect of the pretraining distribution containing dialogue-formatted text, not something trained in directly.

The shift that produced GPT-3.5/InstructGPT and then ChatGPT was not primarily a scaling change but a training-recipe change: reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where a reward model trained on human preference comparisons between candidate outputs is used to fine-tune the base pretrained model (typically via PPO) to prefer outputs humans rate as more helpful, honest, and safe. This is the single most consequential change in how the field builds usable LLMs — it's the difference between a model that predicts plausible internet text and a model that reliably follows instructions and behaves like an assistant.

GPT-4 continued scaling the pretrained base substantially. OpenAI has never published GPT-4's parameter count or exact architecture, so any specific number circulating publicly — the widely cited "8 experts of ~220B each" MoE rumor being the most persistent example — should be treated as unconfirmed speculation, not fact, regardless of how often it's repeated. What is clear from OpenAI's own public statements is the direction of investment: heavier post-training, larger and more carefully curated instruction-following datasets, more sophisticated RLHF, and increasing use of the model itself to help generate and filter its own training signal. GPT-4o added natively multimodal input/output handling — a single model processing text, image, and audio rather than routing between separate encoder models bolted together — and represented OpenAI's push toward lower latency and cost relative to GPT-4 at comparable quality.

### Reasoning Models: The o-Series

The o-series (o1, and its successors) represents a distinct further shift that is worth separating clearly from the "scale the base model and RLHF it harder" story above: these are models explicitly trained to perform extended chain-of-thought reasoning *at inference time* before producing a final answer, and OpenAI has been explicit that this involves reinforcement learning specifically targeting the quality of that internal reasoning process, not just RLHF over final answers.

What is publicly confirmed: the model generates a substantial internal reasoning trace before its visible answer; this reasoning process is trained via RL to improve, using outcome correctness as a signal on domains like math and code where correctness is mechanically checkable; inference is measurably slower and more expensive as a direct consequence of generating that extra reasoning content; and OpenAI deliberately hides the raw chain-of-thought from end users, showing only a summarized version, citing both a competitive-moat rationale and a safety rationale about not wanting to train the visible reasoning trace to look nice for humans rather than actually reflecting the true computation (which would undermine using it as a monitoring/interpretability signal).

What is speculation, and should be labeled as such if it comes up in conversation: the exact RL algorithm used, whether the underlying base model architecture differs structurally from a standard dense/MoE transformer used in non-reasoning GPT models, and the precise scale of RL compute involved relative to pretraining compute. The general pattern — spend more compute at inference time on deliberation in exchange for better performance on hard reasoning tasks — is often described as moving along a "test-time compute" or "inference-time scaling" axis that's conceptually distinct from the pretraining-scaling axis the field focused on almost exclusively through the GPT-3 era, and this general direction (not the specific o-series implementation) is confirmed by multiple labs now shipping similarly-shaped reasoning models.

## The Llama Series

### Open Weights as an Ecosystem Standardizer

Meta's Llama series matters less because any single Llama model was the most capable model at its release (they generally weren't, especially at the top end) and more because of what open weights did structurally to the rest of the field: once Llama's exact architecture and, crucially, its trained weights were publicly downloadable, it became the default substrate the entire open-source LLM ecosystem built on. Fine-tunes, quantized variants, distillations, and countless downstream products all started from a Llama checkpoint rather than training from scratch, and this had the side effect of turning Llama's specific architectural choices into a de facto industry standard "recipe" that other open model builders (Mistral, Qwen, and many others) converged toward, whether or not those choices were objectively optimal versus every alternative.

### The Llama-Style Block

That "Llama-style block" consists of a specific, now-canonical combination that's worth being able to name precisely. RoPE (rotary positional embeddings) replaces learned absolute or sinusoidal positional embeddings, encoding relative position by rotating query/key vectors in a way that generalizes better to longer sequences than the alternatives available at Llama's release. RMSNorm replaces LayerNorm for the normalization layers, dropping LayerNorm's mean-centering step and only rescaling by the root-mean-square of activations — a small change that's cheaper to compute and empirically works just as well for transformer pretraining. SwiGLU replaces plain ReLU or GELU as the feedforward activation function, a gated variant that adds a learnable gating pathway to the MLP block at some extra parameter cost in exchange for consistently better quality at a fixed compute budget.

None of these three choices originated with Llama specifically — RoPE, RMSNorm, and SwiGLU (or GLU variants generally) were each proposed in earlier papers — but Llama is what packaged all three together into a single widely-adopted reference architecture, and "Llama-style" has become the informal shorthand for exactly this combination in both papers and job interviews.

### Attention Evolution: From MHA to Universal GQA

Attention structure is where the Llama series shows a clear, deliberate evolution driven by inference cost rather than training quality. Llama 1 used standard multi-head attention (MHA) throughout. Llama 2 introduced grouped-query attention (GQA) but only in its largest 70B variant, keeping MHA in the smaller 7B/13B models — a sign that at the time, GQA was viewed as a cost-saving measure specifically warranted at the largest, most inference-expensive scale. By Llama 3, GQA was adopted universally across every model size in the family, reflecting the field's broader consensus (echoed independently across most other model families released from roughly 2023 onward) that GQA's KV-cache memory savings are worth its very small quality cost at essentially every scale, not just the largest one.

The mechanics of GQA — sharing key/value projections across groups of query heads to shrink the KV cache, versus multi-query attention's more extreme single-shared-KV-head approach, versus DeepSeek's Multi-head Latent Attention discussed below — are covered in depth in the dedicated attention-variants chapter of this knowledge base rather than re-derived here.

### Tokenizer Growth and Deliberate Overtraining

Llama 3 also made two changes worth knowing specifically because they illustrate a broader industry trend rather than being Llama-specific curiosities. First, it dramatically expanded the tokenizer vocabulary (to 128k tokens, up from Llama 2's 32k), which reduces the average number of tokens needed to represent a given piece of text — directly cutting both training and inference compute per unit of actual content, and improving handling of non-English text and code, which suffer disproportionately from small vocabularies via excessive fragmentation into sub-word or byte-level pieces.

Second, Llama 3 was trained on an unusually large number of pretraining tokens relative to its parameter count — into the multiple-trillions-of-tokens range for even the 8B model — far beyond what the Chinchilla scaling-law "compute-optimal" ratio of tokens to parameters would recommend for a model that size. This is a deliberate, well-understood trade-off often called "overtraining" a small model: Chinchilla-optimal training minimizes pretraining compute cost for a given final loss, but it says nothing about inference cost, and a smaller model trained far past its Chinchilla-optimal token budget can match the quality of a larger Chinchilla-optimal model while being dramatically cheaper to serve at inference time for the entire operational lifetime of the model. Since inference cost, not training cost, dominates total cost of ownership for any model served at real production volume over months or years, intentionally overtraining smaller models has become standard practice across the industry, and Llama 3's token counts are one of the clearest public illustrations of that logic in action.

## The Claude Series

Anthropic's public posture toward architecture disclosure is the most conservative among the major labs, and that's a deliberate choice tied directly to the company's founding thesis rather than an oversight: Anthropic was founded specifically around AI safety research, with "constitutional AI" — training models to critique and revise their own outputs against a written set of principles, reducing reliance on large volumes of human-labeled preference data for every single safety property — as one of its signature public research contributions, alongside a general research emphasis on interpretability and alignment. Given that framing, Anthropic has been explicit that it withholds architectural and training details partly for ordinary competitive reasons (shared with every other lab) and partly because it views capability-relevant technical details as something that plausibly increases misuse or acceleration risk if published, a position that shapes essentially everything publicly known about Claude's architecture being close to nothing beyond high-level descriptions.

What is publicly and reliably known is behavioral and product-level rather than architectural: Claude models are trained around a "helpful, honest, harmless" framing that predates and has continued alongside constitutional AI as Anthropic's guiding alignment philosophy; context window length has grown substantially across generations (from the tens of thousands of tokens in early Claude models to 200k tokens as a standard context length in the Claude 3 generation onward, which is large enough to fit substantial codebases, lengthy documents, or many-shot in-context examples directly in the prompt rather than requiring retrieval); and more recent Claude models expose an "extended thinking" mode, which — like OpenAI's o-series — is a mechanism for the model to generate additional internal reasoning tokens before producing its final answer, trading inference latency and cost for improved performance on tasks that benefit from deliberation, with the amount of thinking budget being a controllable parameter rather than a fixed always-on behavior. On tool use specifically, Anthropic's publicly documented design approach (via its API documentation and published guidance) treats tool calling as a first-class, structured part of the conversation format rather than a special mode bolted onto plain text generation: the model emits a structured tool-use block naming the tool and its arguments, execution happens outside the model, and the result is fed back into the conversation as a distinct message type before the model continues — a philosophy that emphasizes giving the model rich, explicit tool schemas and encouraging multi-step, chained tool use (searching, then reading a result, then calling a second tool based on what it found) as a designed-for capability rather than a workaround. Anthropic has also been publicly vocal about encouraging developers to write clear, well-documented tool descriptions and to test tool-use behavior carefully, framing reliable tool use as much a prompt/schema-design discipline as a model-capability question — a framing worth echoing in an interview if asked how you would improve a flaky tool-calling pipeline: the fix is at least as often in the tool schema and description as in the model choice.

Beyond these points, claims about Claude's specific parameter count, whether it uses a dense or MoE architecture, its exact attention variant, or its exact training data composition are not publicly confirmed, and should be flagged as unknown rather than guessed at in a professional setting.

## Gemini

Google's Gemini family is distinguished by two design choices that Google has been explicit and public about, in contrast to the architectural silence typical of Claude and GPT.

### Natively Multimodal Pretraining

The first is that Gemini was designed and trained to be natively multimodal from the start of pretraining — the model is trained jointly on interleaved text, image, audio, and video data from the outset, rather than the more common earlier-era pattern of pretraining a strong text-only language model first and then attaching a separately-trained vision encoder via an adapter layer afterward. Google has publicly framed this "natively multimodal" approach as producing more deeply integrated cross-modal reasoning than the bolt-on approach, since the model's internal representations were never purely unimodal to begin with — every layer learned in a setting where text and other modalities were mixed together from the beginning, rather than an initial pretraining phase learning only text-shaped representations that a later stage has to retrofit multimodal understanding onto.

### Long Context as a Flagship Capability

The second defining choice is aggressive investment in long-context capability — Gemini 1.5 Pro shipped with a 1 million token context window, with experiments demonstrated even further out, which was, at the time, a substantial jump beyond what other frontier labs were offering and was explicitly marketed around unlocking use cases like reasoning over entire codebases, hour-plus videos, or very long documents in a single prompt without chunking or retrieval.

Supporting that context length in production is a serious infrastructure problem, not just a modeling one: naive transformer attention has quadratic compute and memory cost in sequence length, so context windows at the million-token scale require some combination of architectural techniques (efficient attention implementations, KV-cache compression or offloading strategies) and substantial serving infrastructure investment that Google's TPU-centric infrastructure and years of internal large-scale-serving experience were well positioned to support. The exact combination of techniques Gemini uses to make million-token context economically servable has not been fully disclosed, so specifics beyond "significant infrastructure and likely architectural work went into this" drift into speculation.

Google has also confirmed that at least some Gemini models use a mixture-of-experts architecture — sparse activation of a subset of parameters per token, discussed in more depth in the context of DeepSeek below — consistent with the broader industry trend of frontier labs adopting MoE to scale total parameter count without proportionally scaling per-token inference compute. As with Claude and GPT, though, exact expert counts, routing mechanisms, and per-model configuration details are not fully public.

It's also worth knowing about, and clearly flagging as research rather than a confirmed production detail, a separate line of publicly discussed work out of Google DeepMind called **Mixture-of-Depths**: a 2024 research paper proposing that instead of (or in addition to) routing tokens to different experts within a layer, a model could learn to route tokens through a variable *number of layers*, letting "easy" tokens skip compute-heavy blocks entirely while "hard" tokens get the full depth of the network — a dynamic-compute idea conceptually adjacent to MoE but operating on the depth axis rather than the width axis. This is a published research direction from a lab affiliated with Gemini, not a confirmed component of any shipped Gemini model, and it should be cited in exactly those terms — as an illustration of the kind of dynamic-compute research direction DeepMind is publicly exploring, not as an architectural fact about Gemini itself.

## Mistral and Mixtral

### Efficiency-First, Self-Host-Friendly Positioning

Mistral AI's positioning from its earliest releases has been explicitly efficiency-first: ship open-weight models that get as much capability as possible out of a comparatively small parameter budget, optimized for being genuinely practical to self-host rather than only usable via API. The original Mistral 7B release was notable for outperforming larger contemporaneous open models on many benchmarks at a fraction of the parameter count, and one of the specific architectural techniques it popularized in the open-weight ecosystem was sliding-window attention — restricting each token's attention to a fixed-size local window of nearby tokens, stacked across multiple layers so that, transitively, information can still propagate across a wider effective range through the depth of the network, rather than full quadratic attention over the entire context.

This trades a small amount of the modeling flexibility of full attention for meaningfully lower memory and compute cost at longer sequence lengths, which directly supports Mistral's efficiency-first positioning: it's a concrete architectural lever for making longer-context inference cheaper to self-host, which matters more to an open-weight-focused lab whose users are running inference on their own hardware than to a closed-API lab that can amortize infrastructure investment differently.

### Mixtral: Bringing Sparse MoE to the Open-Weight World

Mixtral extended this efficiency-first philosophy into mixture-of-experts territory and was significant as one of the first widely-used, genuinely capable open-weight MoE models, meaningfully predating most other open labs' MoE releases. Mixtral 8x7B replaces the single dense feedforward block in each transformer layer with eight expert feedforward blocks and a learned router that selects a small subset (2 of 8) of experts to actually process each token; the total parameter count across all experts is large (roughly 47B total parameters), but because only 2 experts run per token, the actual compute cost per token at inference is much closer to that of a roughly 13B dense model.

The routing mechanism itself is simple enough to sketch directly, and the sketch is worth internalizing because the same top-k-over-a-router-softmax pattern reappears, with more experts and extra load-balancing machinery, in DeepSeek-V3 below:

```python
import numpy as np

def softmax(x):
    shifted = x - np.max(x)
    exp = np.exp(shifted)
    return exp / np.sum(exp)

def moe_feedforward(x, router_weights, expert_ffns, top_k=2):
    """x: token hidden state, shape [d_model]
    router_weights: shape [d_model, num_experts]
    expert_ffns: list of feedforward callables, one per expert
    Only `top_k` experts actually run for this token."""
    router_logits = x @ router_weights                  # [num_experts]
    router_probs = softmax(router_logits)
    top_k_idx = np.argsort(router_probs)[-top_k:]        # which experts fire
    top_k_weights = router_probs[top_k_idx] / router_probs[top_k_idx].sum()

    output = np.zeros_like(x)
    for idx, weight in zip(top_k_idx, top_k_weights):
        output += weight * expert_ffns[idx](x)           # weighted sum of the k experts
    return output
```

This delivers the classic MoE value proposition — more total model capacity/knowledge stored in parameters without a proportional increase in per-token inference compute — and Mixtral's release did a lot to demonstrate to the broader open-source community that sparse MoE was a practical, trainable, deployable architecture outside of the handful of large closed labs that had previously been the only ones discussing it seriously.

## DeepSeek

DeepSeek's releases (V2, V3, and the R1 reasoning model built on top of V3) are worth studying closely because they represent some of the most technically detailed and influential open publications on both architecture and training methodology in the last two years, in an industry where openness about training details specifically has generally been decreasing among top-performing labs.

### Multi-Head Latent Attention

Architecturally, DeepSeek-V2 introduced Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA), which compresses the key/value representations into a much smaller shared latent vector before caching, rather than caching full per-head keys and values as standard MHA does or caching reduced-but-still-per-group keys/values as GQA does. The effect is a dramatically smaller KV cache per token than even GQA achieves, which is significant because KV-cache size is frequently the binding memory constraint on how many concurrent requests a server can batch at long context lengths.

The full mechanics of how MLA reconstructs per-head keys/values from the compressed latent at inference time, and how it compares quantitatively to GQA and MQA, are covered in depth in file 004 (`004_Attention_Variants_MHA_MQA_GQA_MLA.md`) of this knowledge base rather than re-derived here. The point worth carrying into this chapter is simply that MLA was a genuine architectural innovation, not just an application of a known technique, and one of the more consequential recent contributions to efficient attention specifically for the KV-cache-bound long-context serving regime.

### Fine-Grained MoE and Auxiliary-Loss-Free Load Balancing

DeepSeek-V3 pushed the mixture-of-experts side further with a fine-grained MoE design — a large number of smaller experts rather than a small number of large ones (256 routed experts plus a shared expert, activating a small subset per token), which gives the router finer-grained control over specialization than a coarser 8-expert-style design like Mixtral's allows. It also introduced an auxiliary-loss-free approach to load balancing across experts. The fine-grained-plus-shared-expert design, and the mechanics of the auxiliary-loss-free bias update shown below, are covered in full depth in file 005 (`005_Mixture_Of_Experts_And_Scaling_Laws.md`); this section recaps only what's needed to place DeepSeek in the broader model landscape.

Standard MoE training typically adds an auxiliary loss term that explicitly penalizes uneven routing (some experts getting overloaded while others are rarely used), but that auxiliary loss is an extra term competing with the main language-modeling objective during training, and getting its weighting wrong can measurably hurt final model quality. DeepSeek-V3's approach instead adjusts a per-expert bias term added to routing scores based on recent load, steering traffic away from overloaded experts through this bias adjustment rather than through a competing gradient-based loss term:

```python
def route_with_bias_correction(router_logits, expert_bias, top_k=8):
    """Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing: a per-expert bias, updated
    slowly outside the gradient graph based on recent load, is added to
    routing scores purely to steer traffic — it does not compete with
    the language-modeling loss the way an auxiliary balancing loss does."""
    adjusted_scores = router_logits + expert_bias        # bias only affects routing choice
    top_k_idx = np.argsort(adjusted_scores)[-top_k:]
    # Note: the bias shifts *which* experts get selected, but the actual
    # combination weights below are still computed from the raw router
    # logits/probabilities, not the bias-adjusted scores.
    router_probs = softmax(router_logits)
    weights = router_probs[top_k_idx] / router_probs[top_k_idx].sum()
    return top_k_idx, weights

# expert_bias is updated after each training step/batch, e.g.:
# expert_bias[overloaded_experts] -= step_size
# expert_bias[underloaded_experts] += step_size
```

The DeepSeek team reported that this achieves good load balance without the quality trade-off the auxiliary-loss approach imposes, since the main language-modeling gradient never has to compromise with a competing balancing objective.

### Training Efficiency Claims

DeepSeek-V3's training cost claims — reportedly on the order of a few million dollars of GPU compute for the full pretraining run, dramatically lower than commonly cited estimates for frontier models from other labs — attracted significant attention specifically because DeepSeek published enough concrete detail (GPU hours, cluster configuration, training recipe specifics) for the number to be scrutinized and largely credited as plausible for the compute actually used, rather than being an unverifiable marketing claim. The efficiency came from a combination of the fine-grained MoE reducing per-token compute, MLA reducing memory pressure, and a number of lower-level systems and numerical-precision optimizations (e.g., FP8 mixed-precision training) documented in DeepSeek's technical reports.

Whether that total captures the *complete* cost picture — data curation, research and experimentation overhead, prior model iterations that informed the final recipe — versus only the final successful training run's direct compute is a fair point of ongoing debate, but the core architectural and systems efficiency techniques themselves are well-documented and were not seriously disputed.

### DeepSeek-R1 and RL with Verifiable Rewards

DeepSeek-R1 is significant for a training-methodology reason that generalizes well beyond DeepSeek specifically: it demonstrated large-scale reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) as a practical, effective paradigm for building reasoning models, distinct from RLHF-for-preferences in an important way that's worth being precise about. RLHF trains against a learned reward model that approximates subjective human preference judgments — which response sounds more helpful, more polite, safer — so there is no ground truth being checked, just a proxy for human taste. RLVR, by contrast, trains against objectively checkable outcomes: did the generated math solution reach the correct final numeric answer, does the generated code pass the test suite, does the generated proof actually verify. This removes the reward model as an intermediate approximation and lets the RL process optimize directly against ground truth on domains where ground truth is mechanically checkable.

DeepSeek-R1's reported results showed that this approach, applied at scale on top of a strong base model, could elicit substantial reasoning-quality improvements. DeepSeek additionally published a distillation recipe showing that reasoning behavior learned this way could be transferred into much smaller dense models via supervised fine-tuning on R1's own generated reasoning traces — a notable result because it suggested the expensive RL-for-reasoning step doesn't need to be repeated at every model size; it can be done once on a capable model and then distilled down cheaply. This general RLVR direction is now understood as one of the key techniques behind the broader industry-wide emergence of reasoning models, alongside OpenAI's o-series and other labs' equivalents, even though the exact training recipes used by closed labs for their own reasoning models remain, per the caveats above, not publicly confirmed to DeepSeek's level of detail.

## A Quick Comparative Frame

The families above cluster along a few axes worth being able to summarize crisply:

| Family | Weights | Signature architectural note | Signature training/product note |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT (OpenAI) | Closed | Undisclosed; o-series adds inference-time reasoning | Heavy RLHF; o-series adds RL-for-reasoning |
| Llama (Meta) | Open | RoPE + RMSNorm + SwiGLU + GQA (universal by v3) | Deliberately overtrained small models per Chinchilla logic |
| Claude (Anthropic) | Closed | Undisclosed | Constitutional AI; extended thinking mode |
| Gemini (Google) | Closed | Natively multimodal pretraining; some MoE use | Very long context (up to ~1M tokens) as a flagship feature |

| DeepSeek | Open (weights) | Multi-head Latent Attention; fine-grained aux-loss-free MoE | Low publicized training cost; RLVR via R1 |

## How to Reason About Model Selection in an Interview

The single biggest mistake candidates make when asked "which model would you use for X" is answering with a model name. That answer is almost always wrong on its face, because it's a moving target (whatever you name will likely be superseded within months) and because it skips past the actual skill being tested, which is whether you can reason about the trade-off axes that determine the right choice for a given system's actual constraints. The strong answer walks through those axes explicitly and only names a specific model, if at all, as an example at the end.

Start with dense versus MoE as an architectural axis, since it's an increasingly common thing an interviewer expects you to reason about explicitly now that MoE models (Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3, some Gemini variants) are mainstream open and closed options rather than a research curiosity. The engineering trade-off is precise: an MoE model gives you more total model capacity — more stored knowledge, generally better benchmark quality at a given *active*-parameter compute budget — because most of its parameters sit idle for any given token, so the per-token FLOP cost tracks the much smaller active-parameter count rather than the large total-parameter count. The price for that is memory and systems complexity, not compute: you still have to hold every expert's weights in GPU memory (or fast storage) regardless of how many are active per token, so total VRAM footprint scales with the large total parameter count, not the small active one, which matters enormously when deciding whether a model fits on the hardware you have. MoE models are also harder to fine-tune well (routing behavior can be brittle to shift under naive fine-tuning, and a fine-tuning dataset too small or narrow relative to the number of experts can leave whole experts undertrained or a large fraction of the parameter space untouched) and harder to serve efficiently at low request volume (the sparse-compute benefit is easiest to realize at the batch sizes and request volumes where routing overhead and expert-parallelism communication costs are amortized well; at very low, bursty traffic, a well-optimized dense model of comparable active-parameter size can be simpler to operate with fewer moving parts). The practical rule of thumb worth stating out loud in an interview: reach for MoE when you need the highest quality obtainable at a fixed inference-compute budget and have the memory capacity and serving-engineering maturity (or are using a managed API where the provider absorbs that complexity) to support it; reach for a dense model when simplicity of fine-tuning, self-hosting, and operational predictability matter more than squeezing the last bit of quality out of a fixed compute budget.

Next, weigh latency versus quality versus cost, because this triangle governs almost every other decision. A customer-facing autocomplete or real-time voice agent has a latency budget measured in low hundreds of milliseconds, which typically rules out large frontier models entirely regardless of their quality ceiling and points toward smaller, faster, possibly distilled or open-weight models served with aggressive inference optimization; a nightly batch job generating detailed reports has essentially no latency constraint and should default to the highest-quality model affordable, since cost per call matters far more than wall-clock time. Cost compounds this: a system making a handful of calls per user session can afford a frontier model's per-token price, while a system firing thousands of LLM calls per user action (agentic loops, per-document RAG re-ranking at scale) needs to take token cost extremely seriously, which is exactly the scenario where smaller or open-weight self-hosted models, or a cheaper model reserved for the bulk of calls with a frontier model escalated to only for hard cases, becomes the financially sane architecture.

Next, open-weight versus closed-API is a genuine trade-off, not a religious preference, and a senior answer names the actual considerations on both sides rather than a blanket preference. Closed APIs win on convenience: no GPU procurement or fleet management, automatic access to the provider's latest quality improvements without any migration work, and generally the highest quality ceiling available at any given time since frontier labs' best models are usually closed. Open weights win when data privacy or data residency is a hard requirement (healthcare, finance, government, or any contractual obligation that data never leaves a controlled environment), when you need deep fine-tuning control (full weight fine-tuning, not just the more limited fine-tuning APIs closed providers expose), when you're operating at a scale where self-hosting is genuinely cheaper than API costs (a real crossover point that depends on volume and utilization, not a fixed rule), or when vendor lock-in and long-term pricing/availability risk from depending entirely on one provider's roadmap is itself a risk the business wants to avoid. The honest trade-off on the open-weight side is real ops burden — you now own GPU capacity planning, serving infrastructure (vLLM/TGI-style serving stacks), model upgrade cycles, and quality monitoring that a closed API abstracts away entirely.

Context length needs should be sized to the actual task, not maximized by default — a very large context window sounds strictly better but in practice costs more per call (most providers charge per input token, so stuffing unnecessary context into every call has a real, compounding cost) and, empirically, models still don't attend perfectly uniformly across extremely long contexts (the "lost in the middle" phenomenon, where retrieval accuracy for information degrades for content placed in the middle of a very long context relative to the beginning or end, remains a real effect worth mentioning). This is exactly the trade-off that motivates RAG as an architecture in the first place: retrieving a small number of highly relevant chunks and feeding only those into a moderate context window frequently outperforms, and is cheaper than, stuffing an entire corpus into a single giant-context call, even when the model's maximum context window would technically fit the whole corpus.

Multimodality needs should be assessed literally — does the task actually require processing images, audio, or video, or is that a "nice to have" the team is defaulting to because the flagship model happens to support it. If a use case is purely text in, text out, there's no reason to pay for or route through a multimodal model's added complexity and cost.

Structured-output and function-calling reliability deserves explicit mention because it's an area with real, measurable differences across providers and directly connects back to the decoding-strategies chapter of this same knowledge base: some providers now offer hard structural guarantees via constrained/grammar-based decoding (OpenAI's `json_schema` response format, for instance), while others rely more on prompting and instruction-following, which is measurably less reliable at the tail. For any system where a downstream parser or tool-execution step depends on the model's output being syntactically valid, this is not a minor implementation detail — it's a first-class selection criterion, and a candidate who brings it up unprompted signals real production experience.

The interview-answer shape that ties all of this together is: restate the actual constraints of the hypothetical system (latency budget, expected volume, data sensitivity, context needs, output format requirements), walk through each axis above against those constraints, and only then land on a class of model (or, if pressed, a specific named model as a current example) as the conclusion that falls out of the analysis — not as the starting point. That ordering is what distinguishes someone who has memorized model names from someone who has actually had to make this decision under real constraints.
