# Unsupervised Learning

## Q1: Explain the K-means clustering algorithm step by step.

**A1:** K-means partitions data into K clusters by iteratively assigning points to nearest cluster centers and updating centers. Initialization randomly selects K cluster centroids. The assignment step assigns each data point to the nearest centroid based on Euclidean distance. The update step recalculates each centroid as the mean of all points assigned to that cluster. These steps repeat until centroids converge or a maximum iteration limit is reached. The algorithm minimizes within-cluster sum of squares, seeking compact, spherical clusters. It's sensitive to initialization and may converge to local minima. K-means assumes clusters are spherical and equally sized, works well for well-separated groups, and is computationally efficient with O(nkd) complexity per iteration where n is samples, k is clusters, and d is dimensions.

## Q2: How does K-means initialization affect results and what methods exist?

**A2:** K-means is sensitive to initial centroid placement, often converging to local optima rather than global minimum. Poor initialization can lead to suboptimal clusters, empty clusters, or slow convergence. Random initialization is simple but inconsistent. K-means++ improves initialization by selecting initial centroids far from each other, reducing the chance of poor local minima. It probabilistically chooses centroids with probability proportional to squared distance from nearest existing centroid. Multiple random initializations with selection of best result helps but increases computation. Forgy method randomly selects K data points as initial centroids. Smart initialization methods significantly improve final cluster quality and reduce the need for multiple runs. The choice affects both convergence speed and final clustering quality.

## Q3: What is the elbow method for choosing K in K-means?

**A3:** The elbow method helps select the optimal number of clusters K by plotting within-cluster sum of squares against K and identifying the "elbow" point where the rate of decrease sharply changes. As K increases, WCSS decreases because clusters become smaller and tighter. Initially, adding clusters significantly reduces WCSS, but beyond the optimal K, improvements diminish. The elbow point represents the trade-off between model complexity and improvement in clustering quality. However, the elbow is often subjective and not always clearly defined. Alternative methods include silhouette analysis, gap statistic, or domain knowledge. The elbow method provides a heuristic starting point but may not always identify the true optimal K, especially when clusters have varying densities or shapes.

## Q4: Explain DBSCAN clustering algorithm and its parameters.

**A4:** DBSCAN groups points based on density, identifying clusters as dense regions separated by sparse areas. It requires two parameters: eps defines the radius for neighborhood search, and minPts specifies the minimum points needed to form a dense region. Points are classified as core points if they have at least minPts neighbors within eps distance, border points if they're within eps of a core point but don't meet minPts, and noise points otherwise. The algorithm starts with a random point, expands clusters by connecting density-reachable points, and handles clusters of arbitrary shapes. It automatically determines cluster count and identifies outliers as noise. DBSCAN works well for non-spherical clusters and noisy data but struggles with varying densities and requires careful parameter tuning.

## Q5: How do you choose eps and minPts parameters for DBSCAN?

**A5:** Choosing eps involves analyzing the k-distance graph, plotting distance to kth nearest neighbor sorted in descending order, and identifying the knee point where distances increase sharply. A common heuristic sets minPts to 2 times the dimensionality for low-dimensional data, or uses domain knowledge. The k-distance graph method sets k equal to minPts and finds where the curve shows the steepest increase. Alternatively, start with minPts around 4-10 and tune eps using the distance graph. Too small eps creates many small clusters and noise, while too large eps merges distinct clusters. Cross-validation isn't directly applicable, but visual inspection of results and domain expertise guide selection. Sensitivity analysis helps understand parameter impact on clustering results.

## Q6: Explain hierarchical clustering and its types.

**A6:** Hierarchical clustering builds a tree-like structure of clusters without requiring a predetermined number of clusters. Agglomerative clustering starts with each point as its own cluster and iteratively merges the closest pairs until one cluster remains. Divisive clustering starts with all points in one cluster and recursively splits clusters. Agglomerative is more common and computationally tractable. Linkage criteria determine how to measure distance between clusters: single linkage uses minimum distance, complete linkage uses maximum distance, average linkage uses mean distance, and Ward's method minimizes within-cluster variance increase. The result is visualized as a dendrogram showing cluster hierarchy. Cutting the dendrogram at different heights yields different numbers of clusters. Hierarchical clustering is deterministic but computationally expensive with O(n³) complexity for agglomerative methods.

## Q7: What are dendrograms and how are they interpreted?

**A7:** Dendrograms are tree diagrams visualizing hierarchical clustering results, showing how clusters merge or split at different similarity levels. The x-axis represents data points, and the y-axis represents distance or dissimilarity. Horizontal lines connect clusters being merged, with height indicating the distance at which merge occurs. Lower merges indicate more similar clusters, while higher merges show less similar groups. Cutting the dendrogram horizontally at a specific height determines the number of clusters, with each vertical line intersection representing a cluster. The optimal cut point balances cluster cohesion and separation. Dendrograms help understand cluster structure, identify natural groupings, and determine appropriate cluster count. They're particularly useful for small to medium datasets where the full hierarchy is interpretable.

## Q8: Explain Gaussian Mixture Models and the EM algorithm.

**A8:** Gaussian Mixture Models represent data as a combination of multiple Gaussian distributions, allowing soft clustering where points belong to clusters with probabilities. Each component has mean, covariance, and mixing weight parameters. The Expectation-Maximization algorithm iteratively estimates these parameters. The E-step calculates posterior probabilities of each point belonging to each component using current parameter estimates. The M-step updates parameters to maximize expected log-likelihood given these probabilities. EM alternates between these steps until convergence. Unlike K-means which makes hard assignments, GMM provides probabilistic cluster memberships and can model elliptical clusters of varying sizes. GMM requires specifying the number of components and can suffer from initialization issues and local optima, similar to K-means.

## Q9: What is the silhouette score and how is it used?

**A9:** The silhouette score measures clustering quality by quantifying how similar points are to their own cluster compared to other clusters. It ranges from -1 to 1, where values near 1 indicate points are well-matched to their cluster and poorly matched to neighboring clusters. Values near 0 suggest overlapping clusters, and negative values indicate misclassification. The score calculates the mean distance to points in the same cluster and the mean distance to points in the nearest other cluster, then combines them. Average silhouette score across all points evaluates overall clustering quality. It helps select optimal cluster count by comparing scores for different K values. Silhouette analysis provides cluster-level diagnostics, identifying well-separated versus problematic clusters. It's useful for comparing different clustering algorithms and parameter settings.

## Q10: Provide an overview of Principal Component Analysis.

**A10:** Principal Component Analysis is a dimensionality reduction technique that finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance in data, called principal components. It transforms correlated features into uncorrelated components ordered by explained variance. The first principal component captures the most variance, with subsequent components capturing decreasing amounts. PCA is performed by eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix or singular value decomposition of the data matrix. It's used for visualization, noise reduction, feature extraction, and removing multicollinearity. The number of components is chosen to retain a desired proportion of variance, typically 80-95%. PCA assumes linear relationships and is sensitive to feature scaling. It's an unsupervised method that doesn't use target labels, making it useful for exploratory data analysis and preprocessing.

## Q11: What is association rule mining and how does it work?

**A11:** Association rule mining discovers interesting relationships between variables in large datasets, commonly used for market basket analysis. Rules take the form "if X then Y" with support measuring how frequently the itemset appears, and confidence measuring how often Y appears when X is present. The Apriori algorithm generates frequent itemsets by iteratively building larger itemsets from smaller ones, using the downward closure property that subsets of frequent itemsets must also be frequent. FP-Growth uses a tree structure for more efficient mining. Lift measures how much more likely Y is given X compared to Y's overall probability. Rules with high support, confidence, and lift are considered strong. Association rule mining helps identify co-occurrence patterns, guide product placement, and understand customer behavior patterns.

## Q12: Explain different approaches to anomaly detection.

**A12:** Anomaly detection identifies rare items or events that differ significantly from the majority. Statistical methods assume normal data follows a known distribution and flag outliers as low-probability events. Distance-based methods identify points far from their nearest neighbors, using techniques like k-nearest neighbor distances or local outlier factor. Density-based methods like DBSCAN flag points in low-density regions. Isolation Forest randomly partitions data and identifies anomalies as points requiring fewer partitions to isolate. One-class SVM learns a boundary around normal data. Autoencoders reconstruct input data and flag high reconstruction error as anomalies. Clustering-based methods identify points that don't belong to any cluster. The choice depends on data characteristics, availability of labeled anomalies, and whether anomalies are point, contextual, or collective.

## Q13: What are Self-Organizing Maps?

**A13:** Self-Organizing Maps are neural networks that produce low-dimensional representations of high-dimensional data while preserving topological properties. They consist of a grid of neurons, each associated with a weight vector in the input space. During training, the neuron closest to an input becomes the winner, and its weights along with neighbors' weights are updated to move toward the input. The neighborhood size decreases over time, allowing global organization followed by local refinement. SOMs create a discretized representation of the input space, useful for visualization, clustering, and dimensionality reduction. They preserve neighborhood relationships, meaning similar inputs map to nearby neurons. SOMs are useful for exploratory data analysis, feature mapping, and understanding data structure, though they require careful initialization and parameter tuning.

## Q14: Explain spectral clustering and when to use it.

**A14:** Spectral clustering uses eigenvalues of similarity matrices to perform dimensionality reduction before clustering. It constructs a similarity graph from data, computes the graph Laplacian, finds eigenvectors of the Laplacian, and clusters points in the eigenspace using K-means. The method can identify non-convex clusters that K-means cannot separate. It's effective when clusters have complex shapes or when data lies on manifolds. The similarity graph can use k-nearest neighbors or epsilon-neighborhoods, and different Laplacian variants exist. Spectral clustering requires choosing the number of clusters, similarity metric, and graph construction method. It's computationally more expensive than K-means due to eigendecomposition but provides better results for non-spherical clusters. The method works well when clusters are well-separated in the similarity graph.

## Q15: What is mean-shift clustering?

**A15:** Mean-shift is a non-parametric clustering algorithm that finds modes in the data distribution by iteratively shifting points toward regions of higher density. It starts with data points and computes a weighted mean of nearby points within a kernel bandwidth, then shifts the point toward that mean. The process repeats until convergence to a mode. Points converging to the same mode belong to the same cluster. Mean-shift automatically determines the number of clusters based on data structure and bandwidth parameter. It can handle clusters of arbitrary shapes and doesn't assume spherical clusters. The bandwidth parameter is crucial: too small creates many small clusters, while too large merges distinct clusters. Mean-shift is computationally expensive for large datasets but provides good results for well-separated, dense clusters without requiring cluster count specification.

## Q16: Explain OPTICS clustering algorithm.

**A16:** OPTICS extends DBSCAN to handle varying densities by creating an ordering of points that represents cluster structure. Instead of producing a single clustering, it generates a reachability plot showing cluster structure at all density levels. The algorithm computes core distances and reachability distances, ordering points to reveal clusters of different densities. Points are processed to build an ordering where clusters appear as valleys in the reachability plot. OPTICS addresses DBSCAN's limitation with varying densities by not requiring a single eps parameter. The reachability plot can be analyzed to extract clusters at different density levels. It identifies hierarchical cluster structure and handles both dense and sparse regions. OPTICS is more computationally expensive than DBSCAN but provides richer cluster information and better handles datasets with varying cluster densities.

## Q17: What is the difference between internal and external clustering evaluation?

**A17:** Internal evaluation measures cluster quality using only the data and clustering results, without external labels. Metrics include silhouette score, within-cluster sum of squares, Davies-Bouldin index, and Calinski-Harabasz index. These assess compactness, separation, or their combination. Internal metrics don't require ground truth but may not align with semantic meaning. External evaluation compares clustering results to known ground truth labels. Metrics include adjusted Rand index, normalized mutual information, homogeneity, completeness, and F-measure. External metrics measure how well clusters match true classes but require labeled data, which defeats the purpose of unsupervised learning. Internal metrics are used when labels are unavailable, while external metrics validate algorithms or measure performance when labels exist for evaluation purposes only.

## Q18: What are latent variable models?

**A18:** Latent variable models assume observed data is generated from unobserved hidden variables that capture underlying structure. These models learn both the latent variables and their relationships to observed data. Examples include factor analysis, which models observed variables as linear combinations of latent factors, and probabilistic PCA, which provides a probabilistic interpretation of PCA. Latent Dirichlet Allocation models documents as mixtures of topics, which are latent variables. Gaussian Mixture Models treat cluster assignments as latent variables. Variational autoencoders learn latent representations of data. Latent variable models enable dimensionality reduction, structure discovery, and generative modeling. They provide interpretable representations and can handle missing data by inferring latent variables. The Expectation-Maximization algorithm is commonly used for parameter estimation in these models.

## Q19: Explain Independent Component Analysis and its applications.

**A19:** Independent Component Analysis separates mixed signals into statistically independent components, assuming observed data is a linear mixture of independent sources. Unlike PCA which finds uncorrelated components, ICA finds independent components with stronger statistical properties. It's used for blind source separation, such as separating audio signals from multiple speakers or extracting brain signals from EEG data. ICA assumes sources are non-Gaussian and statistically independent, and that mixing is linear and instantaneous. The algorithm finds an unmixing matrix that maximizes independence, typically measured by non-Gaussianity or mutual information. Applications include signal processing, feature extraction, and removing artifacts from data. ICA requires careful preprocessing including centering and whitening. It's particularly useful when the goal is to recover original source signals rather than just reduce dimensionality.

## Q20: What is Non-negative Matrix Factorization and when is it used?

**A20:** Non-negative Matrix Factorization decomposes a non-negative matrix into two lower-rank non-negative matrices, representing data as additive combinations of parts. Unlike PCA which allows negative components, NMF constrains factors to be non-negative, leading to parts-based representations that are more interpretable. It's used for image analysis where components represent parts of images, topic modeling where components represent topics, and audio source separation. NMF finds applications in recommender systems, text mining, and bioinformatics. The decomposition is not unique, and algorithms like multiplicative updates or gradient descent solve the optimization problem. NMF provides sparse, interpretable representations but requires non-negative input data and may converge to local minima. It's particularly valuable when interpretability and parts-based decomposition are important.
