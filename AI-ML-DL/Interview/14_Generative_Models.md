# Generative Models

## Q1: What is the difference between generative and discriminative models?

**A1:** Discriminative models learn the decision boundary between classes by modeling the conditional probability P(y|x), directly mapping inputs to outputs. Examples include logistic regression, SVMs, and standard neural network classifiers. Generative models learn the joint probability P(x, y) or the data distribution P(x), enabling them to generate new data samples. By learning P(x, y), Bayes' rule can derive P(y|x) for classification. Generative models capture more about the data structure but are typically harder to train and may waste capacity modeling irrelevant variations. Discriminative models are often more accurate for classification since they focus only on the decision boundary. In modern deep learning, generative models like GANs, VAEs, and diffusion models have become powerful tools for image synthesis, text generation, and data augmentation.

## Q2: How do Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) work?

**A2:** VAEs are generative models that learn a latent representation of data by combining an encoder and decoder with probabilistic principles. The encoder maps input x to a distribution over latent variables z, parameterized as a Gaussian with mean and variance vectors. The decoder samples from this distribution and reconstructs x. The training objective maximizes the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), which decomposes into a reconstruction loss (how well the decoder reconstructs inputs) and a KL divergence term (how close the learned latent distribution is to a prior, typically standard normal). The reparameterization trick enables backpropagation through the sampling step by expressing z = mu + sigma * epsilon where epsilon is sampled from N(0,1). This makes the random sampling differentiable. VAEs produce blurry samples compared to GANs because the reconstruction loss averages over possible outputs, but they provide a principled latent space useful for interpolation and manipulation.

## Q3: What is the reparameterization trick in VAEs and why is it needed?

**A3:** The reparameterization trick solves the problem of backpropagating gradients through a random sampling operation. In a VAE, the encoder outputs mean (mu) and variance (sigma^2) of the latent distribution, and we need to sample z from this distribution. Direct sampling z ~ N(mu, sigma^2) is a stochastic node that blocks gradient flow. The trick rewrites sampling as z = mu + sigma * epsilon, where epsilon ~ N(0, 1) is an auxiliary random variable independent of the model parameters. Now mu and sigma are deterministic functions of the encoder parameters and gradients can flow through them via standard backpropagation. The randomness is isolated in epsilon which does not depend on learnable parameters. This technique is broadly applicable to any differentiable distribution that can be expressed as a deterministic transformation of a fixed noise distribution.

## Q4: Explain the GAN framework including generator and discriminator roles.

**A4:** Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) consist of two networks trained simultaneously in a minimax game. The generator G takes random noise z (sampled from a simple distribution like uniform or Gaussian) and transforms it into synthetic data G(z). The discriminator D takes both real data samples and generated samples and classifies them as real or fake. G is trained to fool D by minimizing log(1 - D(G(z))), while D is trained to correctly distinguish real from fake by maximizing log(D(x)) + log(1 - D(G(z))). At equilibrium, G produces data indistinguishable from real data and D outputs 0.5 for all inputs. In practice, training alternates between updating D for k steps and G for one step. The generator never sees real data directly; it only receives gradients through the discriminator. This adversarial training can produce remarkably sharp and realistic samples but is notoriously difficult to stabilize.

## Q5: What is mode collapse in GANs and how can it be addressed?

**A5:** Mode collapse occurs when the generator learns to produce only a limited variety of outputs rather than covering the full data distribution. In extreme cases, G may produce nearly identical outputs for all noise inputs, capturing only one or a few modes of the data. For example, a GAN trained on digits might only generate 3s and 7s, ignoring other digits. This happens because G finds a single output that consistently fools D and keeps exploiting it. Mitigation strategies include: minibatch discrimination (letting D see batches and detect lack of diversity), unrolled GANs (optimizing G against future D states), feature matching (matching statistics of generated and real data in D's feature space), using Wasserstein distance instead of JS divergence (WGAN), and adding diversity-encouraging regularization. Progressive training, starting from low-resolution and growing, also helps. Mode dropping (partial collapse) is more common than full collapse in practice.

## Q6: What is the Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) and why was it proposed?

**A6:** WGAN replaces the Jensen-Shannon divergence used in standard GANs with the Wasserstein-1 (Earth Mover's) distance as the training objective. The JS divergence can be constant (log 2) when the real and generated distributions have non-overlapping supports, providing no useful gradient for the generator. The Wasserstein distance measures the minimum cost of transporting mass from one distribution to another and is continuous even when distributions do not overlap. By the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, this is equivalent to maximizing E[f(x_real)] - E[f(x_fake)] over 1-Lipschitz functions f, where f replaces the discriminator (called "critic" in WGAN). The Lipschitz constraint was originally enforced by weight clipping, later improved by gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) which penalizes the gradient norm deviating from 1. WGAN provides more stable training, meaningful loss curves that correlate with generation quality, and reduced mode collapse.

## Q7: What are conditional GANs and how do they work?

**A7:** Conditional GANs (cGANs) extend the standard GAN framework by conditioning both the generator and discriminator on additional information y, such as class labels, text descriptions, or input images. The generator receives both noise z and condition y as input: G(z, y). The discriminator evaluates whether the sample is real and matches the condition: D(x, y). This enables controlled generation, such as generating images of a specific digit class, producing images from text descriptions, or performing image-to-image translation. Pix2Pix is a notable cGAN for paired image translation (e.g., edges to photos) using U-Net generator and PatchGAN discriminator. Class-conditional image generation concatenates or projects class embeddings into the generator and discriminator. AC-GAN adds an auxiliary classifier to the discriminator that predicts the class label of both real and generated images, improving both generation quality and class consistency.

## Q8: How does StyleGAN generate high-quality images?

**A8:** StyleGAN introduces a style-based generator architecture that maps the latent code z through a mapping network (8 fully connected layers) to an intermediate latent space W. This W space is less entangled than Z, meaning individual dimensions correspond more to distinct visual attributes. Instead of feeding the latent directly to the first layer, W vectors are transformed into per-layer style vectors via learned affine transformations and injected through Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN) at each resolution. Stochastic noise is added at each layer for fine details like hair strands and pores. Different layers control different attributes: early layers affect pose and shape, middle layers affect facial features, and later layers affect color and fine details. StyleGAN2 replaced AdaIN with weight demodulation to remove water-droplet artifacts, and redesigned the architecture for improved quality. StyleGAN3 addressed the texture-sticking problem by enforcing translation and rotation equivariance.

## Q9: What are diffusion models and how do they generate samples?

**A9:** Diffusion models define a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data over T timesteps until the data becomes pure noise, and learn a reverse process that denoises step by step to generate new samples. The forward process is fixed: q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = N(x_t; sqrt(1-beta_t) * x_{t-1}, beta_t * I), where beta_t is a noise schedule. The reverse process trains a neural network to predict the noise added at each step: the model takes noisy image x_t and timestep t, predicting the noise epsilon. Training minimizes the simple objective E[||epsilon - epsilon_theta(x_t, t)||^2]. At generation time, the model starts from pure Gaussian noise x_T and iteratively denoises through all T steps. The mathematical foundation connects to score matching and stochastic differential equations. Diffusion models have surpassed GANs in image quality metrics while being easier to train and more mode-covering, though they are slower to sample due to the iterative denoising process.

## Q10: What is classifier-free guidance in diffusion models?

**A10:** Classifier-free guidance is a technique to improve the quality and adherence to conditioning signals in conditional diffusion models without needing a separate classifier. During training, the conditioning signal (e.g., text prompt or class label) is randomly dropped with some probability (e.g., 10%), training both conditional and unconditional models simultaneously with the same network. At inference, the model computes both the conditional prediction epsilon_theta(x_t, t, c) and unconditional prediction epsilon_theta(x_t, t). The final prediction is extrapolated: epsilon = epsilon_unconditional + w * (epsilon_conditional - epsilon_unconditional), where w > 1 is the guidance scale. Higher guidance scale produces outputs more aligned with the condition but with less diversity and potential artifacts. This replaced classifier guidance (which required a separately trained classifier and computing its gradients) with a simpler and more effective approach. Most modern text-to-image models like Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, and Imagen use classifier-free guidance.

## Q11: What are latent diffusion models and how does Stable Diffusion use them?

**A11:** Latent diffusion models (LDMs) perform the diffusion process in a compressed latent space rather than pixel space, dramatically reducing computational cost. First, a variational autoencoder (VAE) is trained to encode images into a lower-dimensional latent representation and decode them back. The diffusion model then operates on these latent codes, which are typically 4-8x smaller in each spatial dimension. This makes training and sampling much faster while maintaining high quality. Stable Diffusion applies this architecture with a pre-trained VAE encoder/decoder, a U-Net denoiser with cross-attention for text conditioning, and a CLIP text encoder that converts text prompts into embeddings. Cross-attention layers in the U-Net attend to text embeddings, enabling text-guided generation. The model was trained on the LAION-5B dataset. At inference, random latent noise is denoised guided by the text embedding, then decoded by the VAE decoder into pixel space.

## Q12: How do autoregressive models generate sequences?

**A12:** Autoregressive models generate sequences one element at a time, conditioning each new element on all previously generated elements. They model the joint distribution as a product of conditionals: P(x) = P(x_1) * P(x_2|x_1) * P(x_3|x_1,x_2) * ... This decomposition is exact by the chain rule of probability. In language models like GPT, each token is predicted using a softmax over the vocabulary conditioned on all prior tokens via transformer decoder with causal masking. In image generation, models like PixelCNN predict each pixel conditioned on previously generated pixels in raster scan order. Sampling strategies include greedy decoding (always pick highest probability), temperature sampling (divide logits by T before softmax), top-k sampling (sample from top k tokens), and nucleus/top-p sampling (sample from smallest set with cumulative probability >= p). Autoregressive models produce high-quality, diverse samples and have tractable likelihood computation, but generation is inherently sequential and slow.

## Q13: What are normalizing flows and how do they differ from VAEs?

**A13:** Normalizing flows transform a simple base distribution (e.g., standard Gaussian) into a complex target distribution through a sequence of invertible, differentiable transformations. The key property is that both the forward transformation (generation) and inverse (density estimation) are tractable, along with exact log-likelihood computation via the change of variables formula involving the Jacobian determinant. This contrasts with VAEs which optimize a lower bound (ELBO) on the log-likelihood rather than the exact value. Each flow step must be an invertible function with an efficiently computable Jacobian determinant. Popular architectures include RealNVP (affine coupling layers), Glow (1x1 invertible convolutions), and neural spline flows. The advantage over VAEs is exact likelihood and exact inference with no posterior approximation gap. The disadvantage is that the invertibility constraint limits architectural flexibility, and flows typically require more parameters and computation to achieve comparable sample quality.

## Q14: What are energy-based models?

**A14:** Energy-based models (EBMs) assign a scalar energy value E(x) to each configuration x, with lower energy indicating more probable configurations. The probability density is defined as p(x) = exp(-E(x)) / Z, where Z is the partition function (normalizing constant). Unlike other generative models, EBMs do not require the architecture to be invertible (like flows) or have a specific encoder-decoder structure (like VAEs). The energy function can be any neural network. The challenge is that Z is generally intractable to compute, making exact likelihood evaluation impossible. Training uses contrastive divergence or score matching to avoid computing Z. Sampling uses MCMC methods like Langevin dynamics, which iteratively updates samples by following the gradient of the log-probability plus noise. EBMs are flexible and can be composed (adding energies multiplies distributions), but training can be unstable and sampling is slow. They have connections to diffusion models through score matching.

## Q15: What is VQ-VAE and how does it differ from standard VAEs?

**A15:** VQ-VAE (Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder) replaces the continuous latent space of standard VAEs with a discrete codebook of learned embedding vectors. The encoder maps input to a continuous representation which is then quantized by finding the nearest codebook vector (vector quantization). The decoder receives the quantized representation and reconstructs the input. Training uses a straight-through estimator to pass gradients through the non-differentiable quantization step, plus commitment and codebook losses to keep encoder outputs close to codebook vectors. Unlike standard VAEs, VQ-VAE avoids the posterior collapse problem (where the decoder ignores the latent) and produces sharper reconstructions because the discrete bottleneck forces meaningful encoding. VQ-VAE-2 adds a hierarchical structure with multiple levels of quantization for capturing both global structure and local details. The discrete latent codes can be modeled with an autoregressive prior (like PixelCNN) for unconditional generation.

## Q16: How is FID (Frechet Inception Distance) used to evaluate generative models?

**A16:** FID measures the quality and diversity of generated images by comparing the statistics of generated and real image distributions in a feature space. It extracts features from a pre-trained Inception-v3 network's pool3 layer for both real and generated image sets, then fits multivariate Gaussians to each set (computing mean and covariance). The Frechet distance between these two Gaussians is: FID = ||mu_r - mu_g||^2 + Tr(Sigma_r + Sigma_g - 2*(Sigma_r * Sigma_g)^{1/2}). Lower FID indicates the generated distribution is closer to the real distribution. FID captures both quality (fidelity) and diversity; mode collapse increases FID because the generated distribution's covariance is too narrow. FID is sensitive to sample size (at least 10K-50K samples recommended), the specific Inception model used, and image preprocessing. Limitations include insensitivity to certain artifacts, inability to detect memorization, and the assumption of Gaussian feature distributions. Alternatives include KID (Kernel Inception Distance) which is unbiased for finite samples.

## Q17: What is the Inception Score (IS) and its limitations?

**A17:** The Inception Score evaluates generated images using a pre-trained Inception network by measuring two properties: quality and diversity. For quality, each generated image should be confidently classified (low entropy conditional distribution p(y|x)). For diversity, the marginal distribution p(y) across all generated images should be uniform (high entropy). IS = exp(E[KL(p(y|x) || p(y))]). Higher IS indicates better quality and diversity. However, IS has significant limitations: it only evaluates images the Inception network was trained to classify (ImageNet classes), so it cannot properly evaluate faces, medical images, or other domains. It does not compare against real data at all, so it cannot detect mode dropping for classes not in ImageNet. IS is insensitive to intra-class diversity and can be fooled by adversarial examples. It also does not penalize memorization of training data. FID has largely replaced IS as the primary evaluation metric because it compares against real data distributions.

## Q18: What are the key challenges in text-to-image generation?

**A18:** Text-to-image generation faces several fundamental challenges. Compositional understanding requires correctly rendering spatial relationships ("a red cube on top of a blue sphere"), counting ("exactly five birds"), and attribute binding (assigning the right colors to the right objects). Current models often confuse which attributes belong to which objects. Text rendering in generated images remains poor as models struggle to produce coherent letters and words. Fine-grained control over layout, pose, and style is limited with text prompts alone, driving research into additional conditioning signals like ControlNet (depth maps, edge maps, pose). Consistency across multiple generations of the same subject is difficult without techniques like DreamBooth or textual inversion. Evaluation is challenging since FID does not measure text-image alignment, and CLIP score has biases. Ethical concerns include generating harmful or misleading content, copyright issues from training on web-scraped data, and deepfake potential.

## Q19: What are the challenges in video generation compared to image generation?

**A19:** Video generation introduces several dimensions of complexity beyond image generation. Temporal consistency is the primary challenge: generated frames must maintain coherent object appearance, motion, and lighting across time. Flickering and morphing artifacts occur when each frame is generated somewhat independently. The data dimensionality increases dramatically (adding time as another axis), requiring significantly more computation and memory. Motion modeling requires understanding physics, object dynamics, and scene continuity. Long-range temporal dependencies are hard to capture, making videos beyond a few seconds particularly challenging. Training data is harder to curate than images (videos require temporal annotation, have variable frame rates, contain scene cuts). Evaluation metrics are less established than for images. Current approaches include extending diffusion models with temporal attention layers, video transformers, and autoregressive frame prediction. Models like Sora demonstrate impressive results but still struggle with physics consistency, object permanence, and long-duration coherence.

## Q20: How do modern generative models compare across key dimensions?

**A20:** GANs produce sharp, high-quality samples with fast single-pass generation but suffer from training instability, mode collapse, and lack of density estimation. VAEs provide stable training and a smooth latent space for interpolation but produce blurry outputs due to the reconstruction loss and approximate posterior. Normalizing flows offer exact likelihood computation and invertibility but are architecturally constrained and computationally expensive. Diffusion models achieve state-of-the-art quality with stable training and good mode coverage but require many iterative denoising steps making sampling slow (mitigated by DDIM, consistency models, and distillation). Autoregressive models have exact likelihood and produce high-quality sequential outputs but generation is inherently slow. In practice, diffusion models currently dominate image generation, autoregressive models dominate text generation, and hybrid approaches are emerging. The choice depends on the application: VAEs for representation learning, GANs for real-time generation, diffusion for maximum quality, and flows for density estimation tasks.
