# Natural Language Processing

## Q1: What is tokenization and why is it important in NLP?

**A1:** Tokenization is the process of breaking raw text into smaller units called tokens, which can be words, subwords, or characters. It serves as the first step in any NLP pipeline because models cannot process raw strings directly. Word-level tokenization splits on whitespace and punctuation but struggles with out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words. Subword tokenization methods like BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding) and WordPiece solve this by splitting rare words into meaningful subunits while keeping frequent words intact. For example, "unhappiness" might become ["un", "happi", "ness"]. SentencePiece provides a language-agnostic tokenizer that works directly on raw text without pre-tokenization. The choice of tokenizer affects vocabulary size, sequence length, and ultimately model performance.

## Q2: How does Word2Vec work and what are its two training approaches?

**A2:** Word2Vec learns dense vector representations of words by training a shallow neural network on word co-occurrence patterns. It has two architectures: Skip-gram and CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words). Skip-gram takes a target word as input and predicts surrounding context words, making it better for rare words. CBOW takes context words as input and predicts the target word, being faster to train and better for frequent words. Both use a window size to define context. Training on the full vocabulary is expensive, so techniques like negative sampling (randomly sampling non-context words as negatives) and hierarchical softmax speed up computation. The resulting vectors capture semantic relationships where similar words have similar vectors, and arithmetic operations like vec("king") - vec("man") + vec("woman") approximate vec("queen").

## Q3: What is the difference between GloVe and Word2Vec?

**A3:** Word2Vec learns embeddings through local context windows using a predictive model, while GloVe (Global Vectors) combines local context with global co-occurrence statistics. GloVe constructs a word-word co-occurrence matrix from the entire corpus, then factorizes it to produce word vectors. The key insight is that the ratio of co-occurrence probabilities between two words with a probe word encodes meaningful semantic relationships. For example, the ratio of P("ice"|"solid") to P("steam"|"solid") captures the thermodynamic state distinction. GloVe optimizes a weighted least-squares objective on the log co-occurrence counts. In practice, both methods produce embeddings of comparable quality, but GloVe can be more efficient on large corpora since it processes the co-occurrence matrix rather than iterating over individual windows. Neither captures polysemy since each word gets a single fixed vector.

## Q4: What are contextual embeddings and how do they differ from static embeddings?

**A4:** Static embeddings like Word2Vec and GloVe assign a single fixed vector to each word regardless of context, so "bank" has the same representation whether referring to a financial institution or a river bank. Contextual embeddings generate different representations for the same word depending on its surrounding context. ELMo was the first major contextual model, using bidirectional LSTMs to produce context-dependent vectors by concatenating forward and backward hidden states. BERT advanced this by using transformer encoders with masked language modeling, producing deeply bidirectional representations. GPT uses unidirectional (left-to-right) transformers. Contextual embeddings capture polysemy, syntax, and semantic nuance, which is why they dramatically improved performance on tasks like NER, question answering, and sentiment analysis compared to static embeddings.

## Q5: Explain autoregressive vs masked language modeling.

**A5:** Autoregressive language modeling predicts the next token given all previous tokens, training left-to-right. The model learns P(x_t | x_1, ..., x_{t-1}). GPT uses this approach, making it naturally suited for text generation since it produces one token at a time in sequence. However, it only sees leftward context at each position. Masked language modeling (MLM), used by BERT, randomly masks 15% of input tokens and predicts the original tokens from bidirectional context. This allows the model to attend to both left and right context simultaneously, producing richer representations for understanding tasks. The tradeoff is that MLM models are not naturally generative since they are trained to fill in blanks rather than generate sequentially. T5 bridges both paradigms by framing all tasks as text-to-text, using an encoder-decoder architecture with span corruption during pre-training.

## Q6: What is BERT and how is it pre-trained?

**A6:** BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is a transformer encoder model pre-trained on two objectives: Masked Language Modeling (MLM) and Next Sentence Prediction (NSP). For MLM, 15% of tokens are randomly selected; of these, 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% with a random token, and 10% kept unchanged. The model predicts the original token using bidirectional context. NSP trains the model to predict whether two sentences appear consecutively in the corpus, using the [CLS] token's representation. BERT-base has 12 transformer layers, 768 hidden dimensions, and 110M parameters. BERT-large has 24 layers, 1024 hidden dimensions, and 340M parameters. Pre-trained on BookCorpus and English Wikipedia, BERT is fine-tuned for downstream tasks by adding a task-specific head on top. Later research showed NSP contributes minimally, leading to RoBERTa which drops NSP and uses longer training with more data.

## Q7: How does the GPT series differ from BERT architecturally?

**A7:** GPT uses a transformer decoder with causal (masked) self-attention, meaning each token can only attend to previous tokens. BERT uses a transformer encoder with full bidirectional attention. GPT is pre-trained with autoregressive language modeling (predict next token), while BERT uses masked language modeling. This makes GPT naturally suited for generation tasks and BERT for understanding tasks like classification and NER. GPT-2 scaled to 1.5B parameters and demonstrated strong zero-shot performance. GPT-3 at 175B parameters showed remarkable few-shot capabilities through in-context learning without gradient updates. GPT-4 is multimodal, accepting both text and image inputs. The GPT line emphasizes scaling laws showing predictable performance improvements with more parameters, data, and compute, following power-law relationships.

## Q8: What is the T5 text-to-text framework?

**A8:** T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer) frames every NLP task as converting input text to output text. Classification becomes generating the class label as text, summarization generates a shorter text, translation generates text in another language, and question answering generates the answer span. The input is prefixed with a task descriptor like "translate English to German:" or "summarize:". T5 uses an encoder-decoder transformer architecture, pre-trained with span corruption where random spans of text are replaced with sentinel tokens and the model generates the missing spans. This approach unifies pre-training and fine-tuning under a single framework, simplifying multi-task learning. T5 was trained on C4 (Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus), a cleaned version of Common Crawl. The authors systematically compared architectural choices, pre-training objectives, and dataset sizes to arrive at best practices.

## Q9: How does named entity recognition (NER) work?

**A9:** NER identifies and classifies named entities in text into categories like person, organization, location, date, etc. Traditional approaches used hand-crafted features with CRF (Conditional Random Fields) or HMM models. Neural approaches use BiLSTM-CRF architectures where bidirectional LSTMs capture context and CRF layer models label dependencies (e.g., I-PER cannot follow B-ORG). Modern NER uses pre-trained transformers like BERT, treating NER as token classification where each token gets a BIO/BIOES label. The [CLS] token is ignored and subword tokens are handled by assigning labels only to the first subword piece. Nested NER (entities within entities) requires specialized architectures like span-based models. Evaluation uses entity-level F1, requiring both the boundary and type to be correct. Few-shot NER leverages prompt-based methods or in-context learning with LLMs.

## Q10: What are the main approaches to sentiment analysis?

**A10:** Sentiment analysis classifies text polarity (positive, negative, neutral) or detects fine-grained emotions. Lexicon-based methods use sentiment dictionaries like VADER or SentiWordNet to score words and aggregate. Machine learning approaches use TF-IDF or bag-of-words features with classifiers like SVM or Naive Bayes. Deep learning methods employ CNNs for local pattern detection or RNNs/LSTMs for sequential context. Attention-based models highlight which words most influence sentiment. Transformer-based approaches fine-tune BERT or similar models on labeled sentiment data, achieving state-of-the-art results. Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) identifies sentiment toward specific aspects of an entity (e.g., "great food but terrible service"). Challenges include sarcasm detection, negation handling, and domain-specific language. Modern LLMs can perform sentiment analysis zero-shot through prompting.

## Q11: How has machine translation evolved over time?

**A11:** Machine translation has progressed through several paradigms. Rule-based systems used linguistic rules and dictionaries but were brittle and language-pair specific. Statistical MT (SMT) used phrase tables and language models learned from parallel corpora, with IBM Models defining alignment probabilities. Neural MT (NMT) replaced this with end-to-end encoder-decoder networks, initially using RNNs with attention (Bahdanau attention, 2014). The transformer architecture (2017) brought major improvements through parallelization and self-attention, becoming the dominant approach. Pre-trained multilingual models like mBART and mT5 enable translation for low-resource languages through transfer learning. Current large language models can translate competitively through prompting. Key evaluation metrics include BLEU (n-gram overlap), METEOR (semantic matching), and human evaluation. Challenges remain in low-resource languages, document-level coherence, and domain-specific terminology.

## Q12: Explain extractive vs abstractive text summarization.

**A12:** Extractive summarization selects and concatenates the most important sentences from the source document without modifying them. Methods include TextRank (graph-based sentence ranking), sentence scoring with TF-IDF, and neural approaches that classify each sentence as include/exclude using models like BertSum. The output is guaranteed to be grammatically correct since it uses original sentences, but can lack coherence. Abstractive summarization generates new text that may use words and phrases not in the source, similar to how humans summarize. It uses encoder-decoder models, with early approaches using RNN seq2seq with attention and modern methods using transformers (BART, PEGASUS, T5). Abstractive models can produce more fluent and concise summaries but risk hallucination (generating factually incorrect content). Evaluation uses ROUGE metrics (ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L) measuring n-gram overlap with reference summaries, though human evaluation remains the gold standard.

## Q13: What is perplexity and how is it used to evaluate language models?

**A13:** Perplexity measures how well a language model predicts a held-out test set. Mathematically, it is the exponentiation of the average negative log-likelihood: PP = exp(-1/N * sum(log P(x_i | context))). Intuitively, a perplexity of K means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among K options at each step. Lower perplexity indicates better prediction and a more capable language model. For example, a perplexity of 20 means the model is effectively choosing from 20 equally likely next tokens on average. Perplexity is commonly used to compare language models trained on the same test set, but it has limitations: it does not directly measure generation quality, factual accuracy, or task performance. Models can have low perplexity but still generate repetitive or nonsensical text. It is also not comparable across different tokenizers since different vocabularies change the number of predictions N.

## Q14: What are BLEU and ROUGE scores?

**A14:** BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) measures precision of n-gram overlap between generated text and reference translations. It computes modified precision for unigrams through 4-grams, applies a brevity penalty for short outputs, and takes the geometric mean. BLEU ranges from 0 to 1 (often reported as 0-100). It was designed for machine translation but is used broadly. ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) measures recall of n-gram overlap, designed for summarization. ROUGE-1 measures unigram overlap, ROUGE-2 measures bigram overlap, and ROUGE-L uses longest common subsequence. ROUGE focuses on recall (how much of the reference is captured), while BLEU focuses on precision (how much of the generated text matches). Both have limitations: they measure surface-level overlap and miss semantic equivalence (paraphrases score poorly). Modern evaluation increasingly uses model-based metrics like BERTScore that compute cosine similarity between contextual embeddings.

## Q15: What is few-shot prompting and how does it work with LLMs?

**A15:** Few-shot prompting provides a language model with a small number of input-output examples in the prompt before the actual query, enabling the model to learn the task pattern through in-context learning without any gradient updates. For example, providing three sentiment-labeled sentences followed by an unlabeled sentence and asking for its sentiment. This was prominently demonstrated by GPT-3, which showed that larger models exhibit stronger few-shot capabilities. Zero-shot provides only the task instruction with no examples. One-shot gives a single example. The effectiveness depends on example quality, diversity, and ordering (which can significantly affect results). Few-shot prompting leverages the model's pre-trained knowledge and pattern-matching ability rather than traditional fine-tuning. It is especially useful when labeled data is scarce or fine-tuning is impractical. Limitations include sensitivity to prompt format, inability to learn truly new tasks not represented in pre-training data, and context window constraints on the number of examples.

## Q16: Explain chain-of-thought prompting and its benefits.

**A16:** Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting instructs the model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, rather than directly outputting the result. For example, when solving a math problem, the model explicitly shows each calculation step. This was introduced by Wei et al. (2022) and significantly improves performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks. CoT can be triggered by adding "Let's think step by step" (zero-shot CoT) or by providing examples with detailed reasoning chains (few-shot CoT). The technique works because it decomposes complex problems into manageable sub-problems, allows the model to allocate more computation to harder problems, and makes errors easier to identify. Variants include self-consistency (sampling multiple reasoning chains and taking majority vote), tree-of-thought (exploring multiple reasoning paths), and automatic CoT generation. CoT is most effective in larger models and provides diminishing returns in smaller ones.

## Q17: What is instruction tuning and why is it important?

**A17:** Instruction tuning fine-tunes a pre-trained language model on a diverse collection of tasks formatted as natural language instructions. Instead of training separately on each task, the model learns to follow instructions generally. Datasets like FLAN, Natural Instructions, and Super-Natural Instructions contain thousands of tasks with instructional prompts. The resulting models (FLAN-T5, InstructGPT) follow human instructions much better than base models, even generalizing to unseen task types. Instruction tuning bridges the gap between pre-training (predicting next tokens) and practical usage (following user requests). It improves zero-shot performance dramatically because the model learns the meta-skill of instruction following rather than memorizing specific task formats. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) further refines instruction-tuned models by aligning outputs with human preferences, addressing issues like verbosity, harmful content, and factual accuracy that instruction tuning alone does not fully resolve.

## Q18: How does coreference resolution work?

**A18:** Coreference resolution identifies all expressions in text that refer to the same entity and groups them into clusters. For example, in "John went to the store. He bought milk," the system must determine that "He" refers to "John." Traditional approaches used rule-based systems (like the Stanford Sieve) applying cascading sieves of decreasing precision. Neural approaches treat it as a span-ranking problem: the model considers all possible text spans, scores each pair for coreferentiality, and links mentions to their most likely antecedent. The end-to-end model by Lee et al. (2017) jointly learns mention detection and coreference linking. Modern systems use pre-trained transformers for span representations. Challenges include bridging anaphora ("the room... the door"), event coreference, and long-distance dependencies. Evaluation metrics include MUC (link-based), B-cubed (mention-based), CEAF (entity-based), and the average CoNLL F1 across these three.

## Q19: What is dependency parsing and how does it represent sentence structure?

**A19:** Dependency parsing analyzes the grammatical structure of a sentence by establishing directed relationships (dependencies) between words. Each word is connected to a head (governor) word with a labeled relation like nsubj (nominal subject), dobj (direct object), or amod (adjectival modifier). The root of the sentence is typically the main verb. Unlike constituency parsing which builds phrase-structure trees with non-terminal nodes, dependency parsing creates trees where all nodes are actual words. Transition-based parsers (like the arc-standard system) process the sentence left-to-right using shift-reduce operations, making them fast (linear time). Graph-based parsers (like Eisner's algorithm or biaffine attention) score all possible edges and find the maximum spanning tree, often achieving higher accuracy. Modern neural dependency parsers use BiLSTMs or transformers to compute contextual representations, then apply biaffine attention to predict head-dependent pairs and relation labels. Universal Dependencies (UD) provides a cross-lingual annotation framework.

## Q20: What are the key challenges remaining in NLP?

**A20:** Despite massive progress, several fundamental challenges persist. Commonsense reasoning remains difficult because models lack grounded world knowledge and often rely on statistical patterns rather than true understanding. Multilingual NLP struggles with the 7000+ world languages, most having minimal digital text for training. Long-document understanding is limited by attention complexity scaling quadratically with sequence length, though efficient attention methods are improving this. Hallucination in generative models produces fluent but factually incorrect text, undermining trust. Bias and fairness issues persist as models trained on internet text inherit and amplify societal biases related to gender, race, and culture. Evaluation remains challenging since automated metrics poorly correlate with human judgment for open-ended generation. Robustness to adversarial inputs, domain shift, and temporal drift (knowledge becoming outdated) are ongoing concerns. Finally, interpretability and explainability of large neural models remain limited, making deployment in high-stakes domains like healthcare and law problematic.
