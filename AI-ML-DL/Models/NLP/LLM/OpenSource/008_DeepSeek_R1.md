## DeepSeek-R1 (2025)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

DeepSeek-R1 (technical report "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning," January 2025) is built on top of DeepSeek-V3-Base and demonstrates that large-scale reinforcement learning with **verifiable rewards (RLVR)** is a practical, effective route to strong reasoning behavior — long chain-of-thought, self-correction, and exploration of multiple solution strategies before committing to an answer — without requiring a large hand-curated reasoning-SFT dataset as the primary driver. R1 matched or approached OpenAI's o1 on several reasoning benchmarks (AIME, MATH-500, Codeforces) at release, made this capability openly available (weights + a detailed methodology paper), and additionally showed that the resulting reasoning behavior could be **distilled into much smaller dense models** via SFT alone — meaning the expensive RL step is a one-time cost amortized across an entire family of released model sizes rather than something that must be repeated per size.

The paper's central conceptual contribution is not "RL improves LLMs" (known since RLHF) but rather isolating **what kind of reward signal RL needs to reliably elicit reasoning**: DeepSeek-R1-Zero shows that pure RL against objectively checkable rewards, with *no SFT warm-start at all*, is sufficient to bootstrap sophisticated reasoning behavior from a strong base model — a result that had been debated in the field (whether RL alone, without imitation of reasoning traces, could produce reasoning, as opposed to merely sharpening existing behavior). R1 (the full, released model) then layers a multi-stage pipeline on top of R1-Zero's finding to fix R1-Zero's practical deficiencies (poor readability, language mixing) while preserving the RL-driven reasoning gains.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

R1 does not introduce a new base architecture — it inherits DeepSeek-V3's MLA attention and fine-grained MoE (256 routed + 1 shared expert, 671B total / 37B active) unchanged (see 006/007). The architectural story in R1 is entirely in the **training pipeline and the RL algorithm**, GRPO.

**RLVR vs. RLHF — the reward-signal distinction.** Standard RLHF trains a reward model (RM) on human preference comparisons, then optimizes the policy against that *learned* RM as a proxy for human judgment. The RM is an approximation — it can be gamed (reward hacking), it saturates/miscalibrates outside its training distribution, and it never gives an objectively "correct" signal, only a preference-shaped one. RLVR instead uses reward functions that are **mechanically checkable against ground truth**, with no learned approximation layer between the policy's output and the reward: a math answer is graded by exact-match (or symbolic equivalence) against a known correct answer; code is graded by running it against a test suite and checking pass/fail. This removes an entire failure mode (reward-model exploitation) at the cost of only being applicable to domains where "correctness" is checkable at all (math, code, some logic/puzzle domains — not open-ended writing or subjective preference).

**GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) — the algorithm.** PPO's advantage estimate requires a learned value function/critic (typically a network of comparable size to the policy) predicting expected return from a given state, so that advantage = actual return − predicted baseline. Training and holding this critic in memory throughout RL is a substantial fraction of PPO's total training cost, especially at 671B-parameter policy scale. GRPO removes the critic entirely by estimating the baseline empirically from a **group of sampled outputs for the same prompt**:

1. For each prompt q in a training batch, sample a **group** of G completions from the current policy: `{o_1, o_2, ..., o_G} ~ π_θ(·|q)` (e.g., G = 16 or 64 in DeepSeek's setup).
2. Score each completion with the verifiable reward function: `r_1, ..., r_G` (e.g., 1 if the final boxed answer matches ground truth, 0 otherwise, possibly combined with a format-adherence reward).
3. Compute the **group-relative advantage** by normalizing each reward against the group's own statistics: `A_i = (r_i − mean(r_1,...,r_G)) / (std(r_1,...,r_G) + ε)`. The group mean serves exactly the role a learned critic's value prediction would serve in PPO — a baseline against which each sample's reward is compared — but it requires no trained model, only the other samples drawn for the same prompt.
4. Optimize a PPO-style clipped surrogate objective using `A_i` in place of PPO's critic-derived advantage: `L(θ) = E[ min( ρ_i(θ) A_i, clip(ρ_i(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε) A_i ) ]` where `ρ_i(θ) = π_θ(o_i|q) / π_θ_old(o_i|q)` is the importance ratio between the current and behavior policy, applied per-token over the completion.
5. A KL-divergence penalty term against a reference policy (typically the SFT/cold-start checkpoint) is added to the objective to prevent the policy from drifting too far and degenerating, `− β · KL(π_θ || π_ref)`.

**Why removing the critic matters at scale.** A PPO critic for a 671B policy is itself typically a large network (often the same backbone with a value head, or a separate model of comparable size) that must be trained jointly, held in GPU memory, and kept numerically stable throughout RL — value-function training is notoriously touchy (value loss spikes, bias in early training) and adds a second optimization problem on top of the policy's. GRPO's baseline is a **statistic of already-sampled data**, computed in closed form with no additional parameters, no additional forward/backward passes beyond what's needed to score completions, and no separate training dynamics to stabilize. This roughly halves the model-memory footprint of the RL stage (no critic weights, no critic optimizer state) and removes an entire class of instability. The cost is that the baseline is noisier (empirical mean over G samples rather than a value function's smoothed generalization across states) and requires sampling multiple completions per prompt, so wall-clock/sample efficiency trades against critic-training cost rather than being a free lunch — DeepSeek's finding is that this trade is favorable specifically in the RLVR regime, where rewards are cheap and exact to compute (unlike a learned RM, which is itself an expensive forward pass and does not obviously get "more accurate" from being queried more times for the same prompt).

**R1-Zero's reward design.** Pure RL from the DeepSeek-V3-Base checkpoint, no SFT warm-start, with two rule-based rewards: an **accuracy reward** (correctness of the final answer, verified programmatically) and a **format reward** (adherence to a required `<think>...</think><answer>...</answer>` structure). No neural reward model, no process reward model (step-level supervision) — R1-Zero deliberately avoids process-level rewards because the paper reports that naive process reward models trained for this purpose were prone to reward hacking and did not outperform outcome-only rewards at this scale.

### 3. Scale — Parameters, Data, Compute

R1 does not change the underlying parameter count from V3 (671B total / 37B active) — the RL stages fine-tune the same MoE backbone. Scale-relevant figures specific to R1:

- Cold-start SFT stage: a few thousand curated long-chain-of-thought examples (paper describes it as a relatively small, high-quality set — not a large corpus), used only to fix R1-Zero's readability/language-mixing issues before RL, not to teach reasoning per se (reasoning is primarily an RL-acquired behavior).
- Rejection-sampling SFT stage: approximately **800K samples** (≈600K reasoning-focused, generated by rejection-sampling from the RL-stage-2 model and keeping only correct/high-quality trajectories, plus ≈200K general/non-reasoning samples for broader capability retention) used for the second SFT stage.
- Distilled dense models released at **1.5B, 7B, 8B, 14B, 32B, and 70B** parameters, built on Qwen2.5 and Llama 3 base checkpoints respectively, trained via SFT only (no RL) on R1-generated reasoning traces.
- GRPO group size G is not fixed at one universal number across all stages/domains, but the mechanism (sample a group per prompt, normalize reward within the group) is constant throughout.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

R1's infrastructure inherits V3's distributed training stack (MLA, expert-parallel MoE, FP8 where applicable) for the underlying forward/backward passes, but RL introduces a distinct systems problem on top: at each RL step, the policy must **generate** G completions per prompt (an inference-like rollout workload, typically the dominant wall-clock cost of RL relative to the gradient-update step itself), score them against verifiable reward functions (fast for exact-match math grading, potentially much slower for code — requires actually executing candidate code against test cases in sandboxed environments), then compute the group-normalized advantage and run a policy-gradient update. This rollout-generation phase is naturally parallelized across many inference replicas of the policy (a rollout worker pool), decoupled from the smaller number of GPUs doing the gradient update — an architecture broadly similar to other large-scale RL-for-LLM systems (separate "actor"/generation and "learner"/training roles), though DeepSeek's paper does not disclose this infrastructure at the level of detail V3's report gives for pretraining. Removing the critic (Section 2) also removes an entire model's worth of distributed-training coordination (critic forward/backward, critic optimizer sharding) from this already-complex rollout-and-update loop.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

R1 does not repeat pretraining — it starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base (14.8T-token pretraining corpus, next-token-prediction objective; see 007_DeepSeek_V3.md Section 5). R1's "data" in the relevant sense is training-time-generated: RL rollouts (prompts drawn from math/code/logic-style verifiable-reward datasets) and the rejection-sampled reasoning traces produced by the RL-trained model itself, which then become SFT data for subsequent stages — i.e., R1's own outputs are recycled as training data later in its own pipeline, and again as the entire training signal for the distilled smaller models.

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

R1's post-training pipeline has four stages, each addressing a specific gap left by the previous one:

1. **Cold-start SFT.** Fine-tune DeepSeek-V3-Base on a few thousand curated long-CoT examples (human-refined or lightly-curated outputs from an earlier reasoning-model prototype). Purpose: give the model a reasonable starting distribution over reasoning-trace *style* (fixing the readability/language-mixing failure mode that pure RL-from-base exhibits in R1-Zero) before RL begins, without yet trying to teach reasoning content — reasoning content is still primarily acquired in the next stage.
2. **Reasoning-oriented RL (GRPO).** Starting from the cold-start checkpoint, run the same rule-based accuracy + format reward RL as R1-Zero. This is the stage where most of the reasoning-capability gain is attributed; the model learns to extend chain-of-thought length, backtrack, and re-verify its own intermediate steps purely because doing so raises the probability of the verifiable accuracy reward, not because any trace explicitly modeled that behavior for it to imitate.
3. **Rejection sampling + SFT (stage 2 SFT).** Use the stage-2 RL checkpoint to generate many candidates per prompt across a broader task distribution, keep only correct/high-quality ones (rejection sampling), and combine ~600K of these reasoning traces with ~200K general-capability (non-reasoning) SFT examples. This broadens coverage beyond the RL stage's narrower verifiable-reward task set and restores general instruction-following/helpfulness that pure reasoning-RL does not target.
4. **Secondary RL (all-scenario alignment).** A further RL stage combining the reasoning verifiable-reward signal with more conventional human-preference-style rewards for helpfulness and harmlessness, so the final released model is aligned across both reasoning tasks and general conversational/safety scenarios, not narrowly optimized for math/code correctness alone.

**R1-Zero as a scientific control.** Critically, R1-Zero (stage-2-equivalent RL applied directly to the *base* model, skipping stage 1 entirely) is published specifically to isolate the claim "RL alone, without any SFT reasoning warm-start, produces reasoning behavior" — its known deficiencies (mixing languages mid-response, poor readability) are presented as *cosmetic* problems downstream of skipping stage 1, not evidence that the reasoning capability itself depends on SFT.

**Distillation.** Reasoning behavior is transferred to dense Qwen2.5- and Llama-3-based models of 1.5B–70B parameters via **SFT alone** on rejection-sampled R1-generated reasoning traces — no RL is run on the distilled models. This is the paper's key claim about cost amortization: the expensive part (671B-scale RLVR, rollout generation, reward verification infrastructure) happens once, and every subsequent model size inherits the resulting reasoning *style and capability* through ordinary supervised fine-tuning, which is orders of magnitude cheaper than repeating RL at each size. The paper reports that DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B outperforms OpenAI's o1-mini across several reported reasoning benchmarks, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B sets strong results among dense models of that size — evidence that the distilled behavior is not merely superficial style transfer but carries real capability gain.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

1. **RLVR at large scale as a practical, reproducible reasoning-training paradigm** — the paper's central empirical claim, with R1-Zero as the specific evidence that RL alone (no SFT) suffices to elicit reasoning.
2. **GRPO** as a critic-free RL algorithm: group-sampled empirical baseline replacing a learned value function, with the group-normalized advantage plugged into an otherwise-standard PPO-style clipped objective plus reference-KL penalty.
3. **A four-stage pipeline design** that cleanly separates "acquire reasoning" (pure RLVR) from "make it presentable and broadly capable" (SFT stages) rather than trying to solve both simultaneously in one objective.
4. **Reasoning distillation via SFT-only transfer** demonstrating that the RL step's output (traces) is a more efficient unit of transfer than the RL process itself.
5. **Explicit ablation of process-level vs. outcome-level reward** at this scale, reporting that naive process reward models underperformed/were harder to scale reliably than outcome-only verifiable rewards for this use case — a useful negative result for the field.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

- **R1-Zero's readability/language-mixing problem**: pure RL optimizes only for the reward signal (correctness), which says nothing about output legibility to a human reader — the model is free to mix languages or produce hard-to-follow reasoning as long as the final answer is graded correct. Solved not by patching the reward (which risks reintroducing reward-hacking surface) but by prepending a cold-start SFT stage that anchors output *style* before RL, leaving the reward function itself simple and hard to game.
- **Reward hacking risk in verifiable rewards**: even exact-match/test-execution rewards can be gamed (e.g., a model discovering a spurious pattern in answer formatting, or overfitting to quirks of a specific verifier) — mitigated via the format-reward component and by the multi-stage pipeline's later rejection-sampling filter, which discards low-quality trajectories before they become training data.
- **Group-baseline noise at small group size**: GRPO's baseline quality depends on group size G — too small a group gives a noisy, biased empirical baseline; this is a practical tuning axis (compute cost of extra rollouts vs. baseline variance) distinct from PPO's critic-variance/bias tradeoffs.
- **Coverage gap from RL's narrow task distribution**: RLVR-eligible tasks (checkable math/code) are a small slice of real usage; the rejection-sampling + general-SFT stage exists specifically to prevent the final model from becoming narrowly good at verifiable tasks while regressing on everything else.

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

R1 inherits DeepSeek-V3's serving profile exactly (MLA cache efficiency, MoE expert-parallel dispatch — see 007_DeepSeek_V3.md Section 9), since no architecture changes; the standard 671B/37B split applies. R1's distinct inference consideration is behavioral rather than architectural: reasoning models generate substantially longer outputs (extended chain-of-thought before a final answer), which directly multiplies decode-time cost and KV-cache duration per request relative to a non-reasoning model of the same size — this is exactly the regime where MLA's cache-size advantage (inherited from V3) matters most, since long reasoning traces are, mechanically, long-context decode workloads. The distilled dense models (1.5B–70B) offer a cost/latency lever independent of the reasoning-quality tradeoff: for many applications, a much smaller distilled model captures most of the reasoning-style benefit at dramatically lower serving cost than the full 671B model, since reasoning capability was shown to transfer via SFT rather than requiring the full MoE scale.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

Reported results (DeepSeek-R1 vs. OpenAI o1-1217, at release): AIME 2024 pass@1 ≈ 79.8% (R1) vs. ≈79.2% (o1-1217); MATH-500 ≈ 97.3%; Codeforces rating percentile ≈ 96.3%; MMLU ≈ 90.8%; GPQA Diamond ≈ 71.5%; SWE-bench Verified ≈ 49.2%. R1-Zero (pure RL, no SFT warm-start) is reported to already reach AIME-level performance competitive with early o1 checkpoints, supporting the paper's central RLVR claim before any of the readability-fixing pipeline stages are applied. Distilled models: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B is reported to outperform OpenAI o1-mini on several reasoning benchmarks; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B sets strong dense-model results. Known limitations: RLVR's benefits are concentrated in domains with cheap, unambiguous verifiers (math, code, some logic) — open-ended generation, subjective quality, and tasks without a checkable ground truth do not benefit from this pipeline the same way, and the secondary RL stage's more conventional preference-reward component is what carries alignment quality in those domains, with correspondingly less rigorous verifiability than the reasoning-stage rewards.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**Confirmed (from the technical report):** the four-stage pipeline (cold-start SFT → reasoning RL → rejection-sampling SFT → secondary RL), R1-Zero as a no-SFT RL-only control, GRPO's group-relative-advantage formulation and its removal of the critic network, rule-based accuracy + format rewards for R1-Zero, ~800K-sample rejection-sampling SFT set (~600K reasoning + ~200K general), distilled model sizes (1.5B/7B/8B/14B/32B/70B) and their Qwen2.5/Llama-3 base lineage, SFT-only (no RL) distillation methodology, and the headline benchmark numbers reported by DeepSeek.

**Speculative / not fully disclosed:** exact GRPO hyperparameters (group size G, KL penalty coefficient β, clip range ε) across each RL stage; the precise composition and scale of the verifiable-reward task distribution (how many math vs. code vs. logic prompts, exact reward-verifier implementation details); the rollout-generation infrastructure (numbers of parallel inference replicas, sandboxed code-execution scale); independent third-party reproduction of the headline benchmark figures at the time of the report versus DeepSeek's self-reported numbers; the exact process-reward-model ablation setup that was reportedly tried and found inferior to outcome rewards (described at a summary level in the paper).

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

- Be able to state precisely why RLVR is not just "RLHF with a different reward model" — the defining distinction is the *absence of a learned approximation layer* between output and reward, not merely that the reward happens to be about correctness.
- Be able to derive GRPO's advantage estimator from scratch and explain exactly what role the group mean plays (an empirical, parameter-free stand-in for PPO's learned value baseline) and what is lost by removing the critic (baseline is per-prompt-group rather than a smooths-across-states value function; requires multiple rollouts per prompt to get a usable baseline at all).
- Be ready to explain why R1-Zero is scientifically important independent of R1 itself: it isolates whether RL-without-SFT can produce reasoning, a question that matters for understanding whether reasoning is "elicited" from a strong base model's latent capability versus "instilled" by imitating curated CoT data.
- Understand the distillation result as a *cost-amortization* argument, not merely a compression trick: the expensive artifact worth producing once is the RL-trained *teacher's traces*, and SFT is a sufficient (and far cheaper) transfer mechanism for the resulting behavior into smaller architectures, including ones from an entirely different model family (Llama).
- Be able to discuss the format-reward + accuracy-reward combination as a minimal, hard-to-game reward design choice, and contrast it with the paper's reported negative result on process reward models at this scale.
