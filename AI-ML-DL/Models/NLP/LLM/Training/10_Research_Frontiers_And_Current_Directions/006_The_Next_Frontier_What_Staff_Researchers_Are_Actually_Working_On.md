## The Next Frontier: What Staff Researchers Are Actually Working On

*Scope note: this file deliberately organizes around research thrusts rather than org charts or team names, precisely because the latter are the least stable, least verifiable, and fastest-changing part of the picture. Where a specific team or program name is mentioned, treat it as illustrative of a thrust's existence, not as a claim about current organizational reality.*

### 0. A capstone, and an explicit epistemic warning

This file synthesizes files 001-005 into a picture of what a staff research engineer at a frontier lab is actually likely to spend their time on, organized around a handful of concrete research thrusts rather than vague futurism.

Before anything else, an honest calibration note: this is being written from public research papers, blog posts, model system cards, and job postings, with a training-data cutoff of January 2026 and a stated current date of July 2026 — meaning there is a real, several-month window in which organizational structures, team names, and publicly stated priorities at both labs may have shifted in ways this file cannot reflect.

Anywhere this file names a specific team, org-chart detail, or "currently working on X" claim, read it as "true as of the most recent information available, quite possibly stale by the time you're reading this" rather than as a live status report. The research *thrusts* themselves — the actual technical problems — are far more durable than any specific organizational detail about who is working on them, and the thrusts are where the real interview-relevant content lives.

Keep this distinction between "durable technical thrust" and "perishable organizational detail" active throughout the rest of this file; it is restated at several points below because it is the single easiest thing to lose track of when a synthesis document like this one starts naming specific teams and programs.

This file is deliberately placed last among the six content files in this module, and it assumes familiarity with all five preceding files rather than re-deriving their arguments — every research thrust below is a pointer back into the specific technical file that actually develops it, and this file's own contribution is the organizing synthesis, not new technical content.

This same restraint applies to every specific number, team name, or program title that appears anywhere below: read all of it as illustrative scaffolding for the underlying technical argument, not as a verified organizational fact sheet.

**Confidence tagging used throughout this file:** every claim below is labeled **[PUBLIC]** — stated directly in published papers, blog posts, or system cards; **[INFERRED]** — a reasonable inference from public information (job postings, published research agendas, patterns across papers) but not a direct statement by the lab; or **[SPECULATION]** — this document's own extrapolation, clearly flagged as such and not to be presented as fact in an interview without that caveat.

### 0.1 How the thrusts below were identified

The eight thrusts in Sections 1-7.1 were identified by looking for the intersection of three signals: a technical problem discussed in this module's five preceding files that is explicitly flagged as unresolved rather than settled; a corresponding published research artifact from at least one of the two labs — a paper, a blog post, a system card section — addressing that specific problem; and, where available, corroborating evidence from public job postings or team-page descriptions that the problem corresponds to an actual, ongoing staffed effort rather than a one-off publication.

Thrusts that satisfied only the first criterion (an unresolved technical problem this module identified, with no corroborating lab publication or staffing signal) were deliberately excluded from the main numbered list and are instead folded into the [SPECULATION]-tagged sub-points within the closest related thrust, to keep the distinction between "this is a confirmed area of lab investment" and "this is a problem I think someone should be working on" as sharp as possible throughout.

This methodology is itself worth being able to explain if asked how this file's content was assembled, since "how did you decide what counts as a real research priority versus your own inference" is exactly the kind of meta-question a sharp interviewer might ask about a document like this one, and having a clear, defensible answer to it is more valuable than any single one of the eight thrusts' specific content.

### 1. Research thrust: chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitorability

**[PUBLIC]** Both OpenAI and Anthropic have published research specifically on this topic — the Lanham et al. and 2025 "Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think" work from Anthropic, OpenAI's own stated design rationale for hidden CoT in the o1/o3 system cards — and have co-signed, alongside researchers from other labs, the 2025 cross-lab position paper on chain-of-thought monitorability as a fragile, worth-preserving property of current reasoning models (see `001_Chain_Of_Thought_Faithfulness_And_Monitorability.md`, Section 6).

This is one of the more unambiguous cases of a publicly stated, shared research priority across nominally competing labs, precisely because the underlying safety concern — can we trust CoT enough to use it as an oversight signal — doesn't depend on which lab's model is being discussed.

**[INFERRED]** Concretely, staff-level work in this area plausibly involves: building and refining perturbation-based and hint-insertion-based faithfulness measurement pipelines — the methodologies in `001_Chain_Of_Thought_Faithfulness_And_Monitorability.md`, Sections 3.2-3.3, are exactly the kind of experiment design this work looks like at the ground level, designing a hint, running it across a matrix of models/training stages, measuring verbalization rate; building production-scale CoT monitoring classifiers that flag concerning reasoning content at inference time, directly analogous to, and possibly literally overlapping with, the redacted-thinking mechanism documented for Claude 3.7 Sonnet (`..\..\Claude\005_Claude3_7_Extended_Thinking.md`, Section 8); and running training-intervention experiments that test whether specific RL recipe choices (reward shaping, KL penalties, explicit faithfulness-adjacent auxiliary rewards) measurably move the faithfulness needle without destroying capability.

This is exactly the kind of ablation-heavy, iterate-fast-at-small-scale-before-committing-frontier-compute work that constitutes a large fraction of actual staff research time, as opposed to the "invent a fully novel technique" work that gets the publicity.

This thrust is a good illustration of a pattern that recurs across this entire file: the publicly visible research artifact (a paper on faithfulness measurement) is the tip of a much larger iceberg of infrastructure-building and small-scale ablation work that never becomes a standalone publication.

**[SPECULATION]** The single hardest open sub-problem within this thrust, in this document's own assessment rather than any lab's stated view, is designing a faithfulness-shaping training signal that survives the Goodhart's-law failure mode described in `001_...`, Section 5 — every proposal this document is aware of for training faithfulness directly, rather than merely measuring it, has some plausible gaming vector, and a staff researcher genuinely advancing this thrust would need to either find a training signal that closes that gap or produce rigorous evidence that no such signal exists without a fundamentally different approach (e.g., an interpretability-based check that doesn't rely on the model's self-report at all).

### 2. Research thrust: scalable oversight and weak-to-strong generalization

**[PUBLIC]** OpenAI published "weak-to-strong generalization" research — training a strong model using only supervision from a weaker model or weaker labels, and measuring how much of the strong model's latent capability can be elicited despite the supervision signal being capped at a lower quality level — as a concrete empirical testbed for the general future problem of how humans, weak supervisors in this framing, can continue to productively oversee AI systems that exceed human capability on the relevant task.

Anthropic has published and continued extending debate-adjacent and "sandwiching" experimental methodologies — placing a model between a non-expert and an expert human to test whether oversight-assisted correctness tracks closer to the expert or the non-expert — and both labs' public alignment research agendas name scalable oversight as a named priority area, independent of and prior to any specific superintelligence timeline claim.

**[INFERRED, with an explicit hedge about org-chart currency]** As of the most recent public information available to this document, OpenAI's dedicated "Superalignment" team, announced 2023 with a stated mandate to solve superintelligence alignment specifically, was reported to have been dissolved in 2024, with its work reportedly absorbed into broader safety research efforts rather than continuing as a separately named team.

This document does not have reliable, current information about OpenAI's exact present-day team structure for this work, and this specific claim should be verified independently rather than repeated confidently in an interview — asking a recruiter or interviewer directly about current team structure is both a reasonable and a well-received question in most interview contexts, and is a far better source than any external document. What is more durably true regardless of org-chart specifics: both labs continue to publish scalable-oversight research — debate variants, weak-to-strong generalization follow-ups, recursive reward modeling proposals — and this remains a named research priority in both companies' public safety documentation, independent of which specific team or org structure currently houses it.

**[INFERRED]** Concrete staff-level work here plausibly includes: designing and running sandwiching-style experiments at increasing capability gaps to test whether oversight techniques degrade as the gap widens, directly testing the obfuscated-arguments concern raised in `004_Multi_Agent_Training_And_Emergent_Behavior.md`, Section 2.3; building the infrastructure to run debate-style training loops at meaningful scale, a nontrivial systems problem in its own right, requiring orchestrating multiple concurrent model rollouts with a judge in the loop; and the unglamorous but essential work of building better human/AI-feedback collection pipelines and analyzing where existing preference-based oversight already silently fails — auditing where RLHF-trained models are already being rewarded for looking-good-to-a-rater rather than being-actually-correct, as a diagnostic precursor to any scalable-oversight fix.

**[SPECULATION]** This thrust's hardest open sub-problem is arguably the obfuscated-arguments concern named precisely in `004_Multi_Agent_Training_And_Emergent_Behavior.md`, Section 2.3 — whether debate's soundness holds as debater capability scales past the judge's own capability to detect a well-constructed flaw. Resolving this convincingly likely requires either a theoretical argument this document is not aware of having been published, or an empirical test at a capability gap larger than any published debate experiment has yet used, which is itself a nontrivial infrastructure and experiment-design undertaking.

### 3. Research thrust: verifiable-reward engineering and reward-hacking mitigation

**[PUBLIC]** Both o1/o3's and DeepSeek-R1's technical materials are explicit that RLVR's entire practical value proposition depends on verifier quality and coverage (`..\..\GPT\008_O1_O3_Reasoning_Models.md`, Section 8; `..\..\OpenSource\008_DeepSeek_R1.md`, Section 8), and DeepSeek's own published negative result on naive process reward models — prone to reward hacking, underperforming outcome-only rewards at their training scale — is a direct, citable data point that this is a live, unsolved engineering and research problem rather than a commodity capability.

**[INFERRED]** This translates into a large, concrete, and — this is worth being honest about — often unglamorous slice of actual staff research/engineering work: building and continuously hardening sandboxed code-execution infrastructure that can run untrusted, model-generated code against test suites at the scale of millions of RL rollouts per training run, safely and without becoming a training-throughput bottleneck; extending the verifiable-reward paradigm beyond math and code into domains that don't have an obvious automatic checker — structured extraction tasks, certain agentic tool-use tasks where "did the tool call actually accomplish the stated goal" is checkable via a secondary verification step even though the task itself isn't as cleanly verifiable as a math answer; and running continuous red-team-style audits specifically looking for reward-hacking exploits in the current verifier/reward-model stack before a training run scales them up into a durable, hard-to-unlearn behavior.

A meaningful fraction of what "RL infrastructure researcher" or "reward modeling researcher" roles at frontier labs actually do, based on public job postings and blog content, is this kind of verifier-hardening and infrastructure-scaling work rather than novel-algorithm invention — GRPO-style algorithmic contributions (`..\..\OpenSource\008_DeepSeek_R1.md`, Section 2) are genuinely valuable and get outsized attention, but the volume of actual staff-hours in this space skews toward the verifier-engineering side.

**[INFERRED]** A specific, concrete illustration of what "extending the verifiable-reward paradigm" looks like in practice: a team might take an agentic tool-use task — say, "book a specific kind of appointment via a web interface" — that has no single clean checker the way a math answer does, and construct a layered verification approach instead.

This could combine an exact check on structured sub-outcomes that are checkable (did the agent's final state show a booking confirmation with the correct date), a secondary model-based judge for softer criteria (was the booking actually the one the user asked for, not merely superficially similar), and a periodic human-audit sample specifically to catch cases where the structured check and the model judge agree with each other but are both wrong — a concrete, three-layered pattern that recurs across many attempts to extend RLVR-style verification into less cleanly checkable domains.

**[SPECULATION]** Whether this kind of layered-verification pattern generalizes well enough to become a standard, reusable component across many agentic task domains, rather than requiring bespoke design for each new task category, is an open engineering question this document has not seen a definitive public answer to.

If it does generalize, it would represent a genuinely useful, reusable piece of infrastructure that a staff researcher entering this thrust could contribute to building rather than reinventing per task — a concrete, medium-term research contribution that sits between "novel algorithm" and "one-off engineering fix" in ambition.

### 4. Research thrust: test-time compute allocation and inference economics

**[PUBLIC]** The `reasoning_effort` parameter (OpenAI) and `budget_tokens` parameter (Anthropic) are both shipped, documented product features, and both companies' public materials frame test-time compute as a first-class, ongoing research axis rather than a one-time feature ship — OpenAI's GPT-5-era product framing around routing between fast and reasoning-capable model variants behind a single product surface is a direct, public statement that compute-allocation-per-query is an active area of continued investment, not a solved problem shipped once and left alone.

**[INFERRED]** Staff-level work plausibly includes: training and evaluating difficulty-classification routers that decide, per query, how much test-time compute to allocate — the deployment-layer instance of the compute-optimal-allocation research question in `003_Test_Time_Compute_And_Inference_Scaling_Research.md`, Section 3.3; researching better process reward models and search algorithms to make tree-search-style test-time methods viable in production despite the reward-hacking concerns in Section 3 above, and this thrust and the verifier-engineering thrust overlap substantially in practice, since a better PRM is valuable both for RL training and for inference-time search; and the pure systems-and-serving work of making variable-length, budget-controlled generation efficient at scale — KV-cache management for very long reasoning traces, continuous batching that accommodates wildly variable per-request compute budgets — which connects directly to inference-serving-systems work covered in `..\08_Inference_And_Serving_Systems\` and is a genuine point of overlap between "research" and "systems" job functions at this frontier, worth naming explicitly since the boundary between the two roles is blurrier here than in most other parts of the stack.

**[INFERRED]** A researcher on this thrust is likely to spend real time in direct collaboration with the serving-infrastructure team, since a router policy or an allocation heuristic that looks good in an offline evaluation can fail in production for purely systems reasons — a router that assumes a certain latency budget for the "cheap" path can be invalidated by a serving-capacity change that has nothing to do with the model or the routing algorithm itself, which means this thrust's researchers cannot fully abstract away from the underlying serving stack the way a purely model-level researcher might hope to.

**[SPECULATION]** As reasoning-model deployment matures, it is a reasonable extrapolation that "test-time compute allocation" will increasingly be treated as a single, unified optimization problem spanning model-level stopping policies, router-level difficulty classification, and serving-level capacity planning, rather than three separately-owned pieces — though this document is not aware of a public statement from either lab describing their organization in exactly these terms.

### 5. Research thrust: synthetic data, self-improvement, and data efficiency under a possible data constraint

**[PUBLIC]** Both DeepSeek-R1's distillation pipeline and the broader industry-wide use of rejection-sampling-plus-SFT loops (`002_Self_Improvement_And_Synthetic_Data_Flywheels.md`, Section 3) are published, working techniques, and Epoch AI's public data-stock analyses (`005_Open_Problems_In_Scaling_And_Data_Efficiency.md`, Section 1) are cited widely enough across the field's public discourse to constitute a shared, acknowledged concern, even without every lab explicitly confirming it drives their own strategy.

**[INFERRED]** Concretely: designing and continuously monitoring anti-collapse safeguards for any pipeline that trains on model-generated data at scale — tracking output-diversity metrics, deliberately mixing in fresh human/verified data as an anti-drift anchor, directly the mitigations discussed in `002_Self_Improvement_And_Synthetic_Data_Flywheels.md`, Section 5; extending verifiable-reward-style anchoring to new domains specifically to unlock more self-improvement-loop headroom without the model-collapse risk that comes from unanchored synthetic data; and running the kind of internal data-value-measurement research proposed in `005_Open_Problems_In_Scaling_And_Data_Efficiency.md`, Section 5 — marginal-loss-per-token tracking, held-out canary evaluation for saturation — as a genuinely load-bearing, if invisible-from-outside, piece of frontier-lab research infrastructure.

A lab that doesn't know whether it's approaching a data constraint for its own specific corpus and roadmap is flying blind on a first-order resourcing decision, and building the internal measurement apparatus to answer that question is real staff-level research work, even though it produces no public paper.

**[INFERRED]** This thrust also plausibly includes direct collaboration with data-licensing and partnership teams — the institutional-anchoring pattern described in `002_Self_Improvement_And_Synthetic_Data_Flywheels.md`, Section 10.1 — where a research team's finding that a particular domain's synthetic data pipeline is showing early collapse signatures directly informs a commercial decision about whether to invest in a human-verified data partnership for that specific domain instead. This is a concrete example of research findings feeding into a non-research organizational decision, which is a recurring pattern across several of this file's thrusts, not unique to this one.

The general shape — a research finding about a technical risk directly informing a commercial or resourcing decision, rather than remaining purely academic — recurs across Sections 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 of this file, and is arguably a more reliable signature of "this is genuinely staff-level work" than any specific technical sophistication requirement, since it is the cross-functional consequence of the work, not merely its technical content, that distinguishes it from a purely academic research contribution.

**[SPECULATION]** Given the switching-cost argument raised in `005_Open_Problems_In_Scaling_And_Data_Efficiency.md`, Section 4.4, it is a reasonable guess that a nontrivial share of this thrust's research effort goes toward extending the current recipe's data efficiency rather than toward architectural alternatives, simply because the former has a much lower activation cost than the latter — though this document cannot confirm the actual internal allocation between these two kinds of effort at either lab.

### 6. Research thrust: agentic deployment safety and multi-agent robustness

**[PUBLIC]** Both labs have shipped increasingly autonomous, long-horizon agentic products — Claude Code, computer-use agents, OpenAI's own agentic tool-use products — and both have published dangerous-capability evaluation frameworks (responsible scaling policies, preparedness frameworks) that explicitly include autonomy- and agentic-uplift-relevant evaluation categories. This is public, named, and a growing fraction of both companies' safety-research public output.

**[INFERRED]** Staff-level work plausibly includes: building and continuously extending red-team suites specifically targeting agentic failure modes — prompt injection via tool outputs, an agent being manipulated by adversarial content it encounters mid-task, long-horizon task drift where an agent's behavior degrades or goes off-course over many sequential tool-use steps in a way a single-turn evaluation would never surface; and — this is the piece most directly connected to `004_Multi_Agent_Training_And_Emergent_Behavior.md`, Section 5 — beginning to grapple with evaluation and robustness questions for agents that will predictably interact with other, non-cooperative agents in deployment, a problem area this document has already flagged as thin on published research relative to its likely near-term importance.

**[INFERRED]** A further, better-corroborated data point for this thrust: both companies' published responsible-scaling and preparedness-framework documents include specific evaluation categories for autonomous replication and self-exfiltration risk — a model's capacity to autonomously copy itself, acquire resources, or persist without human oversight — which is a distinct, named evaluation category from the agentic-tool-use safety work discussed above, and is a concrete, publicly-documented example of staff-level work that exists specifically to gate a launch decision rather than to produce a publishable capability result.

**[SPECULATION, clearly flagged]** It is a reasonable extrapolation, but genuinely this document's own speculation rather than a confirmed priority, that as protocols like MCP and other agent-to-agent communication standards mature, both labs will need to invest specifically in adversarial-agent test suites — red-teaming your agent against an adversarially-optimized *other* AI agent, not just against a fixed prompt-injection corpus or a human red-teamer — as a distinct evaluation category from today's more mature agentic-safety evaluation practice.

This is not yet, as far as this document's sources can confirm, a named, published research program at either lab, but it follows fairly directly from the trajectory both companies' own agentic product roadmaps are visibly on.

**[INFERRED]** A concrete illustration of current agentic-safety work: an agent capable of autonomous multi-step web browsing needs to be tested against adversarially-crafted web content specifically designed to hijack its behavior mid-task — a webpage that contains hidden instructions targeting the agent rather than the human user, for instance. Building and continuously updating a red-team corpus of this kind, and re-running it against every new model checkpoint before deployment, is exactly the kind of recurring, infrastructure-heavy safety work this thrust actually consists of, distinct from the more one-off, capability-focused red-teaming that might be associated with a pre-launch safety review for a non-agentic model.

**[SPECULATION]** As this document's `004_Multi_Agent_Training_And_Emergent_Behavior.md`, Section 5.4, argues, the economic pressure to deploy agents with real transactional authority is likely rising faster than the research base for testing them against adversarial counterpart agents specifically — which suggests, speculatively, that this sub-area could become one of the more understaffed-relative-to-urgency research thrusts in this entire file over the next few years, though this document has no direct evidence of current staffing levels at either lab to confirm or refute that guess.

This is precisely the kind of judgment call — identifying an area that seems under-resourced relative to its likely importance, without being able to confirm the staffing gap directly — that a staff-level researcher is expected to be able to make and act on independently, rather than waiting for an explicit organizational mandate before investing attention in it.

### 7. Research thrust: mechanistic interpretability as a complementary bet

**[PUBLIC]** Anthropic's interpretability team has published extensively on dictionary learning / sparse autoencoders for extracting human-interpretable features and circuits from model internals, and has stated this as a long-term bet on understanding models well enough to verify claims about their cognition directly from weights and activations, rather than relying solely on behavioral evidence — like CoT, covered in file 001 — for safety assurance. OpenAI has also published interpretability-adjacent work, though with a smaller public research footprint in this specific sub-area relative to Anthropic's.

This thrust is covered in depth in `..\07_Safety_Alignment_And_Responsible_Scaling\` rather than duplicated here; the connection worth making explicitly in an interview is that interpretability and CoT-faithfulness research are **complementary, not redundant**, bets on how to achieve model transparency.

CoT-based monitoring is behavioral — it reads what the model says about its own reasoning, with the faithfulness caveats of file 001 fully in force — while mechanistic interpretability aims at a lower, more mechanistic level: what the weights and activations are actually doing, independent of what the model claims about itself. A lab pursuing both is hedging against the specific risk that CoT-based monitoring alone turns out to be insufficiently reliable, exactly the risk file 001 documents evidence for, by having a second, structurally independent verification method in development in parallel.

**[PUBLIC]** Concretely, published interpretability work has demonstrated the ability to extract individual, human-interpretable features from a model's internal activations at meaningful scale — a genuinely significant engineering and research achievement, since it requires solving a hard unsupervised-learning problem, decomposing a dense, superposed activation space into sparse, individually-meaningful directions, before any actual interpretation of what a given feature represents becomes possible at all.

**[INFERRED]** Staff-level work on this thrust plausibly spans a spectrum from foundational methods research — better sparse-autoencoder training recipes, better circuit-discovery techniques — to applied work using existing interpretability tools to answer a specific, safety-relevant question about a specific model checkpoint, the latter being more analogous in shape to the applied, ablation-heavy work described for other thrusts in this file, while the former is closer to genuinely open-ended methods research with a less predictable payoff timeline.

### 7.1 Research thrust: evaluation science and benchmark design

**[PUBLIC]** Both labs, and the broader field, have publicly acknowledged the benchmark-contamination problem discussed in `005_Open_Problems_In_Scaling_And_Data_Efficiency.md`, Section 6.1, and both labs maintain internal evaluation suites that go well beyond what is publicly released, precisely because public benchmarks decay in usefulness over time as they become contaminated or saturated.

Published papers from both organizations on evaluation methodology — how to measure genuine capability rather than benchmark-specific pattern matching, how to construct evaluations resistant to memorization — constitute a real, citable, if less publicly prominent research thrust than the other seven in this file.

**[INFERRED]** This thrust overlaps substantially with several others already covered: the CoT monitorability recovery-rate metric proposed in `001_Chain_Of_Thought_Faithfulness_And_Monitorability.md`, Section 6.4, the dangerous-capability evaluation protocol questions raised in `003_Test_Time_Compute_And_Inference_Scaling_Research.md`, Section 5.3, and the held-out canary evaluation practice proposed in `005_Open_Problems_In_Scaling_And_Data_Efficiency.md`, Section 5, are all, at bottom, evaluation-science problems.

A staff researcher whose primary thrust is evaluation science is likely to be a common, if less publicly glamorous, thread running underneath most of the other six thrusts in this file, rather than a fully separate specialization — building and maintaining the measurement infrastructure that every other thrust's empirical claims ultimately depend on.

**[SPECULATION]** Given how many of this module's own "open research questions" sections (across files 001-005) ultimately resolve to "we need a better way to measure X," it is a reasonable, if unconfirmed, inference that evaluation science is one of the more chronically understaffed-relative-to-its-importance research areas at frontier labs — a pattern that shows up across the ML field generally, where building evaluation infrastructure is less immediately rewarded, in publication and promotion terms, than producing a novel capability result, even when the evaluation infrastructure is what makes the capability result trustworthy in the first place.

This pattern, if real, would be a specific instance of a much more general phenomenon in applied ML research: the incentive structure that rewards novel, publishable results does not automatically reward the equally necessary work of building and maintaining the measurement infrastructure that determines whether those results are trustworthy — and a candidate who can articulate this tension clearly, and who expresses genuine interest in the less-rewarded measurement work rather than only the more publicly visible capability work, is signaling exactly the kind of judgment a staff-level hiring process is likely to value.

### 8. What a staff researcher's actual day-to-day looks like

It is worth deflating the popular image of "staff researcher invents a new architecture" against what public job postings, blog posts, and the shape of the research thrusts above actually suggest the job looks like day to day.

- **Small-scale experiment iteration before any frontier-scale commitment.** Testing a hypothesis — a new reward-shaping term, a new data-filtering heuristic, a faithfulness-measurement methodology — at 1B-7B parameter scale, with fast iteration cycles, before anyone commits the compute budget to test it at frontier scale. This is true across essentially every research thrust above, and is a large fraction of actual daily work regardless of which specific thrust a given researcher sits in.
- **Eval and infrastructure engineering.** Building the measurement apparatus itself — a faithfulness probe, a verifier/sandboxed-execution pipeline, a difficulty-classification router, a red-team harness — is frequently a larger time investment than running the experiment the apparatus was built to support, and is treated as first-class research contribution at this level, not merely "engineering support" for someone else's research.
- **Paper reading, reproduction, and adaptation.** A meaningful fraction of the work described across Sections 1-7 is adapting and extending published external results — Lanham et al.'s perturbation methodology, Snell et al.'s compute-optimal framework, Muennighoff et al.'s data-constrained scaling law — to a lab's own specific models and constraints, rather than working purely from a blank page. Staff-level research fluency very much includes knowing the relevant literature precisely enough to reproduce and extend it quickly.
- **Cross-functional work with policy, safety, and product teams.** Especially for the thrusts in Sections 1, 2, and 6, a real fraction of staff time goes into translating a research finding into an actual deployment decision or safety-evaluation gate — does this faithfulness result change what we're willing to certify about a model's CoT-based safety monitoring before shipping it, does this dangerous-capability eval result change a launch decision. This is explicitly cross-functional, not purely a research-lab-in-isolation activity, and is a distinguishing feature of "research" roles at frontier labs relative to a more purely academic research position.
- **A large, unglamorous fraction of the work is unglamorous.** Debugging a broken reward pipeline, tracking down why a training run's KL penalty produced an unexpected collapse, auditing a data pipeline for contamination — none of this appears in a paper abstract, and all of it is a real, large fraction of what determines whether the polished research result at the top of this file's earlier sections is trustworthy at all.
- **Writing, and reading, a lot of internal documentation.** A staff-level researcher's output is not only code and experiment results — it is frequently a clearly-argued internal document explaining why a particular approach was or wasn't worth pursuing further, written specifically so that the next person to touch the same problem area doesn't have to rediscover the same negative results from scratch. This module's own "confirmed versus inferred versus speculation" discipline is directly modeled on the kind of epistemic hygiene this internal-documentation function actually requires in practice.
- **Calibrating claims for a specific audience.** The same research finding gets communicated differently to a fellow researcher (full technical detail, including caveats and failure modes), to a cross-functional safety review (framed around the specific decision the finding should inform), and to leadership (framed around resourcing and prioritization implications) — being able to make this translation accurately, without either overclaiming to leadership or underexplaining to a technical peer, is itself a skill staff-level roles are expected to have developed.

### 8.2 A worked example of how one thrust actually decomposes into weekly work

Taking the verifier-engineering thrust (Section 3) as a concrete illustration of how a single research thrust decomposes into actual weekly tasks, since it is one of the more operationally concrete examples: a researcher on this thrust might spend one week auditing a specific class of code-verification test suites for coverage gaps (does the suite catch a model that satisfies the letter of a test but not its intent), the next week prototyping a fix and re-running a small-scale RL training job to check whether the fix changes training dynamics in an unexpected way, the following week presenting the finding to a cross-functional review that includes both other researchers and a safety-adjacent reviewer who checks whether the change has any safety-relevant side effects, and the week after that moving on to a different, unrelated verifier gap identified during the audit.
