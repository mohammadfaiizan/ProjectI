# Post-Training Datasets: SFT, Preference, and Verifiable-Reward Data

## 1. Framing the Problem

Pretraining data (covered in `001_Pretraining_Data_Sources_And_Composition.md` and its companion files in this directory) is fundamentally an *abundance* problem: the challenge is filtering, deduplicating, and mixing a supply of text that is, if anything, too large — trillions of tokens of raw web crawl, curated corpora, code, and books, engineered down into a corpus a fixed compute budget can actually consume. Post-training data is the opposite problem. It is a *scarcity* problem, because the thing being collected is not naturally occurring text scraped from somewhere — it is a purpose-built record of a human (or, increasingly, a verifier program) judging or producing a specific response to a specific prompt, and every one of those records has to be manufactured, one at a time, through a deliberately designed collection process.

This distinction matters more than it sounds like it should, because it changes what kind of problem the team building the dataset is actually solving. Pretraining-data engineering is mostly a systems and filtering problem: build pipelines that can process petabytes, apply the right quality heuristics, and get token-efficient signal out of a supply that already exists. Post-training-data engineering is mostly an *operational and human-systems* problem: write labeler instructions precise enough that hundreds of different people converge on similar judgments, screen and qualify the people who will produce the data, build an interface that elicits reliable signal rather than noise, and — once RL-with-verifiable-rewards entered the picture — build and validate automatic verifiers that are themselves correct. None of pretraining's usual filtering machinery (perplexity filters, MinHash deduplication, quality classifiers) is the bottleneck here. The bottleneck is designing a process that produces a small number of very high-signal examples, where "small" is thousands to low hundreds-of-thousands rather than billions, and where the *process* by which each example was produced is itself the thing that determines the resulting model's behavior.

This file covers three data types that sit downstream of a pretrained base model and upstream of (or embedded inside) the alignment/RL machinery described in depth elsewhere in this collection: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) demonstration data, human preference/comparison data used to train reward models, and verifiable-reward task data used for RL-with-verifiable-rewards (RLVR). The algorithmic mechanics of what is *done* with this data — the Bradley-Terry reward-model loss, PPO, GRPO, KL penalties — are covered in `../../GPT/004_InstructGPT_And_RLHF.md` and `../../OpenSource/008_DeepSeek_R1.md`; this file's job is the data side: where each dataset comes from, how it is collected, what makes it good or bad, and why the collection process itself is a first-class design decision rather than a preprocessing footnote.

## 2. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Demonstration Data

### 2.1 The prompt-distribution problem: coverage is a curation decision, not a given

Before any demonstration is written, someone has to decide *what to ask*. This sounds trivial and is not. An SFT dataset teaches a model, by direct imitation, how to respond to the class of inputs it contains — so if the prompt distribution is narrow or skewed, the resulting model's competence is narrow or skewed in exactly the same way, regardless of how well-written the demonstrations themselves are. A dataset that is 80% "write me an essay about X" and 5% "debug this code" will produce a model that is disproportionately good at essays and disproportionately bad at debugging, independent of demonstration quality on either category.

Labs address this with a deliberate, explicit taxonomy of use-case categories, and then actively source or write prompts to fill each category rather than passively accepting whatever prompt distribution shows up first. InstructGPT's SFT prompt set is a concrete, disclosed example of this discipline: OpenAI used a mix of prompts labelers were asked to write themselves *across explicit categories* — generation, open question-answering, closed question-answering, brainstorming, chat, rewriting, summarization, classification, and a catch-all "other" free-form category — deliberately engineered for category coverage, blended with prompts drawn from real early OpenAI Playground/API traffic (filtered for personally identifiable information and de-duplicated per customer, so that no single heavy API user's idiosyncratic use case dominates the distribution). Both halves of that mixture are attempts to solve the same coverage problem from different directions: labeler-authored prompts guarantee coverage of categories a team believes matter even if real traffic hasn't produced enough examples of them yet; real-traffic prompts guarantee the dataset reflects what people actually ask a deployed system, which is not fully predictable in advance and does not decompose neatly into a hand-designed taxonomy.

The general principle that generalizes well beyond InstructGPT: prompt-set construction is itself a curation exercise with the same character as pretraining-mixture design (Section 5 of `001_Pretraining_Data_Sources_And_Composition.md`) — a team is making an explicit bet about which slices of task-space matter enough to guarantee representation, and under-covering a category in the *prompt* distribution cannot be fixed later by writing better demonstrations for the prompts that did make it in.

### 2.2 Human-written-from-scratch demonstrations

The most direct way to collect SFT data is to give a skilled human labeler a prompt and have them write, entirely by hand, the response the model should ideally produce. This is the approach InstructGPT used for the bulk of its initial SFT set: for each sampled prompt, a labeler drafts a full, considered answer to what they judge the user actually wants — a direct instantiation of the "ideal demonstration" concept, with no model output in the loop at all as a starting point.

The mechanical cost structure of this approach is the important thing to internalize, because it is what determines dataset size for every lab that does this. Writing a genuinely good, well-reasoned answer to an open-ended prompt — one that correctly interprets ambiguous intent, is factually careful, is appropriately formatted, and reads as something a thoughtful expert would actually say — takes real human time on the order of minutes per example, not seconds, especially for anything beyond a short factual query (a multi-paragraph explanation, a piece of code with correct edge-case handling, a nuanced response to a sensitive question). That per-example cost is why pure from-scratch human SFT datasets are, across the field, small by ML-dataset standards: InstructGPT's own SFT set was on the order of ~13,000 (prompt, demonstration) pairs — not millions, not even one million — a figure worth holding in mind as a calibration point precisely because it contradicts an intuition trained by pretraining-scale numbers. A dataset three to four orders of magnitude smaller than a curated pretraining corpus can nonetheless be the dataset that most directly shapes a model's surface behavior, because unlike pretraining tokens (one weak signal among trillions), every SFT example is a directly-imitated exemplar the model is trained to reproduce with high fidelity.

This also explains why almost no lab relies on pure from-scratch human writing as its only SFT data source once a reasonably capable model already exists — it is simply too slow and too expensive to scale past low tens of thousands of examples, and later-stage SFT sets (rejection-sampled data, model-assisted editing, distillation from a stronger model's outputs) exist specifically to get more coverage and volume without paying the from-scratch-writing cost on every single example.

### 2.3 Model-generated-then-human-edited demonstrations

The alternative that has become dominant once a lab already has a reasonably capable model to work from: sample a candidate response from an existing model for a given prompt, then have a human labeler *edit* that draft — correcting factual errors, fixing tone, tightening or restructuring the response, removing anything unsafe or off-target — rather than writing a response from a blank page. The final training target is the edited version, not the raw model draft, so genuine human judgment is still injected into the label; what changes is the labeler's task, from "generate" to "critique and repair."

The reason this is faster is a straightforward cognitive-load argument: editing a plausible existing draft is measurably cheaper than composing equivalent-quality text from nothing, in the same way that editing a colleague's first draft of a document is faster than writing that document yourself from a blank page, provided the draft is already in the right neighborhood. Once a base or lightly-tuned model is capable enough that its typical draft is *mostly* right — correct structure, roughly the right content, just needing polish rather than wholesale replacement — the marginal human labor per example drops sharply, and a labeling operation can produce many more edited demonstrations per labeler-hour than it could produce from-scratch demonstrations. This is the primary lever labs use to scale SFT data volume beyond the low-tens-of-thousands regime described in Section 2.2, and it is also the mechanism by which a lab's own frontier model becomes an input to training its own (or a smaller/future) model's SFT data, rather than every SFT set being independent of prior model generations.

The risk this method introduces is genuine and worth naming precisely rather than treating edit-based collection as a strict improvement over from-scratch writing. **Anchoring bias** — a well-documented cognitive bias in judgment-and-decision-making research, in which a person's estimate or evaluation is pulled toward an initial reference point they were given, even when that reference point is arbitrary or wrong — applies directly to this setting. A labeler shown a fluent, plausible-looking draft has a systematic tendency to under-correct it: to accept sentences and structural choices that are "good enough" rather than critically re-deriving what the ideal response would look like if they were starting from nothing, because the draft itself sets an implicit reference point for "roughly how good this response should be" that is hard to consciously override even when a labeler is explicitly instructed to be rigorous. The practical consequence is that edit-based SFT data can be systematically lighter-touch than the label "human-reviewed" suggests, and can silently propagate the base model's existing stylistic tics, mild factual sloppiness, or characteristic ways of hedging and padding forward into a dataset that is nominally "human-approved ground truth." This is not a hypothetical risk unique to any one lab's pipeline — it is a structural property of any human-in-the-loop editing process, and it is one of the reasons quality-control measures (spot-checking edited examples against fresh from-scratch re-writes, tracking how much labelers actually change per example, deliberately routing a fraction of prompts to from-scratch writing even after a model-assisted pipeline exists) show up in serious SFT-data operations rather than being treated as unnecessary once model-assisted editing is available.

### 2.4 Labeler qualification, training, and guidelines as an operational design problem

Whichever collection method is used, the actual mechanism connecting demonstration data to model behavior is direct imitation: SFT trains the model, via ordinary autoregressive cross-entropy computed only over completion tokens (loss masked on the prompt, as detailed in `../../GPT/004_InstructGPT_And_RLHF.md`, Section 6), to reproduce whatever pattern of response the demonstrations exhibit. This has a consequence that is easy to state abstractly but worth sitting with concretely: the model does not learn some Platonic ideal of "a good response" — it learns to imitate *this specific population of labelers, writing under this specific set of instructions*. Any systematic tendency shared across a meaningful fraction of the labeler pool — a habit of hedging claims, a particular register of formality, a shared blind spot about what counts as a harmful request, a shared misreading of an ambiguous guideline — becomes, after SFT, a systematic tendency of the model, indistinguishable at the model level from an intentional design choice. InstructGPT's own paper is explicit about this: the resulting model is aligned to *this labeler population's preferences under these specific instructions*, not to some demographically neutral or universal standard, and this is a sourcing limitation carried forward into essentially every RLHF-descended model since, not a solved problem unique to one paper.

This is exactly why labeler qualification and training is treated, in any serious operation, as first-class infrastructure rather than a hiring-logistics afterthought. Before being trusted on production data, labelers typically go through a **screening/qualification process**: a test set of tasks with known-good answers or known-difficult edge cases, used to filter for labelers who demonstrate the judgment and attentiveness the operation needs (InstructGPT's contractor pool, hired via Upwork and ScaleAI, was screened via exactly this kind of qualification test, reportedly aimed in part at selecting for sensitivity to how different demographic groups might read a given output and at the ability to reliably flag potentially harmful content). Beyond initial screening, labelers work from **written guidelines** that attempt to make explicit what would otherwise be tacit, individually-varying judgment: expected tone and register, safety boundaries (what to refuse, how to refuse it, what counts as borderline), factuality/citation expectations, formatting conventions, and worked examples of "good" versus "bad" responses for the task types the labeler will see. The guideline document is, in a real sense, the actual specification of the model's eventual personality — far more so than any architecture choice — because every labeler reading that document and internalizing its judgment calls is a channel through which the document's specific choices get imprinted onto thousands of individual demonstrations, which then get imprinted, through cross-entropy loss, onto the model's weights. A team that wants to understand *why* a shipped model has a particular default tone, a particular pattern of hedging, or a particular set of things it will and won't do without being asked twice, is often better served by reading that team's labeler guidelines (where disclosed) than by looking at anything architectural.

## 3. Preference and Comparison Data for Reward Modeling

### 3.1 The pairwise/k-wise ranking interface, mechanically

Preference data collection looks structurally different from SFT-demonstration collection, because the labeler is not asked to produce text at all — they are shown a single prompt together with two or more candidate completions generated by the model (or by several different model snapshots), and asked to indicate a *relative* judgment: which of two completions is better, or, in the k-wise variant, how a small set of completions (InstructGPT used K = 4 to 9 completions per prompt) should be ordered from best to worst. No labeler in this pipeline writes a response; the entire signal is a comparative judgment over responses someone (or something) else already generated.

A minimal record from this kind of collection, in the pairwise form actually consumed by reward-model training, looks like this:

```json
{
  "prompt": "Explain why the sky is blue, for a curious 10-year-old.",
  "response_chosen": "Sunlight looks white, but it's actually made of all the colors mixed together...",
  "response_rejected": "Rayleigh scattering causes shorter wavelengths to be scattered more...",
  "labeler_id": "L-2291",
  "annotation_source": "human",
  "comparison_id": "cmp-88213",
  "prompt_category": "open_qa",
  "rank_position_source": 1
}
```

`response_chosen` and `response_rejected` are the two completions the labeler compared; `rank_position_source` records that this particular pair was derived from positions 1 and (say) 3 of a larger k-wise ranking rather than an independent head-to-head comparison, which matters for the loss-weighting discussion in Section 3.3. In practice, production datasets carry considerably more metadata per record than this (model/checkpoint identity for each completion, timestamps, guideline version the labeler was working under, sometimes a free-text rationale), but the essential unit of signal is exactly this: a prompt, a pair of completions, and a judgment of which one is preferred.

### 3.2 Why comparative judgment is preferred over absolute scoring

It would be simpler, on its face, to ask a labeler to rate a single completion on an absolute numeric scale — "score this response 1 to 10" — and skip the comparison machinery entirely. Labs consistently do not do this as the primary signal, and the reason is a well-established finding in psychometrics and preference-elicitation research generally, not something specific to language models: humans are demonstrably more reliable and more internally consistent at *relative* judgments ("is A better than B?") than at *absolute* judgments ("how good is A, on its own, on a fixed numeric scale?").

The failure mode absolute scales are prone to is calibration drift: a labeler's internal sense of what "a 7" means is not a fixed, portable unit — it shifts across the course of a single labeling session as they see more examples, it shifts across labelers who have never explicitly synchronized their internal scales with each other, and it shifts across time as a labeling program runs for months and the population of labelers and the population of prompts both evolve. Two labelers can look at the identical response and, in good faith, assign it a 6 and a 9, not because either is being careless but because "9" simply denotes a different absolute standard in each person's head — and worse, the *same* labeler's own "9" from week one may not mean the same thing as their "9" from week six. None of this is a problem the labeling instructions can fully fix by writing a more detailed rubric, because the core issue is that an absolute scale asks for a judgment with no local anchor, and human judgment is comparatively bad at holding an absolute anchor stable over time and across people.

A pairwise (or k-wise) comparison sidesteps this almost entirely, because it only requires a *locally consistent ordinal* judgment: given these two specific responses to this specific prompt, sitting side by side in front of the labeler right now, which one do I prefer? That judgment does not depend on the labeler's notion of what a universal "9" means, does not need to be stable across sessions or across labelers in absolute terms, and is empirically far more reproducible — labelers shown the same pair tend to agree with each other, and with their own past selves, on "which is better" far more often than they would agree on "what absolute score is this." This is the direct, practical reason InstructGPT and essentially every subsequent RLHF pipeline collects comparative rankings rather than per-response absolute scores as the primary signal feeding reward-model training.

### 3.3 From k-wise rankings to pairwise training data

A k-wise ranking of `K` completions is more label-efficient to collect than a series of independent pairwise judgments, because a single full ordering over `K` items implies a preference for every pair within that ordering — `C(K, 2)` pairwise comparisons extracted from one labeler's ranking pass, rather than requiring `C(K, 2)` separate labeling interactions. InstructGPT's paper is explicit about doing exactly this: rank K = 4 to 9 sampled completions per prompt from best to worst, then decompose that ranking into all `C(K, 2)` pairwise comparisons for reward-model training, each pair treated (with the loss-normalization caveat below) as a training example.

The actual training use of a pairwise comparison, at the level relevant to a data-focused discussion, is straightforward to state without re-deriving the full Bradley-Terry model: a reward model `r_theta(x, y)` produces a scalar score for a (prompt, response) pair, and the training loss for a comparison with preferred response `y_w` and rejected response `y_l` pushes `r_theta(x, y_w)` above `r_theta(x, y_l)` — concretely, the loss is (up to sign and normalization) the negative log-sigmoid of the score difference, `-log(sigmoid(r_theta(x, y_w) - r_theta(x, y_l)))`, which is exactly logistic regression on the *difference* between two learned scores. The full derivation of why this is maximum-likelihood estimation under the Bradley-Terry pairwise-choice model, and how the resulting reward model then feeds PPO, is covered in `../../GPT/004_InstructGPT_And_RLHF.md`, Section 6 — the point worth carrying into this file is narrower: every unit of preference data that a reward model consumes is, structurally, a *pair* with a designated winner and loser, and k-wise ranking is simply a labeling-UX-level trick for harvesting many such pairs per unit of expensive labeler time, not a fundamentally different kind of supervision from pairwise comparison.

One data-quality subtlety worth flagging, because it is a genuine trap: the `C(K,2)` pairs derived from a single ranking are **not independent data points**, even though a naive implementation might treat them as such. They all condition on the same prompt and largely the same handful of completions, so a prompt with K = 9 contributes 36 highly correlated pairs while a prompt with K = 4 contributes only 6 — treating each pair as an i.i.d. training example both overweights prompts that happened to get a larger K and risks overfitting, since the *effective* number of independent judgments per prompt is closer to K than to `C(K,2)`. InstructGPT's mitigation — normalizing the loss per prompt by the number of comparisons it contributes and batching all of a prompt's `C(K,2)` pairs together for training efficiency — is a data-engineering detail specific to preference data that has no real analogue in SFT-data collection, where each demonstration is already a single independent unit.

### 3.4 Labeler guidelines as a values artifact, not a neutral technical spec

The instructions given to preference labelers have to specify what "better" means along several dimensions at once, and the standard set that recurs across labs' disclosed guidelines includes: **helpfulness** (does the response actually address what the user asked, correctly interpreting intent even when the request is ambiguous or underspecified), **honesty/factual accuracy** (is the content true, appropriately calibrated, and free of fabricated specifics), **harmlessness/safety** (does the response avoid facilitating harm, respect stated boundaries, and handle sensitive requests appropriately), **format and length adherence** (does the response match reasonable expectations for the task — a one-line factual question probably should not receive a five-paragraph essay), and **tone** (is the response appropriately calibrated in register, warmth, and directness for the request).

The genuinely hard part of writing these guidelines is not defining each dimension in isolation — it is that the dimensions routinely **trade off against each other on the same comparison**, and a labeler has to resolve that trade-off consistently, which means the guidelines have to give them a way to do so. A response that is maximally direct and thorough in addressing a borderline request may be less safe than a more cautious alternative; a maximally cautious, heavily-hedged response may be judged less helpful, less confident, or more evasive than a user actually wanted. There is no dimension-free way to adjudicate "which of these two responses is better" once the dimensions disagree — the guideline has to encode, explicitly or implicitly, a **priority ordering or weighting** across helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness for the labeler to fall back on, and that priority ordering is a genuine values and product decision, not a neutral technical artifact that any two reasonable teams would independently converge on. Two labs can write guidelines that both claim to optimize for "helpful, honest, and harmless" outputs and still produce meaningfully different models, because the specific way each lab's guidelines resolve helpfulness-versus-harmlessness tension on the hard, ambiguous cases is exactly where the guidelines' real content lives — the easy cases where all three dimensions agree barely need a rubric at all. This is a concrete, citable instance of the more general point from Section 2.4: a model's default behavior on hard cases is downstream of a rubric-writing decision made by a small internal team, not an emergent, values-neutral property of the training procedure.

### 3.5 Inter-annotator agreement: what it measures and why it matters

Because preference judgments are the direct training signal for a reward model, and a reward model's reliability is the single largest lever on how safely RL can be pushed against it (Section 4.2 and `../../GPT/004_InstructGPT_And_RLHF.md`, Section 6/8, on RM overoptimization), measuring how *consistent* the underlying preference signal actually is matters as a first-order data-quality question, not a nice-to-have audit.

The standard method is to have a subset of comparisons independently labeled by two or more labelers who do not see each other's judgments, then measure how often they agree. The simplest statistic is raw agreement rate — the fraction of comparisons where independent labelers picked the same winner — but raw agreement is inflated by the base rate of easy, low-ambiguity comparisons (if 80% of comparisons have an obviously-better response, two labelers will "agree" 80% of the time even if they are each guessing randomly on the genuinely hard 20%), so a chance-corrected statistic — Cohen's kappa for two annotators, Fleiss' kappa when more than two independently label the same item — is the more informative figure, since it explicitly subtracts out the agreement rate expected from chance alone and reports how much agreement exists *beyond* that baseline.

The reason low agreement matters mechanically, rather than just being a data-quality curiosity, is twofold. First, when two independent, reasonably careful labelers frequently disagree about which of two responses is better for some class of prompts, the most defensible reading is usually not "one of them is wrong" — it is that the underlying preference being measured is genuinely noisy, subjective, or actively contested for that class of prompt (open-ended creative writing quality, contested value judgments, humor, matters of taste), and no amount of additional guideline-writing will fully resolve genuine, legitimate disagreement among reasonable people into a single ground truth. Second, and more consequentially for downstream training: a reward model trained on a noisier comparison signal is itself a noisier, less reliable proxy for whatever underlying preference it is meant to approximate, and a noisier reward model is more exploitable during RL — the policy has more room to find outputs that score well under the reward model's own error/variance rather than under genuine human preference, which is precisely the RM-overoptimization/reward-hacking failure mode discussed at the algorithmic level in `../../GPT/004_InstructGPT_And_RLHF.md`. Low inter-annotator agreement on a prompt category is, in effect, an early warning sign of exactly where that failure mode is most likely to bite later in the pipeline, discoverable at the data-collection stage rather than only after an RL run has already gone sideways.

What a team should actually do in response to a low-agreement category is itself a judgment call with a few live options, not a fixed procedure: revisit and sharpen the guidelines for that category on the theory that some of the disagreement is guideline ambiguity rather than genuine taste disagreement; route that category to a different labeling protocol entirely — for technical, medical, legal, or other specialist content, this often means routing to domain-expert labelers rather than generalist contractors, on the theory that a chemistry PhD and a generalist labeler will disagree with each other far less than two generalists will, because much of the "disagreement" in a generalist pool on technical content is actually one or both labelers lacking the expertise to judge correctness at all; or, having done what can reasonably be done, accepting that the category is inherently high-disagreement and treating the resulting reward model's behavior on it with real epistemic humility — not over-trusting a reward score on a prompt type where humans themselves cannot agree what "better" means, and considering whether that category is better served by a different alignment lever (explicit rules, refusal behavior, deferring to the user's own stated preference) than by optimizing against a reward model at all.

## 4. Verifiable-Reward Data for RLVR

### 4.1 What makes a task "verifiable"

A task is verifiable, in the precise sense that matters for RL, if there exists a cheap, automatic, close-to-ground-truth procedure for checking whether a given model output is correct — a procedure that requires no human judgment call and no learned reward model standing in for one. The two dominant, well-established instances of this are math problems with a single checkable final answer, verified by exact-match or symbolic equivalence against a known ground-truth value, and code problems verified by execution — run the generated code against a held-out suite of unit tests and check whether they pass. A more marginal but real third category is certain structured-output and constraint-satisfaction tasks, where a validator program can mechanically check compliance with a specification (does this generated JSON conform to a schema, does this generated proof step follow a formal system's rules) without needing a human in the loop at all.

The defining property, worth stating precisely because it is the whole basis for why this category is treated so differently from preference data: verification here is not a *learned approximation* of a judgment — it is the judgment itself, computed directly and (for these narrow task types) essentially exactly. This is the distinction `../../OpenSource/008_DeepSeek_R1.md` and `../../GPT/008_O1_O3_Reasoning_Models.md` both foreground as the defining contrast between RLVR and RLHF: an RLHF reward model is trained to *predict* what a human labeler would have preferred, and is therefore always an imperfect, learned stand-in with its own blind spots; a verifiable-reward function for "does this code pass these tests" or "does this final answer equal the known solution" has no such approximation layer sitting between the policy's output and the reward it receives.

### 4.2 Why this changes the RL risk profile

The central practical problem with RLHF-style RL, discussed at length in `../../GPT/004_InstructGPT_And_RLHF.md` (Section 6/8, on RM overoptimization and the KL-penalty mechanism that manages it), is that optimizing a policy against a learned reward model is optimizing against a *proxy*, and proxies can be gamed — a policy pushed hard enough against a fixed, imperfect reward model will eventually find outputs that score well under the reward model's specific errors and blind spots rather than outputs that are genuinely good by the standard the reward model was meant to approximate. This is why RLHF pipelines need a KL-divergence anchor to a reference policy: without it, nothing stops the policy from drifting arbitrarily far in whatever direction exploits the reward model's weaknesses, and the further it drifts, the less trustworthy the reward model's scores become as a proxy for real preference.

A verifiable reward removes this specific failure mode for the narrow property it checks, because there is no proxy gap to exploit on that property — a unit test either passes or it does not, a final numeric answer either matches the known solution or it does not, and no amount of policy drift changes what "passing" means the way policy drift changes what a learned reward model's score means. This is precisely why large-scale RL for reasoning became practical at the scale it has: `../../OpenSource/008_DeepSeek_R1.md` reports that DeepSeek-R1-Zero was trained via RL directly from a base model with **no SFT warm-start at all**, using nothing but a rule-based accuracy reward (correctness of the final answer, checked programmatically) plus a format reward (adherence to a required reasoning/answer structure) — no neural reward model and, notably, no process-level reward model either; the paper reports that process reward models it tried for this purpose were prone to reward hacking and did not outperform simple outcome-only verifiable rewards at this scale. `../../GPT/008_O1_O3_Reasoning_Models.md` describes OpenAI's o1/o3 series in essentially the same terms at the level of what OpenAI has disclosed: RL against automatically verifiable correctness in math, code, and similar checkable domains, contrasted explicitly against RLHF's learned-preference-model reward, as the mechanism that lets the RL stage be "pushed much harder and longer" than would be safe against a learned proxy — with the important caveat, made in both files, that reward hacking is not eliminated entirely even here, only changed in character (Section 4.3 below).

### 4.3 Where verifiable problems actually come from, and the quality bar they have to clear

Neither `../../OpenSource/008_DeepSeek_R1.md` nor `../../GPT/008_O1_O3_Reasoning_Models.md` discloses the exact composition of its verifiable-reward training set in full detail — both files are explicit that this is one of the less-disclosed parts of an otherwise fairly detailed methodology (R1's report does not break down exactly how many math versus code versus logic prompts fed the RL stage; OpenAI has disclosed essentially nothing about o1/o3's specific training-data composition beyond the qualitative claim that it draws on domains with checkable correctness). What can be said with confidence, both from general knowledge of what verifiable problem sources exist in the field and from the shape of what these reports do confirm, is the *kind* of source this data has to come from: curated competitive-mathematics and competitive-programming problem sets (which have long had unambiguous final answers or test-suite-checked solutions as a natural feature of being competition material in the first place), existing code repositories' own test suites (a codebase's unit/integration tests are, by construction, an executable correctness check for that codebase's functions), and synthetically generated problem variations — programmatically perturbing an existing verified problem's parameters to produce many new instances that share the same verification procedure, a cheap way to multiply the volume of verifiable training data without a proportional increase in human curation effort.

Whatever the source, a candidate verifiable problem has to clear a real quality bar before it is usable as RL training data, and the bar is more demanding than it might first appear:

- **An unambiguous, single correct final answer** — a math problem with multiple valid answer forms, an unstated assumption that changes the answer, or genuine mathematical ambiguity is not usable, because the verifier has no way to know which form or interpretation to check against, and will silently mis-grade otherwise-correct model outputs that happen to phrase the answer differently.
- **A final-answer format the automatic verifier can reliably parse out of a long, free-form reasoning trace.** A reasoning model may produce thousands of tokens of exploratory chain-of-thought before committing to an answer, and if the verifier cannot robustly extract *which part of that trace is the final answer* (typically enforced via a required format like a boxed answer or an explicit answer tag — recall R1-Zero's explicit format reward, which exists partly to make this extraction reliable), correct reasoning that is not formatted as the verifier expects gets scored as incorrect, and the training signal degrades into noise around a formatting convention rather than actual correctness.
- **Test suites that are themselves correct and reasonably complete**, in the code-verification case. A weak or buggy test suite is a direct route to a corrupted reward signal in either direction: an incomplete suite can reward incorrect code that happens to satisfy the tests it has (passing tests without solving the actual problem, a textbook instance of reward hacking against a weak verifier, which both the R1 and o1/o3 files flag as a real, persisting risk even in the verifiable-reward setting), while an incorrect or overly rigid reference test can penalize genuinely correct code that fails on an edge case where the *reference tests themselves* are wrong or make an unwarranted assumption. Verifier quality is therefore not a one-time setup cost — it is an ongoing engineering investment with the same character as labeler-guideline quality in Section 3.4: a flawed verifier propagates its flaws directly and mechanically into every rollout it grades, at RL training scale, with no human in the loop to catch the error the way a labeler might catch an ambiguous preference comparison.

### 4.4 The real limitation: verifiability is a narrow slice of the task space

The obvious and important limitation of everything in Section 4 is that verifiable-reward RL only directly applies to the bounded slice of tasks that admit a cheap, automatic correctness check at all. Most of what a general-purpose assistant is actually asked to do — open-ended writing, summarization, giving advice, holding a conversation, answering questions with no single correct phrasing — has no automatic checker, and no amount of investment in better verifiers changes that, because the underlying notion of "correct" for those tasks is not a mechanically checkable property; it is closer to the preference-based, human-judgment-dependent notion of quality that Section 3's comparison data is built to capture. RLVR therefore complements rather than replaces preference-based RLHF: both `../../OpenSource/008_DeepSeek_R1.md` and `../../GPT/008_O1_O3_Reasoning_Models.md` describe pipelines where a verifiable-reward RL stage for reasoning-heavy domains sits alongside — not instead of — a more conventional preference/RLHF-style stage for everything else (R1's fourth pipeline stage explicitly blends the reasoning verifiable-reward signal with human-preference-style helpfulness/harmlessness rewards for general-scenario alignment; OpenAI's system-card material for o1/o3 similarly indicates RLHF-style and deliberative-alignment safety training remain layered on top of the RLVR reasoning stage). A full post-training pipeline for a general-purpose model, as best can be told from what both labs have disclosed, uses both kinds of reward signal side by side, applied to different slices of the task distribution, rather than treating either as a universal substitute for the other.

There is also a more speculative, strategically important claim worth flagging explicitly as such: informal and academic discussion in the field (not a settled, rigorously quantified empirical finding, and this file is not claiming otherwise) suggests that reasoning behavior acquired via RLVR on math and code — extending chain-of-thought, checking intermediate steps, backtracking when a line of reasoning looks wrong — transfers at least partially to other reasoning-adjacent tasks that are *not* themselves directly verifiable, on the theory that "reasoning carefully and checking your own work" is a somewhat domain-general skill rather than a skill narrowly bound to the syntax of math and code problems. This is part of the strategic logic behind a lab investing heavily in verifiable-domain RL even when building a general-purpose assistant product whose actual usage is overwhelmingly non-verifiable open-ended queries: the bet is that RLVR is not merely "make the model better at math and code" in isolation, but a lever on general reasoning quality that happens to be trainable at scale specifically because math and code are the domains where a hard, unambiguous, automatically-checkable reward signal exists to train against. Whether — and how far — that transfer actually generalizes beyond reasoning-adjacent domains remains an open empirical question rather than a settled result, and should be presented as exactly that in an interview: a real, disclosed strategic bet, not a proven law.

## 5. What This Is Really Testing in a Staff Interview

An interviewer probing this area is very rarely trying to find out whether a candidate can re-derive the Bradley-Terry loss or the GRPO advantage estimator from memory — that is algorithm territory, well-covered elsewhere, and comparatively easy to look up. What is harder to fake, and therefore more diagnostic of someone who has actually thought carefully about how these models get built, is whether the candidate treats post-training data collection as a genuine operational and human-systems design problem with the same seriousness they'd bring to an ML architecture question: do they understand that a labeler-guideline document is functionally a specification for the model's personality, not a footnote; that prompt-distribution coverage has to be actively engineered rather than passively collected; that model-assisted labeling trades cost for a real and specific bias (anchoring) rather than being a strictly dominant improvement over from-scratch writing; that inter-annotator agreement is a load-bearing diagnostic for reward-model reliability and downstream RL stability, not an academic aside; and that they can state precisely — not just gesture at — why removing the learned-proxy layer in RLVR changes the RL risk profile, while still being honest that verifiability is a narrow, bounded slice of the real task distribution and that weak verifiers reintroduce a version of the same reward-hacking problem RLVR is often credited with solving outright. A candidate who can move fluently between "here is the concrete mechanical reason absolute rating scales are worse than pairwise comparisons" and "here is exactly what a buggy unit-test suite does to an RL run at scale" is demonstrating the thing staff-level post-training work actually requires: equal fluency in the data-and-process design that most public papers compress into a single sentence, and the algorithmic machinery that consumes whatever that process produces.
