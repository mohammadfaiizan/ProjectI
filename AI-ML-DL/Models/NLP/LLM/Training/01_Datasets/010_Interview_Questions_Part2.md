## Q1: In GPT-3's Table 2.2 mixture, Common Crawl supplies roughly 410B filtered tokens and gets ~60% of a ~300B-token training budget, while Wikipedia supplies roughly 3B tokens and also gets meaningful weight. Walk through, mechanically, what "60% weight" and "upweighted" actually mean in terms of how many times each source is actually seen during training.

The key distinction is between raw availability and training-time sampling weight, and the arithmetic makes the mismatch concrete. Common Crawl has 410B tokens available after filtering, and the training budget allocates it roughly 60% of 300B total tokens, which is 180B tokens of the training run. 180B drawn from a 410B-token pool means well under one full pass through even the filtered pool — Common Crawl, despite being by far the largest source, is undersampled relative to its own size, and most of the filtered pool is never touched in this specific run at all.

Wikipedia, Books1, Books2, and WebText2 combined supply only about 89B raw tokens, yet are collectively upweighted to roughly 40% of the 300B budget — call it 120B tokens drawn from an 89B-token pool. That's necessarily more than one full pass: on average, every document in that combined pool is seen more than once over the course of training, i.e., these smaller curated sources are repeated across multiple effective epochs while Common Crawl doesn't even complete a single epoch. The premise being encoded here is a value judgment: curated, high-quality tokens are worth more per unit than an equivalent count of filtered web tokens, and the mixture-weighting decision is a deliberate bet on repetition rates per source, not a passive union of everything available. This is exactly the general shape every subsequent disclosed mixture in the field follows, even where the specific ratios differ — sampling weight tracks a lab's belief about per-token value, not raw token count.

## Q2: You're asked to add meaningful coverage of a language with very little clean web text to an existing English-dominant pretraining mixture. Walk through the decisions you'd actually need to make, and where the hard trade-offs are.

The first thing I'd resist is treating this as "just add more of that language's data and reweight," because the actual constraint isn't token-allocation arithmetic — it's that high-quality text in that language may simply not exist at the volume or cleanliness a naive allocation would want. Raw web-text availability is itself wildly skewed toward English and a handful of other high-resource languages, so even before any deliberate weighting decision, an unweighted crawl-based corpus is already far from linguistically balanced. If I do nothing, I've made the choice to be English-dominant by omission, which is worth naming explicitly rather than treating as a neutral default.

Given that, I'd separate two genuinely different levers rather than conflating them. The first is pretraining-token allocation itself — how many training steps get sampled from that language's data, which is a temperature-style reweighting decision (Section 3 of the mixture file) trading off "respect the natural corpus distribution" against "give this language a meaningful voice regardless of how little of it exists." The second, analytically distinct, lever is tokenizer construction — whether the tokenizer's own fitting corpus gives that language's script and morphology enough merge budget to compress efficiently, which can be decoupled entirely from the pretraining mixture's language balance, since tokenizer fitting needs only a comparatively modest, frequency-stabilized sample rather than a full pass over the pretraining data.

The hard trade-off that doesn't go away no matter how I set the weights: equal token-count allocation is not the same as equal achieved-quality allocation, because high-quality web text is scarcer and noisier for the overwhelming majority of the world's languages than for English — a token's worth of a low-resource language and a token's worth of English are not interchangeable in expected quality, however evenly a mixture recipe tries to weight them. So I'd want to actually assess, before committing to an aggressive upsample, whether this is a case of genuine data scarcity (a narrow, low-diversity source pool that will hit a memorization ceiling quickly if pushed too hard) versus merely under-collected data (abundant material that simply wasn't crawled or curated yet, where the right fix is sourcing more raw data before upsampling further, not upsampling the existing thin pool). Pushing the temperature dial hard on a genuinely scarce, narrow pool is exactly the over-upsampling failure mode that produces memorization rather than generalization — repeated exposure to the same small set of documents dozens of times, with a real overfitting signature (domain-specific training loss dropping far faster than held-out loss on that domain) that I'd want to monitor for directly rather than assume away.

## Q3: Implement the Pareto/stochastic retention rule GPT-3's pipeline uses to turn a quality classifier's score into a keep/discard decision, and explain why this is often preferred over a hard threshold.

```python
import numpy as np

def pareto_keep_probability(score: float, alpha: float = 9.0) -> float:
    """
    Monotonic in score, not a hard cutoff: probability of keeping a document
    increases smoothly with its classifier score rather than jumping
    discontinuously at some fixed threshold. alpha controls how aggressively
    low scores are downweighted.
    """
    return 1.0 - (1.0 - score) ** alpha

def filter_corpus_stochastic(documents_with_scores, alpha=9.0, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    kept = []
    for doc, score in documents_with_scores:
        if rng.random() < pareto_keep_probability(score, alpha):
            kept.append(doc)
    return kept
```

A hard threshold — keep everything scoring above `t`, discard everything below — draws a sharp line through what is actually a continuous, noisy score distribution. Documents just below the cutoff are discarded with certainty even though the classifier's score near the decision boundary carries relatively little real information: a document scoring 0.49 is not meaningfully worse than one scoring 0.51, especially given that the underlying classifier here is a shallow linear model over hashed n-gram features, not a finely calibrated instrument. Real-world text quality is not bimodal — there's a continuum from clearly spam-like to clearly encyclopedic, and a large fraction of legitimately useful text (informal blogs, non-native-English writing that's coherent and informative, niche technical forums, minority-dialect content) sits in the middle of that continuum, exactly where a hard cutoff tuned to separate obviously-good from obviously-bad exemplars is most likely to systematically excise content it shouldn't.

The stochastic rule instead says: the higher your score, the more likely you survive, but nothing is guaranteed to survive purely by being above a line and nothing is guaranteed to be excluded purely by being below one. This is a genuine, if partial, mitigation for the over-filtering/representational-narrowing risk that every quality-classifier-based filter carries — the classifier itself is a proxy trained to recognize resemblance to curated reference text, and any proxy has a systematic bias baked into what it rewards, so preserving some tail probability of retention for lower-scoring documents keeps some of that structurally underrepresented content in the corpus rather than sharply amputating it. It's not a complete fix, since the underlying classifier's biases are still baked into the score driving the keep probability in the first place, but it's a meaningfully softer failure mode than an arbitrary threshold.

## Q4: What does line-level exact deduplication catch that document-level fuzzy (MinHash/LSH) deduplication does not, and why would a real pipeline run both rather than relying on the more sophisticated one alone?

These operate at different granularities and catch structurally different redundancy, so "more sophisticated" isn't actually the right axis to compare them on. Document-level fuzzy dedup flags a pair only if the aggregate similarity of two entire documents crosses a threshold. That's exactly right for catching mirror pages and syndicated articles republished with minor edits — cases where most of the document is shared. It does nothing for a legal disclaimer paragraph, a cookie-notice sentence, or a standard code-license header that appears verbatim, embedded inside thousands of otherwise-distinct documents, because each containing document as a whole is unique — the repeated fragment gets diluted by everything around it into a Jaccard similarity well below any reasonable near-duplicate threshold. The MinHash/LSH machinery, however well-tuned, structurally cannot see this, because it's answering "are these two whole documents similar," not "does some sub-document span recur across many documents."

Line-level (or paragraph-level) exact deduplication hashes each line independently and can strip that recurring disclaimer out of every document it appears in without discarding the surrounding unique content — this is exactly the mechanism CCNet-style pipelines apply at paragraph granularity, on the reasoning that the redundancy that matters most in a web-scale corpus is very often sub-document, not whole-document. The cost is a large multiplicative increase in hashing/lookup operations (roughly proportional to average lines-per-document) and a genuine precision risk in the opposite direction: some short lines legitimately recur across many distinct, useful documents for reasons that have nothing to do with redundant training signal — common section headers, standard license text, frequently-quoted lines — so a naive first-seen-wins line-level dedup risks stripping legitimately-repeated content. Production pipelines address this with a frequency threshold (only treat a line as boilerplate if it recurs above some minimum count across the corpus) rather than deduplicating indiscriminately on every single line.

So the two techniques are complementary along granularity, not competing solutions to the same problem — a real pipeline runs both because each is specifically blind to the failure mode the other one catches: document-level fuzzy dedup for whole-document near-copies, line/paragraph-level exact dedup for repeated sub-document fragments.

## Q5: A quality-filtering pipeline is tuned to be very aggressive — a strict classifier threshold plus tight perplexity bounds — and produces measurably lower training loss on held-out clean text. Is this an unambiguous win? What would you want to check before shipping it?

Lower held-out loss on a clean validation set is real evidence that the filtered corpus is, on average, closer to whatever register the validation set represents — but it's not evidence about the thing that actually matters, which is whether the filtering decision cost you representational diversity in ways that don't show up in that specific metric. Every filter in this pipeline is calibrated against a proxy for quality: the classifier's positive class is curated reference text (Wikipedia-linked pages, WebText-style content), and perplexity is scored under a reference LM trained on similarly clean text. Both proxies systematically reward text that resembles the register they were built from and systematically penalize text that's legitimately informative but stylistically distant from it — informal writing, non-native English that's coherent but not idiomatic, code-mixed multilingual text, minority-dialect speech, niche jargon a shallow classifier was never positively trained to recognize. Tuning the filter aggressively enough to move held-out loss on a clean validation set is, almost by construction, tuning it to look more like that validation set — which doesn't tell you anything about what got excluded along the way, because the validation metric is defined in terms of the exact same proxy the filter was optimized against.

Before shipping, I'd want to actually measure the thing the aggregate loss number can't show me: break down what got filtered out by some independent axis — language/dialect ID, estimated register or formality, source-domain diversity — and check whether the discard rate is disproportionate for specific populations or content types rather than roughly uniform. I'd also want to sanity-check downstream, not just upstream: evaluate the resulting model (even at small scale) on tasks or dialects that are known to be underrepresented in "textbook-like" corpora, not only on the clean benchmark suite the filter was implicitly optimized to resemble. And I'd want an explicit answer to the counterfactual-cost question — how much genuinely useful, non-redundant signal did the aggressive filter throw away, versus how much clearly-bad content did it correctly remove — since tolerating some noise for the sake of scale and diversity is a real, defensible design point, not automatically the losing side of this trade-off. A lower loss number in isolation is not sufficient evidence that the aggressive setting is the right one; it's evidence that needs to be weighed against a cost that the metric itself is structurally unable to show.

## Q6: Implement the exponentiated-gradient domain-weight update used in DoReMi-style automatic mixture optimization, and explain what makes this a minimax rather than a plain loss-minimization procedure.

```python
import math

def update_domain_weights(weights: dict, excess_losses: dict, eta: float) -> dict:
    """
    One step of exponentiated-gradient-ascent reweighting toward domains with
    the largest excess loss (proxy_loss - reference_loss) -- i.e. the domains
    the proxy model is currently doing relatively worst on, controlling for
    how hard each domain is in absolute terms.
    """
    unnormalized = {
        i: w * math.exp(eta * excess_losses[i])
        for i, w in weights.items()
    }
    total = sum(unnormalized.values())
    return {i: v / total for i, v in unnormalized.items()}
```

Each call multiplicatively boosts a domain's weight in proportion to `exp(eta * excess_loss_i)` and renormalizes: a domain with large positive excess loss — the proxy model doing relatively worse there than a reference model does — gets scaled up; a domain the proxy already matches or beats the reference on gets scaled down relative to the others. Run repeatedly, interleaved step by step with ordinary gradient descent on the proxy model's own parameters under whatever weights are currently proposed, this implements the game DoReMi is built around: the weighting player is trying to find domain weights that make the worst-case excess loss across domains as large as possible — pushing sampling attention toward whichever domain is currently most neglected — while the model-training player is simultaneously trying to minimize loss under whatever mixture the weighting player currently proposes.

What makes this a minimax game rather than plain average-loss minimization is precisely that a pure loss-minimization procedure over a mixture would happily sacrifice a small domain entirely if doing so improved loss on the bulk of the corpus — there's nothing in "minimize average loss" that prevents starving an underrepresented domain if the aggregate number still goes down. The minimax framing explicitly resists that, by construction: the weighting player's objective is to keep the worst-served domain from being neglected, which is a robustness property distinct from, and in real tension with, simply minimizing the mean.

I'd also flag the two caveats that matter for evaluating whether to trust this in practice, since they're exactly what a sharp follow-up question would probe. First, the method's output is only as trustworthy as the reference model it measures excess loss against — the reference model's own mixture and quality embed a value judgment about what "acceptable loss on this domain" means, so the method relocates human judgment rather than removing it, from "hand-pick the final mixture" to "hand-pick the reference mixture that defines excess loss." Second, the domain taxonomy itself — how the corpus is partitioned into the groups being reweighted — is a prior human decision the algorithm doesn't question; if two genuinely distinct sub-populations are lumped into one labeled domain, minimax reweighting can only move mass toward or away from that domain as a whole and cannot discover a more granular split the taxonomy never exposed to it.

## Q7: A junior engineer sets a domain's sampling weight q_i to 5% for an experiment and later reports "the domain only has 0.5% of total tokens, so we must have trained on 10x more of this domain's actual content than exists." What's wrong with that reasoning?

The error is conflating a per-step sampling probability with a statement about total token count that exists — they're genuinely different quantities, and this is, in my experience, the single most common way informal reasoning about mixture mechanics goes wrong. `q_i` controls the rate at which gradient signal is drawn from a domain over the course of training — at each step (or when assembling each micro-batch), the data loader decides how many examples to pull from domain i according to `q_i`, either by sampling a domain index from the categorical distribution or allocating a fixed fraction of batch slots to it. If `q_i` is set higher than the domain's raw availability implies under proportional sampling, the loader's finite pool of documents for that domain gets cycled through repeatedly — once exhausted, it wraps around and starts again from the beginning. That's the actual operational meaning of "upsampling equals more than one epoch on that domain": the domain's underlying content did not multiply, the model just saw the same, still-finite pool of documents multiple times.

So "we trained on 10x more of this domain's actual content than exists" is simply not what happened — no additional unique content was created. What happened is that a fixed, finite pool of unique documents was sampled from more frequently, meaning each document in that pool was, on average, seen roughly ten times more often than under proportional sampling, not that ten times more content existed to draw from. Whether this is a good idea or a problem depends entirely on how large that domain's raw pool actually is and how many effective epochs that implies: data-constrained scaling-law work suggests returns from repeating a fixed pool decay noticeably beyond something like four epochs, so if 5% sampling weight against 0.5% raw availability implies roughly ten effective epochs over that domain's finite pool, that's squarely in the range where I'd want to check the domain's held-out loss curve for the classic overfitting signature — loss on that domain dropping much faster than validation loss on it — rather than treating the higher weight as a straightforward capability boost.

## Q8: You're designing the final phase of a pretraining run and want to add an annealing/cooldown stage. What would you actually change, and why does this specific practice have stronger evidentiary support than a full easy-to-hard curriculum across the whole run?

The two things that typically change together in a documented annealing phase are the data mixture and the learning-rate schedule. On the data side, I'd shift the mixture toward a smaller, more heavily curated subset — upweighting carefully filtered, high-quality, or specifically target-capability-relevant data at the expense of the broader, noisier web-scale mixture used for the bulk of training — rather than continuing to sample from the full corpus at the same proportions used throughout the run. On the learning-rate side, I'd decay the rate toward a small value as the terminal segment of whatever schedule the run uses, rather than sustaining it at its plateau value into this final stage.

The rationale for combining these two changes is that they reinforce each other: a decaying learning rate makes the model's parameters more sensitive to whatever data is shown late in training, because later small updates are comparatively hard for even-later, smaller updates to overwrite — so late-training exposure has outsized influence on the final parameters relative to an equivalent amount of exposure earlier in the run, when a large learning rate meant any given update's influence would likely be substantially revised by subsequent updates anyway. Deliberately controlling what occupies that high-leverage, hard-to-undo window with a curated, high-quality mixture is a way of using that leverage intentionally rather than leaving it to whatever the random draw from the full mixed-quality corpus happens to serve up in the model's final steps.

The reason this has real, broadly documented adoption evidence across independently published pretraining recipes — unlike a full easy-to-hard ordering across the entire run — comes down to what's actually being changed. This phase deliberately breaks stationarity: the mixture itself changes as a function of training progress, not merely the order in which a fixed mixture's examples are drawn. That's structurally different from reordering examples within an otherwise-stationary mixture, where a clean argument from stochastic optimization theory suggests order shouldn't matter much (permuting the draw order from a fixed distribution doesn't change the marginal composition of what's been seen by any given point, and SGD is already built to be robust to per-step noise). The annealing phase isn't a counterexample to that argument — it's a different claim entirely, about deliberately time-varying the distribution being sampled from, which is exactly the one place the "stationary mixture implies order-invariance" reasoning doesn't apply, and exactly why it's the curriculum-adjacent practice that actually has broad, independently-replicated support behind it.

## Q9: Implement a function that measures a tokenizer's compression-ratio disparity across languages, and explain what you'd conclude if a mitigation you tried made the aggregate average better but the gap between the best- and worst-served languages unchanged.

```python
def measure_compression_by_language(parallel_corpus: dict, tokenizer) -> dict:
    """
    parallel_corpus: dict mapping language_code -> list of comparable-content
    strings (ideally a professionally-translated parallel corpus, so content
    is held constant across languages by construction).
    tokenizer: object exposing .encode(text) -> list of token ids.
    Returns dict mapping language_code -> average bytes-per-token.
    """
    results = {}
    for lang, texts in parallel_corpus.items():
        total_bytes = sum(len(t.encode("utf-8")) for t in texts)
        total_tokens = sum(len(tokenizer.encode(t)) for t in texts)
        results[lang] = total_bytes / max(total_tokens, 1)
    return results

def fragmentation_ratio(compression_by_lang: dict, reference_lang: str = "en") -> dict:
    """Higher ratio = more tokens needed per unit content relative to reference."""
    ref = compression_by_lang[reference_lang]
    return {lang: ref / bpt for lang, bpt in compression_by_lang.items()}
```

This is measurable entirely offline, with no model inference at all, which is exactly what makes it a rigorous claim rather than an impressionistic one — holding content constant via a parallel corpus and just counting bytes-per-token per language directly isolates the tokenizer's own contribution to fragmentation disparity from anything about model capability.

If a mitigation improved the aggregate average bytes-per-token across languages but the gap between the best- and worst-served languages stayed the same, I would not call that a real fix, and I'd want to know specifically why before reporting it as progress. The most likely explanation is that the mitigation added capacity or merges that mostly benefited already-well-represented languages or the specific already-large languages driving the average, rather than actually reallocating merge budget toward the tail. This is exactly the failure mode you'd expect from, say, simply increasing overall vocabulary size without also imposing a minimum per-language or per-script merge-budget floor — a bigger vocabulary gives more total slots, but pure frequency-driven selection over a still-imbalanced fitting corpus will still favor whichever languages are most represented in that corpus, even a somewhat rebalanced one, if the rebalancing wasn't aggressive enough or wasn't paired with an explicit floor. The right response isn't to report the aggregate improvement as the finding — it's to go back and check whether the fitting-corpus rebalancing was actually applied with enough weight on the tail languages, or whether an explicit minimum-allocation constraint is needed on top of frequency-driven selection, because an aggregate metric moving while the worst-case gap doesn't is a classic case of a summary statistic hiding exactly the disparity you set out to measure and fix in the first place.

## Q10: Llama 2 used a ~32K-token vocabulary; Llama 3 switched to a 128,256-token vocabulary built on OpenAI's cl100k_base lineage rather than extending its own prior SentencePiece-based tokenizer. Walk through the actual cost-benefit calculus a lab faces at this kind of generational boundary.

The case for retraining is that a tokenizer's merge table is fit to a specific corpus snapshot and vocabulary size, and both inputs typically look very different by the time a new generation is planned — the available pretraining corpus is usually larger, more multilingual, and more code-heavy than what the previous tokenizer was fit against, and the vocabulary size that the embedding-cost-versus-sequence-length trade-off favors has trended upward release over release as training-token budgets and serving volumes have both grown by orders of magnitude. Retraining captures both improvements directly: a merge table actually fit to the current data distribution, and a vocabulary sized for current scale rather than whatever scale the prior generation was designed around.

The case against is a real, often underestimated backward-compatibility cost, and it's broader than just "users have to re-encode their prompts." A tokenizer defines a fixed mapping from text to integer token IDs, and that mapping is a load-bearing interface for a surprising number of systems: any prompt cache keyed on token sequences becomes invalid; any fine-tuned adapter trained against the old embedding space isn't directly usable with a model whose embedding table has different dimensions and different ID-to-subword mappings; any tooling that reasons about specific token IDs (logit-bias-style API parameters, token-level log-probability analysis) breaks the moment the vocabulary changes, because "token 4237" means something different under the new mapping; and users' own cached embeddings, few-shot prompts, and client-side token-counting logic all silently become inaccurate. There's also a pure engineering cost distinct from external compatibility: the entire pretraining pipeline's tokenization step has to be rerun from scratch over however many trillion tokens are about to be trained on, though that's a one-time cost rather than a permanent liability the way the compatibility break is.

There's a genuine middle ground worth knowing about, which is exactly why the retrain-versus-preserve framing isn't strictly binary: production tokenizers reserve a block of special-token IDs beyond what's immediately used, specifically so a future post-training update — a new tool-call delimiter, a new chat-role marker — can be added using previously-reserved-but-unused IDs without touching a single existing merge or existing token's meaning, preserving full backward compatibility for everything previously defined. That only works for adding a handful of atomic special tokens, though, not for improving general-text compression or multilingual coverage, since those require new merges derived from actual corpus frequency statistics — the expensive, breaking operation retraining entails. So the realistic option set at a generational boundary is three-way — freeze the vocabulary, extend it cheaply via reserved slots for new control tokens, or retrain the whole merge table and accept the full break — and Meta's move to 128,256 tokens for Llama 3, explicitly justified by Meta in inference-cost and multilingual-coverage terms, is squarely the third option: a deliberate judgment that the sequence-length and coverage gains were worth the compatibility break at that specific boundary, alongside the other major changes shipped in the same release.

## Q11: What are reserved special tokens in a production tokenizer's vocabulary for, and why are they never learned via the ordinary BPE merge procedure?

A production vocabulary is not entirely consumed by learned byte-pair merges — labs carve out a block of special tokens outside the merge procedure entirely: beginning/end-of-sequence markers, padding, and, increasingly important for instruction-tuned and agentic models, an entire family of chat-formatting and tool-use control tokens — role markers distinguishing a system/user/assistant turn, delimiters marking the start and end of a tool call and its result, fill-in-the-middle markers for code models trained to complete inside a file rather than only append at the end.

These are inserted as atomic vocabulary entries by construction rather than being learned from corpus frequency statistics, and the reason is specifically about unambiguity and security, not just convenience. You want these tokens to be unsplittable single units that can never arise from merging ordinary text, because if they could, a malicious or careless input string could forge a token sequence that looks like a control token to the model — a prompt-injection-adjacent concern where user-controlled text accidentally or deliberately produces the exact byte sequence that decodes to, say, an "end of user turn, begin assistant turn" control token, effectively letting untrusted input inject fake structure into the conversation format the model was trained to trust. Keeping these tokens entirely outside the frequency-driven merge process — reserved IDs that never compete for merge slots and are never derivable by combining ordinary byte-level tokens through the learned merge table — closes that specific attack surface at the tokenization layer.

Labs also typically reserve considerably more special-token IDs than they have an immediate concrete use for at release time. Llama 3's 128,256-token vocabulary, for instance, allocates a meaningful block of reserved-but-initially-unused special tokens specifically so a later post-training update can add a new control token using an already-reserved ID, without changing the total vocabulary size and therefore without resizing the embedding and unembedding matrices or breaking every existing consumer's assumption about vocabulary size — a small, deliberate piece of forward-compatibility engineering that sits directly next to the broader retrain-versus-preserve trade-off, since it's exactly the mechanism that lets a lab avoid a full retrain for the narrow case of adding new atomic control tokens.

## Q12: A lab detects that 8% of a benchmark's test items have at least one 13-gram match in its pretraining corpus. Explain the two different things it could do about this, and why they are not interchangeable levels of the same fix.

The first option is to remove the flagged content from the training corpus before the expensive training run begins — either the whole contaminated document, or, in a more granular pipeline capable of span-level excision, just the specific overlapping passage within an otherwise long and useful document. This is the option that actually prevents the model's parameters from ever being influenced by the contaminated text during optimization, so the eventual benchmark score is a genuinely clean measurement with respect to that specific contamination. The cost is that it requires knowing the benchmark, and having its reference data on hand to build the n-gram index, before or well before the training run concludes — if a new benchmark becomes relevant only after training has already finished, which happens constantly since new benchmarks are published continuously, this route either means accepting the already-trained model was never decontaminated against it, or re-running some portion of an extremely expensive training pass purely for decontamination purposes on a single benchmark, which is frequently not economically realistic.

The second option is to leave the training corpus untouched and instead filter the benchmark itself after training: drop the flagged 8% of test items and report a score computed only on the remaining, verified-uncontaminated subset. This is much cheaper — no retraining required, applicable at any point after the fact, and it can be used for benchmarks that didn't exist or weren't on anyone's radar when the training corpus was originally assembled. GPT-3's paper describes doing exactly this as one of its disclosed mitigations.

The reason these aren't interchangeable is that they answer genuinely different questions, and it's important to be precise about which one any given decontamination claim is actually making. Filtering the eval set after the fact does nothing to undo whatever influence the contaminated examples already had on the trained model's parameters during optimization — if the model's weights were shaped, even slightly, by having seen a benchmark's questions and answers during pretraining, that influence persists inside the weights regardless of which benchmark examples you later choose to score it on. Post-hoc filtering gives you a partially cleaner measurement of the model as it already exists; it does not give you a measurement of a model that was never exposed to the contaminated data in the first place. A rigorous decontamination writeup needs to be explicit about which of these two claims it's making, rather than treating "we decontaminated" as one undifferentiated statement.

## Q13: Contamination is usually framed as a pretraining-corpus problem. Explain how the same failure mode reappears in an RLVR training pool for math and code, and why pretraining-corpus decontamination tooling doesn't catch it.

RLVR draws its training problems from pools of math competition questions, coding problems, and similar verifiable tasks — sourced from competition archives, public problem sets, and community-curated collections. These are broadly the same universe of sources that popular reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, or various competitive-programming suites are themselves built from, which means an RL training pool can overlap with a benchmark's actual test items not through any pretraining-corpus leak at all, but simply because both were drawn from the same public archive of competition problems. The measurement-validity concern is identical to pretraining contamination — a reported "reasoning capability" score is partly measuring direct exposure to that exact problem during the RL stage — but the leak path is entirely different, and it never passes through the pretraining corpus.

This is exactly why pretraining-side n-gram-and-Bloom-filter decontamination tooling doesn't catch it: that tooling is built to check a specific artifact — the pretraining corpus — against a specific reference set. If the contamination originates from a completely separate pool, curated independently for the RL stage and never merged into pretraining data at all, the pretraining decontamination pass has no visibility into it whatsoever; it's checking the wrong artifact. The same applies to supervised fine-tuning data: instruction-tuning sets are frequently built in part from responses to a broad sample of "typical" questions, and if that sampling process happens to reproduce a benchmark's actual items — again plausibly just because both draw on similar public source material, not through deliberate copying — SFT can contaminate a benchmark just as effectively as pretraining can.

The mechanism to actually catch this is the same one used for pretraining — index the protected benchmark's items, check candidate training examples against that index — just applied to a different, typically much smaller and differently-sourced pool at each stage. Any lab running a serious post-training pipeline needs, in principle, the same kind of benchmark-aware overlap check run separately against its RL problem pools and its SFT data as against its pretraining corpus, treated as its own standing pipeline stage rather than assumed to be covered by whatever decontamination happened upstream at the pretraining step.

## Q14: A PM asks you to "just use synthetic data" to solve a data-scarcity problem without specifying which kind. Explain why "synthetic data" is not one technique, and what you'd need to know before recommending an approach.

I'd push back on treating this as a single decision, because "synthetic data" spans at least three mechanistically different techniques that apply at different training stages, draw on different sources of the generator's capability, and carry different risk profiles — conflating them is exactly the mistake that leads to picking the wrong tool. Self-instruct-style bootstrapping generates (instruction, response) pairs for SFT by repackaging a strong generator's already-latent instruction-following-adjacent capability into a paired format — it's a data-format problem, and it can't inject any capability the generator doesn't already have; its main quality gate is heuristic dedup, diversity filtering, and format checks, and its main failure mode if under-gated is a narrow, repetitive instruction pool. Reasoning-trace distillation takes an already-solved, expensive RL exploration problem — a model that reliably produces verifiably-correct long reasoning traces — and converts it into a cheap supervised imitation-learning target for a different, typically smaller, student model; its quality gate is verifiable correctness via rejection sampling, and its failure mode if under-gated is silently inheriting the teacher's wrong answers, since nothing else in the pipeline would catch them. Textbook-style synthetic pretraining data generates entire pretraining documents designed to be more pedagogically dense than average web text, betting that information density per token can partially substitute for raw corpus scale; its quality gate is human-curated topics and prompts mixed with real data, and its failure mode if under-gated is stylistic homogeneity and benchmark-skewed capability gains that don't transfer proportionally to messier real deployment traffic.

Before recommending anything, I'd want to know which of these three problems is actually being solved: is this a shortage of paired instruction-response examples for SFT, a shortage of demonstrated reasoning behavior for a smaller model, or a shortage of high-quality pretraining tokens? Each answer points to a different technique with a different primary quality gate. I'd also want to know, regardless of which one it is, whether the target domain is verifiable — math and code admit a mechanical correctness check that lets you gate synthetic output cheaply and reliably; open-ended domains (general factual claims, subjective content, creative writing) have no such automatic check, which is precisely where the real risks in this space — model collapse from naive recursive self-training, and systematic-error amplification when a generator's specific mistakes get reinforced rather than corrected — actually bite hardest. "Just use synthetic data" without pinning down which of these three problems and which risk regime we're in isn't an actionable recommendation; it's a placeholder for one.

## Q15: Implement a rejection-sampling filter that only retains reasoning traces with verifiably correct final answers before they're used as SFT data for distillation, in the style of DeepSeek-R1's distillation pipeline, and explain why this specific quality gate is what makes distillation safe against model-collapse-style risk.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class ReasoningTrace:
    problem_id: str
    trace_text: str
    final_answer: str

def verify_math_answer(candidate_answer: str, ground_truth: str) -> bool:
    """Exact-match / normalized-equivalence check against a known ground truth.
    A real pipeline would use a computer-algebra library for symbolic
    equivalence rather than raw string equality, to catch cases like
    '1/2' vs '0.5' referring to the same value."""
    return candidate_answer.strip().lower() == ground_truth.strip().lower()

def verify_code_solution(code: str, test_suite, sandbox_run) -> bool:
    """Execute the candidate code against a held-out unit test suite in a
    sandboxed environment; verification is pass/fail on actual execution,
    not a learned judgment."""
    result = sandbox_run(code, test_suite)
    return result.all_tests_passed

def rejection_sample_traces(candidate_traces: list, ground_truths: dict,
                             verifier) -> list:
    """
    Keep only traces whose final answer is verified correct. Everything else
    -- including plausible-looking but wrong reasoning -- is discarded before
    it ever becomes SFT training data.
    """
    kept = []
    for trace in candidate_traces:
        truth = ground_truths.get(trace.problem_id)
        if truth is None:
            continue
        if verifier(trace.final_answer, truth):
            kept.append(trace)
    return kept
```

This gate is what separates a controlled, safe use of synthetic data from the naive recursive-self-training scenario the model-collapse literature actually warns about. Model collapse, in its clearest form, describes repeatedly training on unfiltered, unverified output from a previous generation, with no fresh real data and no correctness check gating what enters the next round — each generation's errors and mode-collapse-prone sampling behavior compound, disproportionately eroding rare or unusual outputs since those are exactly what an imperfect generator is most likely to underrepresent on any single sampling pass.

Rejection sampling on verifiable correctness breaks that compounding mechanism at its source: a wrong reasoning trace — however fluent and plausible-looking — is discarded before it ever becomes training data for anything downstream, because math and code admit a mechanical check (exact-match or symbolic equivalence for a final answer; actual execution against held-out tests for code) that requires no learned model and no human judgment call standing in the loop. This is precisely why R1's distillation traces are filtered this way before feeding the roughly 800K-example SFT pool used to train the smaller Qwen2.5- and Llama-3-based distilled checkpoints: incorrect trajectories are discarded before they can propagate error, and only verified-correct traces get to influence a downstream student model. The gate doesn't eliminate every risk — a buggy or incomplete test suite can still silently pass incorrect code, and an ambiguous problem with multiple valid answer forms can cause the verifier to mis-grade an actually-correct trace — but it's a categorically stronger safeguard than anything available in open-ended, non-verifiable domains, where a confidently wrong statement has no automatic check catching it before it becomes training signal for the next round.

## Q16: A team proposes training model N+1 purely on filtered outputs generated by model N, iterated across several generations, with no fresh human-authored data mixed in at any step, arguing this is cheaper than continuing to source real data. What would you tell them?

I'd flag this as close to the textbook setup the model-collapse literature is specifically warning about, and walk through the mechanism concretely rather than just citing the term. A generative model never samples exactly from the true underlying data distribution — it has its own errors, biases toward certain modes, and sampling/calibration behavior that tends to underrepresent rare or unusual outputs relative to their true frequency. If model N+1 trains on data drawn from model N's output distribution, and model N+2 later trains on data drawn from model N+1's distribution, the approximation errors don't average out across generations — they compound, and the tails of the distribution (rare words, unusual phrasings, minority viewpoints, infrequent-but-real phenomena) are exactly what erodes fastest, because those are what an imperfect generator is most likely to underrepresent on any single pass. A simple numeric intuition: if some rare pattern occurs in 0.1% of true text and an imperfect generator reproduces it at only 60% of its true relative frequency, after two recursive rounds with no correction the pattern's effective frequency is on the order of 0.1% × 0.6 × 0.6 ≈ 0.036% — roughly a third of its true rate, with no single generation needing to be dramatically bad for this to happen, just consistently, geometrically compounding.

The specific feature of the proposal that makes this risk acute rather than theoretical is exactly the two things the team is proposing to skip: no fresh real data at any step, and — as stated — no explicit quality or correctness filtering gating what model N's outputs actually enter model N+1's training set. That combination is the naive, uncurated, fully recursive scenario the literature demonstrates collapse in; it is specifically not what any of the real, publicly documented synthetic-data pipelines I'd point to as counter-examples actually do. Self-instruct pipelines apply heuristic quality and diversity filtering at every round and stay anchored to a real human-written seed set. Distillation pipelines like R1's gate on verified correctness via rejection sampling before any trace becomes training data. Phi-style pretraining pipelines mix in substantial real, filtered web/code data alongside synthetic text rather than replacing the corpus wholesale.

So my actual recommendation would be: don't do the fully recursive, unfiltered version at all, regardless of the cost savings — the savings are being purchased with exactly the risk that's best documented to bite in precisely this configuration. If there's a real cost problem to solve, the fix is to keep a substantial, non-shrinking proportion of real, independently-sourced data in the mix at every generation, and to gate whatever synthetic content is included behind a genuine quality or correctness check — with the achievable strength of that gate depending directly on whether the domain is verifiable — rather than removing real data and verification simultaneously to save on both fronts at once.

## Q17: Explain anchoring bias as it applies to model-generated-then-human-edited SFT data collection, and describe a concrete quality-control measure you'd put in place to detect it.

Anchoring bias is a well-documented finding in judgment-and-decision-making research: a person's estimate or evaluation is pulled toward an initial reference point they were given, even when that reference point is arbitrary or wrong, and even when they're explicitly instructed to evaluate critically. Applied to SFT data collection, a labeler shown a fluent, plausible-looking model-generated draft has a systematic tendency to under-correct it — to accept sentences and structural choices that are "good enough" rather than critically re-deriving what the ideal response would actually look like starting from nothing, because the draft itself sets an implicit reference point for "roughly how good this response should be" that's hard to consciously override even under explicit instructions to be rigorous.

The practical consequence is that edit-based SFT data can be systematically lighter-touch than the label "human-reviewed" suggests, and can silently propagate the base model's existing stylistic tics, mild factual sloppiness, or characteristic hedging patterns forward into a dataset nominally treated as human-approved ground truth. This isn't a hypothetical risk unique to one lab's pipeline — it's a structural property of any human-in-the-loop editing process, and it's exactly why serious SFT-data operations build in checks against it rather than treating model-assisted editing as a strictly-dominant improvement over from-scratch writing once it becomes available.

The concrete measure I'd put in place: take a random sample of prompts that went through the edit pipeline, and have a separate group of labelers write from-scratch responses to the same prompts with no model draft shown to them at all — a blind, independent re-derivation. Then compare the edited version against the from-scratch version, either via a third-party quality judgment or via a direct diff-style analysis of what changed versus what a from-scratch writer would have changed. If the edited responses are systematically closer to the original model draft than the from-scratch responses are to each other, that's a direct, measurable signature of anchoring — labelers converging toward the draft rather than toward some independent notion of an ideal response. I'd also track a simpler proxy continuously rather than only in periodic audits: how much labelers actually change per example (edit distance, or a coarser proportion-of-sentences-modified metric), since a labeling population whose edits shrink over time as they become more comfortable with a model's typical draft quality is a warning sign worth catching before it accumulates across a large fraction of the dataset, not after.

## Q18: You measure inter-annotator agreement on a category of preference comparisons and find it's low — close to chance-corrected zero — specifically for creative-writing prompts. What do you actually do?

First I'd make sure I'm measuring the right thing: raw agreement rate is inflated by how many comparisons in the sample are simply easy, so I'd want a chance-corrected statistic — Cohen's kappa for two annotators, Fleiss' kappa for more — which explicitly subtracts out the agreement expected from chance alone, before concluding the disagreement is real rather than an artifact of how agreement was computed.

Assuming the low chance-corrected agreement holds up, the most defensible reading usually isn't "one of the labelers is wrong" — it's that the underlying preference being measured is genuinely noisy, subjective, or actively contested for creative-writing quality specifically, which is exactly the kind of category (alongside humor, contested value judgments, matters of taste) where reasonable people legitimately disagree, and no amount of additional guideline-writing fully resolves that into a single ground truth. This matters mechanically, not just as a data-quality curiosity, because a reward model trained on a noisier comparison signal is itself a noisier, less reliable proxy for whatever preference it's meant to approximate — and a noisier reward model is more exploitable during RL, since the policy has more room to find outputs that score well under the reward model's own error and variance rather than under genuine human preference. Low agreement discovered at the data-collection stage is an early warning sign for exactly where that reward-hacking failure mode is most likely to bite later, and it's much cheaper to discover it here than after an RL run has already gone sideways against an unreliable reward signal.

Given that, I'd treat the response as a live judgment call rather than a fixed procedure, with a few genuinely different options depending on what I find when I dig in. I'd first check whether some of the disagreement is guideline ambiguity rather than genuine taste disagreement — sharpening the rubric specifically for this category — since that's the cheapest fix if it's actually the cause. If disagreement persists after that, I'd consider whether creative writing needs a different labeling protocol entirely, potentially routing it to labelers specifically selected or trained for literary judgment rather than generalist contractors, on the theory that some of the "disagreement" in a generalist pool might reflect uneven expertise rather than purely divergent taste. And if disagreement is genuinely inherent to the category even among qualified judges, I'd recommend treating the resulting reward model's behavior on creative-writing prompts with real epistemic humility — not over-trusting a reward score on a prompt type where humans themselves can't agree what "better" means, and considering whether this category is better served by a different alignment lever (explicit stylistic guidelines, deferring more directly to user-stated preference) than by optimizing hard against a reward model that's measuring something close to noise for this specific slice of the task distribution.

## Q19: Contrast a learned reward model (RLHF) against a verifiable reward function (RLVR) in terms of what "reward hacking" actually means in each setting, and explain whether RLVR eliminates the problem or just changes its character.

In RLHF, the reward signal is a learned approximation of human preference — a neural network trained on comparison data to predict what a human labeler would have preferred — and optimizing a policy against it is optimizing against a proxy that necessarily has blind spots and systematic errors relative to the true preference it's meant to approximate. Reward hacking here means the policy, pushed hard enough, discovers outputs that score well specifically under the reward model's particular errors rather than outputs that are genuinely good by the standard the reward model was meant to stand in for — and the further the policy drifts from its reference distribution in whatever direction exploits those errors, the less trustworthy the reward model's scores become, which is exactly why RLHF pipelines need a KL-divergence anchor to a reference policy as a structural safeguard rather than an optional nicety.

A verifiable reward removes this specific proxy-gap failure mode for the narrow property it actually checks: a unit test either passes or it doesn't, a final numeric answer either matches the known solution or it doesn't, and no amount of policy drift changes what "passing" means the way policy drift changes what a learned reward model's score means — there's no approximation layer sitting between the policy's output and the reward it receives on that specific, narrow property. This is why DeepSeek-R1-Zero could be trained via RL directly from a base model with no SFT warm-start at all, using nothing but a rule-based accuracy reward plus a format reward, and why both R1's and OpenAI's o1/o3-series reasoning-model training are described as being able to push RL "much harder and longer" against verifiable rewards than would be safe against a learned proxy.

But I would not say this eliminates reward hacking — it changes its character rather than removing it, and both the R1 and o1/o3 material are explicit about this. A weak or incomplete verifier reintroduces essentially the same failure mode through a different door: an incomplete test suite can reward incorrect code that happens to satisfy the tests it has without actually solving the problem — passing tests without solving the task is a textbook instance of gaming a weak verifier, just with a mechanical rather than a learned verifier being gamed. An overly rigid or actually-incorrect reference test can also penalize genuinely correct code that fails on an edge case where the reference test itself is wrong. And DeepSeek's own report notes that process reward models it tried alongside outcome-only verifiable rewards were prone to reward hacking and didn't outperform simple outcome-based verification at scale — evidence that introducing a learned component anywhere in the loop, even to try to get finer-grained credit assignment, reopens some version of the proxy-gap problem RLVR is otherwise credited with closing. So the honest characterization is: verifiability closes the specific gap between a learned proxy and true preference for the narrow, mechanically-checkable property being verified, but it shifts the locus of risk to verifier correctness and completeness itself, which is an ongoing engineering investment, not a one-time setup cost, and not a guarantee that the RL run can be pushed indefinitely hard with no gaming risk at all.

## Q20: Explain why deduplication and mixture-shuffling — both discussed elsewhere in this collection as data-quality techniques — are specifically the operations that destroy data-provenance information, and why that matters even to a lab that isn't worried about litigation.

Both operations are, by design, doing exactly the kind of transformation that erases "which original source did this come from" as a side effect of doing their intended job well. Deduplication identifies documents, or near-duplicate documents, across the entire corpus regardless of which original source contributed them, and the natural, storage-minimizing implementation keeps one representative copy and discards the rest. If that discard step doesn't separately record which other sources also contained the now-deleted duplicate, the information "this content also existed in source X" is permanently lost the moment the duplicate is dropped — not merely inconvenient to look up later, but actually gone, since the deletion isn't reversible without a persisted record. Mixture weighting and shuffling then interleave documents from every source into training shards in an order determined by the sampling recipe, not by source — which is exactly the point of shuffling, since training on data ordered by source would introduce unwanted correlation between training order and source identity. But the direct consequence is that a training shard, inspected on its own, carries no inherent signal about which original source contributed any given document unless that information was explicitly retained as metadata traveling with the document through every transformation. Once that metadata is dropped — often for entirely reasonable proximate reasons like reducing storage overhead or simplifying a data loader's interface — recovering "which source contributed this exact token span in this specific shard" after the fact ranges from difficult to flatly impossible, because merge and shuffle operations generally aren't invertible: many different pre-shuffle corpora could in principle produce the same post-shuffle shard, and without a persisted mapping there's no way to tell which one actually did.

This matters to a lab with zero interest in litigation for reasons that are just good research practice. A team that can slice its own corpus precisely by source, license category, collection vintage, and pipeline transformation history can run far more informative ablations about which sources actually drive which downstream capabilities, diagnose a quality or bias issue back to a specific contributing source rather than to the corpus in aggregate, and make evidence-based mixture-weighting decisions rather than guessing — the exact kind of question Section 4 of the mixture-weighting file is about, and provenance tracking is a direct enabler of doing that analysis rigorously rather than by intuition.

It also matters for a genuinely hard practical reason that has nothing to do with legal exposure specifically but becomes acute in exactly the scenario a legal or opt-out concern would raise: complying with a request to stop using a specific source is straightforward for future training runs — just exclude it going forward — but is a fundamentally different and much harder problem for a model that's already been trained on that source. Once a document has contributed to a set of trained weights through however many gradient updates it participated in, there's no known clean technical mechanism to selectively identify and remove that document's specific influence from the model — a trained model's weights aren't a lookup structure that maps cleanly back to individual training examples, and the influence of any one document is smeared, in a way that isn't straightforwardly reversible, across billions of shared parameters. Machine unlearning is an active research area aimed at approximating this, but it's an open research problem, not a mature production capability — which means the only fully reliable remediation available today is retraining without the source, an enormously expensive option a lab can't exercise casually per request. That asymmetry — cheap to prevent going forward, extremely expensive or practically infeasible to undo after the fact — is itself the strongest argument for building provenance tracking in proactively at ingestion time, entirely independent of how any legal question eventually resolves.
