# Tokenizer and Vocabulary Construction at Scale

## 1. First Principles: What Byte-Level BPE Actually Does

Every modern frontier LLM (GPT-family, Llama, Claude, DeepSeek, and essentially every other production system since roughly 2019) tokenizes text using some variant of byte-level Byte Pair Encoding. Before discussing what changes at scale, it is worth being precise about the mechanism itself, because the "at scale" story is entirely about how you compute the same object faster and over more data, not a different algorithm.

BPE starts from a base vocabulary of the 256 possible byte values. This is the critical design choice that distinguishes byte-level BPE from earlier word-level or character-level tokenizers: any input string, in any language, script, or encoding — including malformed Unicode, emoji, raw binary-looking sequences, or text mixing a dozen scripts in one line — first gets decomposed into its UTF-8 byte sequence, and every one of those 256 byte values is already a vocabulary entry from step zero. This means a byte-level tokenizer structurally cannot produce an out-of-vocabulary token. A character-level or word-level tokenizer has to reserve a special `<UNK>` token for anything it didn't see during vocabulary construction, which both loses information (the model literally cannot distinguish two different unknown inputs that both map to `<UNK>`) and creates a training/inference mismatch if the distribution of unknowns differs between the two. Byte-level BPE sidesteps this entirely: worst case, a novel or rare sequence just gets represented as a longer run of single-byte tokens, which is a compression inefficiency, not a representational failure.

Given that byte-level base vocabulary, the BPE training algorithm is an iterative frequency-driven merge procedure:

1. Represent every document in the training corpus as a sequence of byte-level symbols (initially, each symbol is exactly one byte).
2. Count the frequency of every adjacent symbol pair across the entire corpus under the current segmentation.
3. Find the single most frequent pair, and add a new vocabulary entry that is the concatenation of that pair (e.g., if `t` and `h` are the most frequent adjacent pair, `th` becomes a new symbol).
4. Replace every occurrence of that pair in the corpus with the new merged symbol.
5. Repeat steps 2-4 until the vocabulary reaches a predetermined target size (e.g., 32,000, 100,000, 128,256).

Each iteration adds exactly one new token to the vocabulary and one new entry to an ordered merge list. At inference/encoding time, a new piece of text is first split into bytes and then the learned merges are applied in the same order they were learned (this ordering matters — it is what makes BPE encoding deterministic and reproducible given a fixed merge list), producing the final token sequence. There is no learned model doing the segmentation at inference time; it is a fixed, greedy, rule-based procedure applied against a table that was fit once, offline, during tokenizer training.

### A Worked Toy Example

Take the corpus `"low low lower lowest"` (word boundaries typically get a special marker in real implementations, e.g. an end-of-word symbol or an explicit space-prefix convention, but ignore that here for clarity). Represented at the character level for readability (real byte-level BPE works on raw bytes, but bytes and ASCII characters coincide for this example):

Initial symbol sequence per word: `l o w`, `l o w`, `l o w e r`, `l o w e s t`.

Pair frequency count across the corpus: `(l,o)` appears 4 times, `(o,w)` appears 4 times, `(w,e)` appears 2 times, `(e,r)` appears 1 time, `(e,s)` appears 1 time, `(s,t)` appears 1 time. `(l,o)` and `(o,w)` are tied at 4; take `(l,o)` first (tie-breaking is implementation-defined, e.g. lexicographic or insertion order — this is a real, if minor, source of non-determinism across BPE implementations that should be flagged rather than glossed over).

Merge 1: `l,o -> lo`. Corpus becomes: `lo w`, `lo w`, `lo w e r`, `lo w e s t`.

Recount: `(lo,w)` now appears 4 times — the clear maximum.

Merge 2: `lo,w -> low`. Corpus becomes: `low`, `low`, `low e r`, `low e s t`.

Recount: `(low,e)` appears 2 times, `(e,r)` appears 1, `(e,s)` appears 1, `(s,t)` appears 1.

Merge 3: `low,e -> lowe`. Corpus becomes: `low`, `low`, `lowe r`, `lowe s t`.

This continues until the target vocabulary size is reached. Note the shape of what happened: the algorithm discovered, purely from frequency statistics with no linguistic supervision, that `low` is a useful atomic unit in this corpus, while `er` and `est` (the comparative/superlative suffixes) would emerge as merges too if the corpus contained more instances of the `-er`/`-est` pattern. This is the entire mechanism by which BPE ends up learning something that looks like morphology (common prefixes, suffixes, whole short words) without ever being told what morphology is — it is a direct consequence of frequent substrings getting merged first.

### A Correct, Pedagogical BPE Training Loop

The following is a real, runnable implementation of the core training loop described above — simplified in that it works over a list of pre-tokenized "words" (a common practical detail: BPE merges are almost always restricted to not cross a pre-tokenization boundary, e.g. GPT-2's regex-based pre-splitter that prevents merges across whitespace/punctuation boundaries, so that the algorithm doesn't spend merge budget re-deriving word segmentation from scratch) but otherwise faithful to the textbook algorithm.

```python
from collections import Counter

def get_pair_counts(corpus: dict[tuple[str, ...], int]) -> Counter:
    """corpus maps a tuple-of-symbols (a 'word') to its frequency count.
    Returns a Counter of adjacent-symbol-pair frequencies across the whole corpus."""
    pair_counts = Counter()
    for symbols, freq in corpus.items():
        for i in range(len(symbols) - 1):
            pair_counts[(symbols[i], symbols[i + 1])] += freq
    return pair_counts

def merge_pair(pair: tuple[str, str], corpus: dict[tuple[str, ...], int]) -> dict[tuple[str, ...], int]:
    """Replace every adjacent occurrence of `pair` with the merged symbol,
    across every word in the corpus, preserving frequency counts."""
    merged_symbol = pair[0] + pair[1]
    new_corpus = {}
    for symbols, freq in corpus.items():
        new_symbols = []
        i = 0
        while i < len(symbols):
            if i < len(symbols) - 1 and (symbols[i], symbols[i + 1]) == pair:
                new_symbols.append(merged_symbol)
                i += 2
            else:
                new_symbols.append(symbols[i])
                i += 1
        new_corpus[tuple(new_symbols)] = new_corpus.get(tuple(new_symbols), 0) + freq
    return new_corpus

def train_bpe(word_freqs: dict[str, int], vocab_size: int) -> tuple[set[str], list[tuple[str, str]]]:
    """word_freqs: raw word -> frequency (from a pre-tokenization pass over the corpus).
    Returns the final vocabulary and the ordered list of merges learned."""
    # Base vocabulary: every distinct byte/character seen, as single-symbol words.
    corpus = {tuple(word): freq for word, freq in word_freqs.items()}
    vocab = set(sym for word in corpus for sym in word)
    merges: list[tuple[str, str]] = []

    while len(vocab) < vocab_size:
        pair_counts = get_pair_counts(corpus)
        if not pair_counts:
            break  # no more adjacent pairs left to merge (corpus fully collapsed)
        best_pair = max(pair_counts, key=lambda p: pair_counts[p])
        corpus = merge_pair(best_pair, corpus)
        vocab.add(best_pair[0] + best_pair[1])
        merges.append(best_pair)

    return vocab, merges
```

This is correct and will actually run and produce the merges shown in the worked example above given a suitable `word_freqs` dict of single characters. It is also, deliberately, the version of BPE training that is completely infeasible at real pretraining-corpus scale, which is the pivot into the next section: `get_pair_counts` is a full `O(corpus size)` pass, and it is called once per merge, inside a `while` loop that runs `vocab_size` times (tens or hundreds of thousands of iterations for a real target vocabulary).

### Pre-Tokenization and the Two Dominant Implementation Lineages

One detail glossed over above deserves to be made explicit: production BPE implementations essentially never let merges cross an arbitrary boundary in the raw byte stream. Before the merge-counting procedure ever runs, the corpus is first split by a pre-tokenizer into chunks that merges are not permitted to cross — GPT-2's tokenizer, for instance, uses a fixed regular expression that splits on whitespace/letter/number/punctuation-category boundaries, so a training document is broken into candidate "words" (each still represented internally as a byte sequence) before pair-frequency counting ever begins. This matters for two reasons. First, it is a large practical speedup: instead of counting pairs across an undifferentiated multi-gigabyte byte stream, you count pairs within each pre-split chunk independently, which is exactly why the toy example and the `train_bpe` code sketch above operate on a dict of `word -> frequency` rather than one giant byte sequence — that per-word representation is the pre-tokenized form, not an implementation simplification. Second, it is a modeling choice with consequences: a regex that splits, say, on whitespace prevents the algorithm from ever learning a merge that spans a whitespace boundary, which is generally desirable (you don't want "e " and "the" competing to be considered a mergeable unit with the following word), but the exact regex is itself a design decision that shapes downstream tokenization behavior — GPT-2's regex, for example, was documented to handle contractions and certain punctuation clusters specifically, and different pre-tokenization regexes measurably produce different final vocabularies even off the identical BPE merge algorithm and identical training corpus.

Two implementation lineages dominate production usage today. One is Google's SentencePiece library, which implements both BPE and an alternative unigram-language-model tokenization algorithm, and which historically has been used with byte-fallback (falling back to raw byte tokens only for the specific bytes not covered by the trained vocabulary, rather than being byte-level from the ground up) — this is the lineage Llama 1 and Llama 2's roughly-32K-token tokenizer descends from. The other is OpenAI's `tiktoken`, a byte-level-from-the-start BPE implementation (matching the description in Section 1: base vocabulary is literally all 256 bytes, with no separate byte-fallback mechanism needed because every input is already byte-decomposed before any merge is applied) used across the GPT-3.5/GPT-4-era named encodings such as `cl100k_base`. As noted in `../../OpenSource/003_Llama3.md`, Llama 3's tokenizer is a notable cross-lineage move: rather than continuing to extend Llama 1/2's SentencePiece-derived vocabulary, Meta built Llama 3's 128,256-token tokenizer by extending `cl100k_base` itself, adopting OpenAI's byte-level `tiktoken` approach and vocabulary lineage as the starting point rather than iterating on its own prior tokenizer family — a detail worth knowing because it means "which lineage a tokenizer descends from" is not a fixed property of a lab, but a per-generation choice that can and does change.

## 2. What Changes When Training BPE at Trillion-Token Scale

### The Naive Algorithm Does Not Survive Contact With Real Corpus Sizes

The loop above has an asymptotic cost of roughly `O(vocab_size * corpus_size)`: every one of the (tens to hundreds of thousands of) merge steps re-scans the entire corpus to recompute pair frequencies from scratch. For a toy corpus of a few thousand words this is instant. For a tokenizer-training corpus of hundreds of billions of bytes (see "Tokenizer Training Corpus Is a Sample" below for why it's a sample rather than the full multi-trillion-token pretraining corpus, but even the sample is enormous), re-scanning the whole thing tens of thousands of times is not a "slow but tractable" operation — it is many orders of magnitude beyond any practical compute budget. If a single full pass over the sample corpus takes, say, several minutes at a realistic single-machine throughput, and you need on the order of 100,000 merges to reach a 128K vocabulary from a 256-byte base, the naive loop costs on the order of 100,000 full passes — weeks to months of wall-clock time for what should be a preprocessing step measured in hours.

Real implementations avoid this by never fully recomputing pair counts after a merge. Instead, they maintain the corpus's pair-frequency table incrementally:

- Build the full pair-frequency count once, at the start, over the whole corpus — this single `O(corpus size)` pass is unavoidable, but it happens only once, not once per merge.
- Maintain this frequency table in a priority queue (typically a max-heap keyed by count, often paired with a hash map from pair to its current count and to the set of corpus locations/word-occurrences containing that pair, so a given pair's entries can be found and updated directly rather than searched for).
- Pop the max-frequency pair from the heap, perform that merge, and then update only the counts of pairs that were adjacent to the merge site — i.e., only the pairs immediately touching the tokens that just got merged change count as a result of this specific merge; every pair elsewhere in the corpus that didn't involve the merged tokens is completely unaffected and does not need to be touched. This is the same trick that turns naive "recompute everything" graph/DP algorithms into local-update algorithms elsewhere in computer science: a merge is a local edit, so its effects are local, and only the local neighborhood of counts needs to be repaired.
- Because a heap can contain stale entries (a pair's true count may have changed since it was pushed, if it happened to overlap with an already-processed merge), practical implementations use lazy deletion / lazy update: when popping from the heap, verify the popped count against the current authoritative count in the hash map, and if it's stale, discard and pop again, rather than trying to eagerly keep the heap perfectly synchronized on every update (which would itself be expensive). This converts the total training cost from `O(vocab_size * corpus_size)` to something much closer to `O(corpus_size * log(corpus_size))` for the initial count plus a comparatively small additional cost per merge proportional to the number of pair occurrences actually touched by that merge, which is typically a small, roughly-constant-size neighborhood, not the whole corpus.

Concretely, this looks like the following (still a single-machine, pedagogical sketch — the distributed/sharded version discussed below layers on top of this same local-update idea):

```python
import heapq
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

def train_bpe_incremental(word_freqs: dict[str, int], vocab_size: int):
    """Same algorithm as train_bpe above, but the pair-frequency table is built
    once and then only locally repaired after each merge, instead of being
    fully recomputed by a fresh get_pair_counts() call every iteration."""
    corpus = {tuple(word): freq for word, freq in word_freqs.items()}
    vocab = set(sym for word in corpus for sym in word)

    pair_counts: Counter = Counter()
    pair_to_words: dict = defaultdict(set)          # pair -> words containing it
    for word, freq in corpus.items():
        for i in range(len(word) - 1):
            pair = (word[i], word[i + 1])
            pair_counts[pair] += freq
            pair_to_words[pair].add(word)

    heap = [(-count, pair) for pair, count in pair_counts.items()]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    merges = []

    while len(vocab) < vocab_size and heap:
        neg_count, pair = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if pair_counts.get(pair, 0) != -neg_count or pair_counts[pair] <= 0:
            continue  # stale heap entry (its true count changed since it was
                      # pushed) -- lazy deletion, just discard and keep popping

        merged_symbol = pair[0] + pair[1]
        vocab.add(merged_symbol)
        merges.append(pair)

        # Only touch words that actually contain the merged pair -- every word
        # elsewhere in the corpus, and every pair count not adjacent to this
        # merge, is left completely untouched.
        for word in list(pair_to_words.get(pair, ())):
            freq = corpus.pop(word, None)
            if freq is None:
                continue
            for i in range(len(word) - 1):
                p = (word[i], word[i + 1])
                pair_counts[p] -= freq
                pair_to_words[p].discard(word)

            new_word, i = [], 0
            while i < len(word):
                if i < len(word) - 1 and (word[i], word[i + 1]) == pair:
                    new_word.append(merged_symbol)
                    i += 2
                else:
                    new_word.append(word[i])
                    i += 1
            new_word = tuple(new_word)
            corpus[new_word] = corpus.get(new_word, 0) + freq

            for i in range(len(new_word) - 1):
                p = (new_word[i], new_word[i + 1])
                pair_counts[p] += freq
                pair_to_words[p].add(new_word)
                heapq.heappush(heap, (-pair_counts[p], p))  # push updated count

    return vocab, merges
```

This produces the identical merge sequence as the naive `train_bpe` on the toy `low`/`lower`/`lowest` corpus from Section 1 (verified: both yield `('l','o') -> ('lo','w') -> ('low','e') -> ('lowe','r') -> ('lowe','s') -> ('lowes','t')`), and the difference is purely computational: the inner `for word in list(pair_to_words.get(pair, ()))` loop only ever iterates over the words actually containing the just-merged pair, never over the whole corpus, which is exactly the "update only pairs adjacent to a just-performed merge" behavior described above. (Note that when several distinct pairs are exactly tied on frequency, the naive and incremental versions can choose different, equally valid, next merges depending on how each resolves the tie — one more concrete instance of the tie-breaking non-determinism flagged in Section 1.)

At genuinely enormous scale, this still isn't sufficient on a single machine, because even a single `O(corpus_size)` pass over hundreds of billions of bytes needs to be distributed. The natural framing here is a generalization of the classic MapReduce word-count pattern: shard the corpus across many workers, have each worker maintain local pair-frequency counts over its shard, and periodically aggregate (sum) those local counts across shards to get the global counts needed to decide the next merge. The distinctive complication relative to plain distributed word-count is that BPE's merge decisions are sequential and global — you cannot decide merge #50,000 without knowing merge #1 through #49,999 already happened, because each merge changes the symbol alphabet that subsequent counting operates over. So the practical distributed pattern looks like: (a) each shard computes and holds local pair counts under the current, globally-agreed symbol vocabulary; (b) counts are aggregated (map-reduced) to identify the single globally-most-frequent pair; (c) that merge decision is broadcast back to every shard; (d) each shard applies the merge locally to its own copy of the corpus and updates its local counts incrementally, using exactly the local-update trick described above, so that no shard re-scans its full local partition either; (e) repeat. This is a synchronization-heavy pipeline — every merge requires a global reduction step across shards before the next merge can be decided — and is a legitimate large-scale distributed-systems problem in its own right, distinct from (though related in spirit to) distributed training of the model itself. Exact production implementations of this (e.g., what specific sharding, aggregation frequency, or heap-update strategy any given lab uses) are generally not disclosed in technical reports, and should not be stated with false precision; what is confidently true is the shape of the problem — naive full re-counting is infeasible, and the standard solutions are incremental local updates plus MapReduce-style distributed aggregation.

### Tokenizer Training Corpus Is a Sample, Not the Full Pretraining Corpus

A second, easily-overlooked point: the corpus used to fit the tokenizer's merge table is, in essentially every real system, a large but strictly bounded sample of the eventual pretraining corpus, not a pass over the full multi-trillion-token dataset the model will actually be trained on. This is worth justifying rather than just asserting, because at first glance it looks like it should undercount rare-language or rare-domain pairs relative to fitting on everything.

The justification is a sample-complexity argument, and the key insight is that BPE merge selection and language-model training are answering fundamentally different statistical questions with fundamentally different sample-complexity requirements. BPE's merge decisions are driven purely by pair-frequency statistics, and natural-language (and code) token/subword frequency distributions are famously heavy-tailed (Zipfian): a comparatively small number of distinct pairs account for the overwhelming majority of pair occurrences, and that ranking stabilizes quickly as sample size grows, because you only need enough data to correctly identify which pairs are frequent, not to enumerate every pair that will ever occur. A few hundred billion characters of representative text is already enough to see "th", "ing", common short words, and common code idioms (`def `, `    `, `import`, `{\n`) recur often enough that their relative frequency ranking is essentially locked in; seeing ten times more data mostly adds more instances of pairs you'd already identified as frequent, not new information that would change the top of the merge list. Contrast this with what the language model itself needs from data: the model must learn syntax, semantics, factual/world knowledge, reasoning patterns, and long-tail associations, which is a vastly higher-sample-complexity task — a model plausibly benefits from seeing a rare fact, an unusual coding pattern, or a specific phrasing dozens or hundreds of times across trillions of tokens in a way that has no analogue in fitting a frequency-ranked merge table, where "have I seen this pair enough times to be confident it's common" saturates far earlier. This is why it is entirely standard, and not a shortcut that sacrifices quality, to fit the tokenizer on a sample (commonly on the order of many billions to a couple hundred billion bytes/tokens, though exact figures are generally not precisely disclosed by major labs and should be treated as approximate when discussed) while the actual pretraining run consumes an amount of data one to two orders of magnitude larger.

### Normalization Is a Corpus-Wide Decision With No Universally Correct Answer

A further complication that only becomes visible at real corpus scale, and that a from-scratch description of the algorithm easily elides, is Unicode normalization. The same visually-identical character can have multiple distinct byte-level encodings — an accented letter like "é" can appear either as the single precomposed Unicode codepoint U+00E9 or as the two-codepoint sequence "e" followed by the combining acute accent U+0301, both rendering identically but producing entirely different byte sequences and therefore, under byte-level BPE, potentially entirely different token sequences for what a human reader would consider the same text. Similarly, different whitespace characters (ordinary space, non-breaking space, various Unicode space variants) and different quotation-mark or dash variants proliferate across real-world web-scraped text. A tokenizer-training pipeline must decide, before counting a single pair, whether to apply a normalization pass (commonly Unicode Normalization Form KC, NFKC, which canonicalizes many of these variants to a single preferred form) to the corpus, and that decision is itself consequential at scale: skip normalization and the merge algorithm will waste vocabulary budget learning separate merges for what are semantically identical strings in different encodings, directly reducing the effective vocabulary available for genuinely distinct content; over-aggressively normalize and you risk collapsing distinctions some downstream use case actually cares about (certain full-width/half-width character variants matter in some East Asian text-processing contexts, for instance). There is no universally correct answer here, only a corpus-specific and application-specific trade-off, and it is a real engineering decision baked into the tokenizer-training pipeline well before the BPE merge loop itself ever runs — one more reason "just run BPE over the corpus" undersells how much preprocessing judgment goes into a production tokenizer.

### Multilingual and Code Content Reshapes Which Merges Get Learned

Because the entire merge-selection procedure is frequency-driven off whatever corpus (or corpus mixture) is used to fit the tokenizer, the composition of that corpus directly determines which subword units end up with dedicated single-token representations. A tokenizer fit predominantly on English web text will spend the bulk of its merge budget on English morphology and common English words/phrases. A tokenizer fit on a corpus with a meaningful fraction of source code will instead spend some of that same fixed merge budget on code-specific frequent pairs — common indentation patterns (runs of spaces, or the specific byte sequences produced by common editor tab-width conventions), keywords (`def`, `return`, `function`, `const`), and punctuation sequences that recur constantly in code but rarely in prose (`():`, `{}`, `=>`, `->`). A tokenizer fit on a genuinely multilingual mixture will allocate merge slots to frequent subwords in whichever non-English languages and scripts are well-represented in that mixture. Since the total number of merges is fixed by the target vocabulary size, this is a genuine zero-sum allocation decision: every merge slot spent on a Python-specific idiom or a Hindi-specific subword is a merge slot not spent on an additional English word, and vice versa. This isn't a minor implementation detail — it is the direct mechanism behind both the practical benefit of code/multilingual-aware tokenizer training (better compression on the content types the model will actually see a lot of) and the fairness problem discussed at length in Section 4 (worse compression on content types the tokenizer's training corpus underrepresented).

## 3. Vocabulary Size Trade-offs at Scale

### The Direct Costs of a Larger Vocabulary

Increasing the target vocabulary size `V` has two direct, mechanically clear costs. First, both the input embedding table and the output unembedding (LM head) projection are `V x d_model` matrices (or one such matrix if the two are weight-tied, as in the original GPT-2/GPT-3 lineage, though many modern large models untie them), so their parameter count — and the memory required to store them, along with their optimizer state during training — scales linearly in `V` for a fixed `d_model`. Second, and distinct from the parameter-count cost, the output unembedding carries a recurring per-token compute cost at both training and inference time: producing next-token probabilities requires a `d_model x V` matrix multiply followed by a softmax over `V` logits, for every single token, every single forward pass. At inference specifically, this sits directly on the critical path of every autoregressive decode step — you cannot emit the next token without computing this full softmax over the entire vocabulary first (absent approximations like hierarchical softmax or sampling shortcuts, which are not standard in production LLM serving), so a larger `V` means strictly more FLOPs on the single most latency-sensitive operation in the serving loop, once per generated token.

It's worth sizing this compute cost concretely rather than leaving it qualitative. The final projection's FLOPs per token are approximately `2 * d_model * V` (a standard matrix-multiply FLOPs count: one multiply and one add per output element, and the output has `V` elements each requiring a dot product over `d_model` dimensions). At `d_model = 4096` and `V = 128,256` (Llama-3-8B's dimensions), that's `2 * 4096 * 128,256 ≈ 1.05` GFLOPs per token just for the unembedding projection (the softmax itself is comparatively cheap — `O(V)` additions and exponentials, negligible next to the matrix multiply). A rough estimate of an 8B dense model's total forward-pass FLOPs per token is `2 * N_params ≈ 16` GFLOPs (the standard `2N` approximation for a forward pass through a dense transformer, treating essentially every parameter as participating in one multiply-add per token). Under that approximation, the unembedding projection alone accounts for roughly 6-7% of total per-token forward-pass compute at this scale — not dominant, but a non-trivial and, notably, fixed tax that scales with `V` regardless of how large the rest of the model is, which is exactly the flip side of the "embedding table is a shrinking fraction of total parameters as the model grows" observation later in this section: the parameter cost shrinks in relative terms as the transformer body grows, but the per-token compute cost of the final projection does not shrink in the same way, because it's paid identically on every token regardless of how many transformer layers sit in front of it.

### The Compensating Benefit: Fewer Tokens Per Unit of Real Text

Working against that direct cost is a compensating, and at scale usually dominant, benefit: a larger vocabulary means more distinct multi-byte and even multi-word units are available as single tokens, so the same underlying text compresses into fewer tokens. This is the mechanism, not just a correlation — with a 32K vocabulary dominated by relatively short subword units (because there simply isn't room in a 32K budget for very many whole-word or multi-word entries once you've covered a reasonable range of subword fragments across the languages and scripts the tokenizer needs to handle at all), average bytes-per-token tends to be noticeably lower than with a 128K+ vocabulary, where the larger merge budget allows whole common words, common multi-word phrases, and more complete coverage of frequent non-English subwords to each collapse into a single token. Concretely, if a 32K-vocabulary tokenizer averages roughly 3.5-4 bytes per token on English text and a 128K-vocabulary tokenizer fit on a comparable but more diverse corpus averages something closer to 4.5-5+ bytes per token (illustrative, not a claim about any specific lab's measured figures, which vary by evaluation corpus and are usually not published with this level of precision), then encoding the same document produces meaningfully fewer tokens under the larger vocabulary.

Fewer tokens per unit of real text is not a cosmetic win — it compounds through the cost structure of both training and inference in two distinct ways. During training, the attention mechanism's compute cost scales roughly quadratically in sequence length under standard (non-linear-attention) implementations, so if a larger vocabulary shortens the tokenized sequence length for the same underlying document by, say, 15-20%, the attention FLOPs for that document drop by considerably more than 15-20% (quadratically, a 20% length reduction is closer to a 36% compute reduction for the attention term specifically, before accounting for the FFN term which scales only linearly with sequence length). During inference, each additional token of generated output is one additional sequential autoregressive decode step — there is no way to parallelize across decode steps within a single generation — so fewer tokens per unit of generated content directly and linearly reduces the number of sequential steps (hence wall-clock latency) needed to produce a given amount of actual output text, independent of any attention-cost consideration. This dual mechanism — superlinear savings on the training/prefill side, linear savings on the sequential-decode-step side — is precisely the reasoning documented in `../../OpenSource/003_Llama3.md` for Llama 3's jump from Llama 2's 32K-token vocabulary to 128,256 tokens: the stated rationale is directly "fewer tokens needed to represent the same text reduces both the sequence length attention must process and the number of decode steps needed to generate a fixed amount of output," and this is presented in that file as one of three defining architectural changes of the Llama 3 generation, alongside universal GQA and the extreme-token-count "overtraining" strategy — both of which are, not coincidentally, also inference-cost-driven design choices for a model expected to be served at enormous volume.

### Quantifying the Trade-off With Real Numbers

It's worth actually running the arithmetic rather than treating "bigger embedding table" as a vague concern. Take `d_model = 4096` (Llama-3-8B's dimension) and compare a 32K vocabulary to a 128K vocabulary. The embedding table alone goes from `32,000 * 4096 ≈ 131M` parameters to `128,256 * 4096 ≈ 525M` parameters — roughly a 4x increase in that single matrix, consistent with the roughly 4x vocabulary increase, and if the unembedding is untied from the embedding (adding a second matrix of the same shape), the total embedding+unembedding parameter cost goes from roughly 262M to roughly 1.05B. Against an 8B-parameter total model, that 1.05B is a bit over 13% of total parameters — noticeable, but the model's non-embedding transformer blocks (32 layers' worth of attention and SwiGLU FFN weights at `d_model=4096`) still account for the large majority of the parameter budget. Scale the same comparison up to a 70B or 405B model at larger `d_model` (8192 and 16,384 respectively for Llama 3.1) and the embedding-table cost becomes almost negligible in relative terms — the *same* 128,256 x 16,384 unembedding matrix is about 2.1B parameters, against a 405B-parameter total, or roughly 0.5%. This is the general shape of the trade-off: the embedding/unembedding cost is a fixed absolute cost (it only depends on `V` and `d_model`, not on how many transformer layers or how much data you train on), while the transformer body's parameter count and the total compute spent processing tokens both grow with model depth/width and with total training and serving volume — so as models and serving volumes get larger, a fixed absolute embedding-table cost becomes a shrinking relative cost, while the sequence-length savings from the same larger vocabulary keep paying out on every single token of every future training run and every inference request, without bound. This is precisely why the field's chosen vocabulary sizes have grown steadily larger release over release (GPT-2/3-era ~50K, Llama-2-era 32K, Llama-3/GPT-4-era 100K-128K+) even though the embedding-table argument against a larger vocabulary was, in isolation, exactly as true in 2018-2019 as it is today. What changed is not the arithmetic of the trade-off itself but the scale of the volumes on the other side of it: at GPT-1/BERT-era training-token budgets and negligible public serving volume, a larger vocabulary's fixed cost genuinely wasn't clearly worth paying, because there wasn't enough downstream token-processing volume for the sequence-length savings to compound over. At today's multi-trillion-token training runs and massive-scale API serving, the compounding benefit dominates by a wide margin, and the calculus has flipped.

The one place this reverses is at the small end of the model-size spectrum, and it's worth being explicit about when the "vocabulary cost is negligible" argument stops holding. For a genuinely small or edge-deployed model — something in the few-hundred-million-parameter range, the kind of model that might run on-device — a 128K-vocabulary embedding+unembedding pair at even a modest `d_model` (say 1024-2048) can represent a substantial double-digit percentage of total parameters, because there's no longer a large transformer body to dilute it against. In that regime, vocabulary size is a first-order design lever on model size and memory footprint, not a rounding error, and it is entirely rational for small/edge models to deliberately choose a smaller vocabulary than their large-model siblings even when trained on a similar data mixture, accepting worse tokenization efficiency in exchange for a meaningfully smaller and cheaper-to-run model. This is a genuine counter-consideration, not just a footnote — it's the reason you see some model families ship a shared, large vocabulary across all sizes (accepting the small-model cost) while others deliberately vary vocabulary size by target deployment scale.

### Reserved Slots: Vocabulary Budget Is Not All Spent on Merges

A detail that matters in practice and is easy to omit from a purely algorithmic description of BPE: the final vocabulary size a lab targets is not entirely consumed by learned byte-pair merges. Production tokenizers reserve a block of special tokens outside the merge procedure entirely — beginning-of-sequence and end-of-sequence markers, padding tokens, and, increasingly important for instruction-tuned and agentic models, a whole family of chat-formatting and tool-use control tokens (role markers like "this segment is a system/user/assistant turn," delimiters marking the start and end of a tool call and its result, fill-in-the-middle markers for code models trained to complete in the interior of a file rather than only append at the end). These are inserted as atomic vocabulary entries by construction — they are never produced by the merge algorithm, precisely because you want them to be unambiguous, unsplittable single tokens that can never accidentally arise from merging ordinary text (which would let a malicious or careless input string forge a token sequence that looks like a control token to the model, a genuine prompt-injection-adjacent concern). Labs typically reserve considerably more special-token IDs than they have an immediate concrete use for at release time — Llama 3's 128,256-token vocabulary, for instance, allocates a meaningful block of reserved-but-initially-unused special tokens specifically so that a future post-training update (a new tool-calling format, a new chat-template control token) can be added without changing the vocabulary size and therefore without resizing the embedding and unembedding matrices or breaking every existing consumer's assumption about vocabulary size — a small piece of forward-compatibility engineering that sits directly adjacent to the retraining-versus-compatibility discussion in Section 5.

## 4. The Multilingual Tokenizer Fairness Problem

### Why This Is a Measurable Inequity, Not a Vague Concern

Section 2's discussion of multilingual and code content established the mechanism: a BPE merge table is fit to the frequency statistics of whatever corpus trained it, and merge budget allocation across languages/scripts/domains is a zero-sum competition for a fixed number of merge slots. The direct, mechanical consequence is that languages and scripts underrepresented in the tokenizer's training corpus end up with fewer merges specific to their orthography and morphology — fewer common words, fewer common multi-character sequences, and in the worst case (a script the tokenizer training corpus contained very little of) individual characters that require multiple bytes in UTF-8 encoding get segmented at a finer granularity, close to the byte level, because there simply wasn't enough frequency signal in the fitting corpus for the algorithm to learn coarser merges for that script.

The consequence is directly measurable, not speculative: encode a semantically equivalent sentence in a well-represented language (English, in nearly every widely-deployed tokenizer, given the composition of typical training corpora) versus an underrepresented language, and count tokens. It is a commonly observed and reproducible result across publicly available tokenizers that a sentence conveying equivalent semantic content in a low-resource language can require on the order of 2-4x more tokens than its English equivalent — and this ratio is purely an artifact of the tokenizer's training-data composition, entirely independent of the underlying language model's actual fluency or capability in that language. A model could be excellent at reasoning and generation in a given language and still be penalized purely at the tokenization layer if that language's script or morphology was underrepresented when the tokenizer's merge table was fit. This decoupling — tokenization efficiency as a property of the tokenizer's training corpus, separate from and prior to anything about the model's learned capability — is exactly what makes this a distinct, independently-diagnosable problem rather than just a restatement of "the model is worse at low-resource languages."

### Measuring It: Compression Ratio as a Per-Language Benchmark

Because the disparity is a direct property of the tokenizer rather than of the model, it is measurable entirely offline, without running any model inference at all — which is precisely what makes it a rigorous, reproducible claim rather than an impressionistic one. The standard measurement is a compression-ratio benchmark: take a fixed, parallel or comparable-content corpus across many languages (a common choice is a professionally-translated parallel corpus, so that content is held constant across languages by construction), tokenize each language's text with the tokenizer under evaluation, and report bytes-per-token or characters-per-token, or equivalently tokens-per-fixed-unit-of-content, broken out by language. This produces a concrete, ranked table — languages the tokenizer's fitting corpus represented well cluster at high bytes-per-token (efficient, few tokens for a given amount of content), languages it underrepresented cluster at low bytes-per-token (inefficient, many tokens for the same content) — and it is exactly this kind of benchmark, run against publicly available tokenizers, that produces the commonly cited 2-4x fragmentation-ratio figures referenced above. The benchmark is also the natural tool for validating whether a mitigation lever from the next subsection actually worked: rerun the same parallel-corpus compression-ratio measurement after rebalancing the tokenizer-fitting corpus, and a genuine improvement should show up as a reduced gap between the best- and worst-served languages in the table, not merely as an aggregate average improving while the tail stays flat.

### The Direct Downstream Consequences

This fragmentation disparity is not merely an efficiency curiosity; it produces concrete, quantifiable harms that follow mechanically from more tokens being required for equivalent content:

First, context-window consumption: every deployed model has a fixed maximum context length measured in tokens. A user writing or reading in a language that fragments 3x more finely than English is, for the same amount of actual content, consuming roughly 3x more of that fixed token budget — their effective usable context window, measured in "amount of real content that fits," is proportionally smaller than an English-speaking user's, even though both users are nominally given the same context-length limit.

Second, cost: essentially every commercial LLM API prices usage per token (input and output tokens, often at different rates). If a unit of content in an underrepresented language costs 2-4x more tokens to encode than the equivalent English content, a user working in that language pays 2-4x more money for equivalent value received, purely as a downstream consequence of tokenizer-training-corpus composition, with no relationship to the actual compute cost of serving that user's request being proportionally higher in some justified way (the per-token compute cost is roughly the same regardless of what language the token represents — the disparity is entirely in how many tokens a fixed amount of content requires, not in the cost of processing each individual token). This is a direct, quantifiable cost-fairness gap, and it is fully derivable from tokenizer statistics alone without needing to inspect model behavior at all.

Third, latency: autoregressive decoding is sequential, one forward pass per output token. If generating an equivalent amount of content in an underrepresented language requires more output tokens, it requires more sequential decode steps, and therefore takes measurably longer wall-clock time to produce, for the user on the disadvantaged side of the tokenizer's fitting-corpus imbalance.

All three of these compound the same underlying tokenizer-level statistic into three independent, user-facing harms — reduced effective context, higher monetary cost, and higher latency — for reasons that have nothing to do with model capability and everything to do with a preprocessing decision made long before any model weights are trained.

### Mitigation Levers

A lab has several concrete levers available to reduce this disparity, and it is useful to be specific about them rather than gesturing at "training on more diverse data" as a single undifferentiated fix. The most direct lever is decoupling the tokenizer-fitting corpus's language mixture from the pretraining corpus's language mixture: because Section 2 established that fitting a tokenizer requires only a comparatively modest, frequency-stabilized sample rather than a full pass over the pretraining data, a lab can deliberately construct a more linguistically-balanced corpus specifically for tokenizer fitting — upweighting underrepresented languages and scripts well beyond their natural share of the web-text distribution — without having to make the same trade-off in the actual pretraining corpus (where language-mixture choices are governed by a different set of considerations, namely how much capability the model should actually acquire in each language, which is a separate design question from how efficiently that language gets tokenized). This means a lab can meaningfully reduce fragmentation disparity purely as a tokenizer-construction-time decision, without changing anything about how much English-dominant or otherwise-skewed data the model itself is pretrained on.

A second lever is imposing an explicit minimum merge-budget allocation per language or script — rather than letting pure frequency ranking across a mixed corpus determine every merge (which will always favor whichever languages happen to be most represented, even in a deliberately rebalanced fitting corpus, if the rebalancing is imperfect), a tokenizer-training procedure can reserve a guaranteed minimum number of merge slots for specified scripts or languages, functioning as an explicit fairness constraint layered on top of the frequency-driven algorithm rather than trusting frequency statistics alone to produce an equitable outcome.

A third lever, discussed already in Section 3 from a pure efficiency angle but equally relevant here, is simply choosing a larger overall vocabulary size, because merge-slot competition is inherently zero-sum only up to the size of the vocabulary — a larger total budget gives high-resource and low-resource languages more room to each receive adequate coverage without competing as directly for the same limited slots. This is part of why the jump to 100K+-token vocabularies (Llama 3's 128,256, described in `../../OpenSource/003_Llama3.md` as including roughly 28,000 tokens specifically targeted at improving non-English-language compression beyond the base `cl100k_base`-derived vocabulary) is framed by labs partly in multilingual-coverage terms and not purely in English-compression terms — a bigger vocabulary is one of the few levers that improves both problems simultaneously rather than trading one off against the other.

## 5. Retraining vs. Preserving a Tokenizer Across Model Generations

### The Case for Retraining

A new model generation is a natural moment to reconsider the tokenizer, because the tokenizer's merge table is fit to a specific corpus snapshot and vocabulary size, and both of those inputs typically look very different by the time a new generation is being planned — the available pretraining corpus is usually larger, more multilingual, and more code-heavy than what was available (or than what was actually used to fit the tokenizer) for the previous generation, and the target vocabulary size that Section 3's trade-off arithmetic favors has, empirically, been trending upward release over release. Retraining the tokenizer lets a lab capture both of those improvements directly: a better-fit merge table for the current data distribution, and a vocabulary sized appropriately for current training-token and serving-volume scales rather than whatever scale the previous generation was designed around.

### The Case for Preserving the Old Tokenizer

Against that sits a real, and often underestimated, backward-compatibility cost. A tokenizer defines a fixed mapping from text to a sequence of integer token IDs, and that mapping is a load-bearing interface, not an internal implementation detail, for a surprising number of downstream systems: any prompt cache keyed on token sequences becomes invalid; any fine-tuned adapter (e.g., a LoRA) trained against the old token embedding space is not directly usable with a model whose embedding table has different dimensions and different ID-to-subword mappings entirely; any downstream tooling or evaluation harness that reasons about token counts, token-level log-probabilities, or specific token IDs (for example, logit-bias-style API parameters that let a caller boost or suppress specific token IDs) breaks the moment the underlying tokenizer changes, because "token 4237" simply means something different under a new vocabulary; and users' own few-shot prompts, embeddings computed against the old space, and any client-side token-counting logic (used, for instance, to estimate API cost or stay under a context limit) all silently become inaccurate or wrong. Beyond the external-facing cost, retraining a tokenizer also means the entire pretraining data pipeline's tokenization step must be rerun from scratch over however many trillion tokens are about to be trained on — not a small operation at the scale discussed in Section 2, though this is a one-time engineering cost rather than a permanent compatibility liability.

### Llama's Documented Case Study

The Llama lineage is a clean, publicly-documented example of a lab explicitly accepting the backward-incompatibility cost at a generational boundary rather than incrementally patching an existing vocabulary. Llama 1 and Llama 2 share a roughly 32,000-token SentencePiece-derived vocabulary lineage. Llama 3 makes a clean break: as documented in `../../OpenSource/003_Llama3.md`, it adopts an entirely new 128,256-token vocabulary built by extending OpenAI's `tiktoken`-style `cl100k_base` byte-level BPE vocabulary, with roughly 28,000 additional tokens specifically added for non-English-language compression. That file frames the change explicitly in inference-cost terms — a larger vocabulary reduces tokens-per-character, which reduces both attention sequence length and decode-step count for a fixed amount of content — with multilingual coverage as an explicit secondary justification. Llama 3 is not offered as a drop-in tokenizer-compatible upgrade to Llama 2; anyone with Llama-2-token-ID-dependent tooling, cached Llama-2 prompts, or Llama-2-space fine-tunes must treat Llama 3 as requiring a fresh integration, not an in-place swap. Meta evidently judged, at that specific generational boundary — alongside the other major changes documented in the same release (universal GQA, the deliberately-past-Chinchilla-optimal token count) — that the sequence-length and multilingual-coverage gains were worth the compatibility break, rather than trying to preserve the 32K vocabulary indefinitely across generations.

It's worth noting there is a genuine middle ground between "retrain the whole merge table from scratch" and "freeze the vocabulary forever," which is exactly what the reserved-token block described in Section 3 exists to enable: appending a small number of new special tokens (a new tool-call delimiter, a new chat-role marker) to an existing vocabulary, using previously-reserved-but-unused token IDs, without touching a single existing merge or existing token ID's meaning. This preserves full backward compatibility for every previously-defined token while still allowing incremental extension — but it only works for adding a handful of atomic special tokens, not for meaningfully improving general-text compression or multilingual coverage, since those require new merges derived from corpus frequency statistics, which is exactly the expensive, breaking operation retraining entails. So the realistic option set a lab faces at each generational boundary is three-way, not binary: freeze the vocabulary entirely, extend it cheaply and compatibly via reserved special-token slots for a narrow set of new control tokens, or retrain the whole merge table and accept the full compatibility break for a genuine compression/coverage improvement — and Llama 3's jump to 128,256 tokens is squarely the third option, not the second.

Contrast this with OpenAI's public tokenizer lineage, which by outside appearance has evolved more incrementally: the GPT-2/GPT-3-era tokenizer used a vocabulary in the neighborhood of 50,257 tokens, and later `tiktoken` vocabularies used for GPT-4-era models are substantially larger. Beyond that qualitative direction, exact vocabulary sizes, version history, and the specific rationale/timeline for each intermediate OpenAI tokenizer generation are not something that should be stated with high confidence here — OpenAI has not published a detailed tokenizer-design document comparable to Meta's Llama 3 paper, and precise figures beyond what is directly observable from released tokenizer artifacts (such as the publicly inspectable `cl100k_base` and later encodings distributed via `tiktoken`) would be invented precision rather than confirmed fact. The honest position is: OpenAI's tokenizer vocabulary has clearly grown across generations, and appears to have done so via a sequence of somewhat more incremental encoding updates (multiple named `tiktoken` encodings exist across the GPT-3.5/GPT-4 era) rather than a single dramatic 4x jump at one generational boundary the way Llama 3 executed — but the exact sizes, dates, and internal reasoning behind each OpenAI tokenizer transition are not public knowledge that should be asserted with specific numbers.

### The Staff-Level Framing

At bottom, this is a versioning and compatibility-debt decision with exactly the same shape as any breaking change to a public API or a serialized data format: you are choosing between accumulating technical debt (an aging vocabulary increasingly mismatched to your actual data distribution and target scale) and paying a one-time, but real and externally-visible, migration cost. What makes the tokenizer case somewhat harder than a typical API-versioning decision is that the set of "clients" depending on stability extends well beyond external API consumers with prompts and integrations — it includes the lab's own internal cached artifacts (prompt caches, precomputed embeddings), its own eval harnesses and regression-test suites (which may assert against specific token counts or token-level behavior), and any fine-tuned or adapter-based derivative models built by the lab or its ecosystem against the old vocabulary. A lab deciding whether to retrain the tokenizer for the next generation is, in effect, running the same cost-benefit analysis any engineering organization runs before a breaking schema change, just with an unusually broad and partly-external blast radius, and the Llama 1/2 to Llama 3 transition is a rare case where a major lab both made that call publicly and documented the reasoning behind it.

## 6. What This Topic Is Really Probing in a Staff Interview

A staff interviewer raising tokenizer and vocabulary construction is very rarely testing whether you can recite the BPE algorithm — that part is table stakes. What they are actually listening for is, first, whether you can reason quantitatively about the embedding-cost-versus-sequence-length trade-off with real numbers rather than a qualitative "bigger vocabulary helps compression" hand-wave: can you compute that a 32K-to-128K vocabulary jump is roughly a 4x increase in one matrix's row count, translate that into an actual parameter-count delta at a given `d_model`, and then correctly argue why that delta is a shrinking fraction of total cost as model size and serving/training volume grow, while the sequence-length benefit compounds across every token processed. Second, and often the sharper differentiator, is whether you treat the multilingual tokenizer fairness problem as a concrete, mechanistic, and independently measurable phenomenon — traceable directly to merge-slot competition during tokenizer fitting, producing quantifiable and directly measured consequences in context-budget consumption, per-token API cost, and decode latency — rather than gesturing at it as an abstract equity concern disconnected from the actual algorithm. A candidate who can walk from "the merge table is fit to corpus frequency statistics" all the way through to "this is why a fixed context window and per-token pricing are not actually fair across languages" without needing the connection spelled out for them is demonstrating exactly the kind of mechanism-first, first-principles fluency that this class of interview question is designed to surface.
