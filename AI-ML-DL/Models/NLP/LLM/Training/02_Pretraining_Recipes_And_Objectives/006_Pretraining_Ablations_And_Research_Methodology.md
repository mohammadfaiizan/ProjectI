# Pretraining Ablations and Research Methodology

Ablation methodology in this field is best understood as applied decision theory under an extreme cost asymmetry between the price of evidence and the price of being wrong; everything below unpacks what that means in practice.

## 0. Scope

This file covers the methodology for validating pretraining-time decisions — architecture, objective, optimizer, and curriculum changes — before committing them to a full frontier-scale training run. It assumes familiarity with the specific decisions being validated (covered in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`, `003_Model_Architecture_Decisions_At_Pretraining_Time.md`, `004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md`, and `005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md`) and focuses purely on the meta-question of how confidence in any of those decisions is actually earned given this field's cost structure.

Read linearly, the file moves from *why* this is hard (Section 1), through the two primary tools for managing it (proxy models, Section 2; trajectory monitoring, Section 3), into the central unsolved tension (does a proxy result transfer, Section 4) and the organizing principle for spending validation budget wisely against that tension (cost asymmetry, Section 5), before turning concrete: statistical sizing (Section 6), documentation practice (Section 7), a worked case study (Section 8), a decision taxonomy (Section 9), common failure patterns (Section 10), instrumentation and tooling (Sections 11-12, 14b), real-world fingerprints of this methodology in the public record (Section 13), a closing checklist (Section 14), the diminishing-returns counterpoint (Section 14a), and finally an explicit statement of this methodology's limits (Section 16).

## 1. The cost structure that makes this problem unique to this field

In most areas of applied ML research, an idea can be validated at (approximately) the scale it will be deployed at — train the production-size model with and without the change, compare. Frontier LLM pretraining cannot do this: the "production-size" run costs tens to hundreds of millions of dollars, takes weeks to months of wall-clock time on a scarce cluster, and — critically — you generally get to run it **once**. There is no budget for "train DeepSeek-V3 twice, once with and once without the auxiliary-loss-free load-balancing scheme, and compare" (`..\..\OpenSource\007_DeepSeek_V3.md`), no budget for "train Llama-3-405B twice to check whether the 15T-token overtraining strategy was actually necessary" (`..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md`). Every architectural, data, and optimizer decision that goes into the one full-scale run has to be validated at a *smaller* scale first, and then a judgment call has to be made about whether the smaller-scale result will hold at the target scale — a judgment call that cannot itself be empirically verified before the money is spent, because verifying it *is* the expensive run.

This is the core methodological tension this file is about: **ablation methodology at small scale is cheap, fast, and statistically well-powered; the thing you actually care about (does the conclusion hold at target scale) is expensive, slow, and — by the time you can check it directly — the decision has already been made and the budget already spent.** Every technique in this file is a way of managing that tension, not eliminating it; the tension is structural to the cost curve of this field, not a solvable engineering problem.

## 2. Scaled-down proxy models as the primary ablation tool

### 2.1 The basic pattern

To test whether a candidate change (a new attention variant, a different data mixture, a different optimizer setting, a different MoE routing scheme) is worth committing to a frontier run, a lab trains a **matched pair (or family) of much smaller models** — commonly in the tens-of-millions to a few-billion parameter range, at a token budget scaled to keep the comparison meaningful (matched compute, matched \(D/N\) ratio, or matched wall-clock, depending on what question is being asked) — one with the candidate change, one without (the baseline "control" recipe), with every other variable held fixed. This is exactly the same proxy-run apparatus used for fitting scaling-law curves (`002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`, Section 5.1) — in practice, the same proxy grid frequently serves double duty: fitting the \((N,D)\) frontier for the target run, *and* comparing architectural variants along that frontier, because both questions require the same underlying infrastructure (many small, fast, well-instrumented training runs).

### 2.2 What "held fixed" actually requires

Getting a clean ablation at small scale is harder than it sounds, and getting it wrong produces a confidently-misleading answer, not just a noisy one. Genuinely holding "everything else" fixed requires:

- Identical data mixture and identical (or matched-seed) shuffling/ordering, since data order effects are a real, if usually second-order, source of run-to-run variance.
- A learning-rate schedule and peak LR that are each independently *appropriate for the model size being tested* — reusing one recipe's tuned LR for a variant that changes the effective parameter-gradient scaling (e.g., a different normalization scheme, a different attention mechanism with different gradient magnitudes) can make the variant look worse than it is, for reasons that have nothing to do with the thing being ablated.
- Enough training tokens that the comparison is being made past the noisy early-training regime and into a portion of the loss curve where the relative ranking between variants has stabilized (see Section 3 on why *early* trajectory comparisons specifically are risky).
- Ideally, more than one random seed per configuration, since a single training run's loss curve has genuine run-to-run variance from initialization and data-batch-order randomness — a difference between two single-seed runs that's within the seed-to-seed noise band is not evidence of anything, and frontier labs' internal ablation practice (not always disclosed in public reports) generally accounts for this even when a published report shows only one curve per configuration for space reasons.

### 2.3 What this method is, and is not, good for

Proxy-scale ablations are well-suited to answering **relative, same-scale questions**: does variant A produce lower loss than variant B, at this scale, under this compute budget, holding everything else fixed. They are explicitly *not*, by themselves, answers to the question that actually matters for the frontier decision: does that same ranking hold at the target scale. That gap is the subject of Section 4, and is the single most important thing to keep separate from "the ablation showed X was better" — an ablation result is a fact about the scale it was run at, not automatically a fact about any other scale.

## 3. Early loss-curve trajectory as a leading indicator

### 3.1 The practice

Given the multi-week-to-multi-month duration of even a proxy-scale run (let alone the full frontier run), labs routinely make **go/no-go decisions well before a run finishes**, using the *shape* of the loss curve so far as a leading indicator rather than waiting for a final number. Concretely: fitting the empirical loss-vs-step (or loss-vs-tokens) trajectory to the expected power-law shape (`002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`, Section 2) partway through a run, and checking whether the fitted curve's *extrapolated* endpoint is competitive with a baseline's extrapolated endpoint — or, for the actual target-scale run itself, whether the trajectory so far is consistent with the loss the proxy-scale fit predicted for this point in training, which is a direct, real-time check on whether the scaling-law extrapolation that justified the run's \((N,D)\) choice is holding up as the run actually progresses.

This matters because loss curves are not perfectly monotonically informative at every point — a curve that looks worse than a competitor's early on can still cross over and end up better (different schedules, different warmup lengths, and different points on a cosine decay curve all distort a naive "just compare current loss" reading), and a curve that looks *better* early can be on a trajectory that's expected, by the fitted power law, to plateau at a worse final value. Reading trajectory *shape* against a fitted extrapolation, rather than reading absolute loss at a fixed step, is the more defensible version of this practice — though it is still fundamentally an extrapolation, with the same structural risk as any other scaling-law application (Section 4).

### 3.2 Why this is a genuinely load-bearing operational practice, not just a nice-to-have

For a full frontier run specifically, early-trajectory monitoring is one of the only levers available *during* the run itself: if the loss curve several percent of the way through a run is trending meaningfully worse than the fitted expectation, that is real, actionable signal — potentially triggering a hyperparameter intervention (an LR adjustment, investigating a data-pipeline bug, checking for a numerical-stability issue), or in the most severe cases, a decision to abort and restart rather than continue sinking compute into a run that's unlikely to reach its target. Given that a full frontier run cannot be meaningfully "re-tried" from scratch after completion without re-spending the entire budget, catching a problem at 10% of the way through (even if that "10%" is still an enormous absolute compute expenditure) is far preferable to catching it only at the end.

## 4. The core hard problem: does the ablation's conclusion transfer to target scale

### 4.1 Stating the problem precisely

An ablation run at, say, 1B parameters shows technique X reduces loss relative to baseline, at matched compute, at that scale. The question the frontier decision actually needs answered is: does X still reduce loss (and by a similar or larger margin, i.e., is it worth the engineering cost of adopting it) at 500B+ parameters and multi-trillion-token scale? These are **not the same question**, and there is no general theoretical guarantee that a scale-1B answer generalizes to scale-500B. This is not a hypothetical concern raised for completeness — it is a well-documented pattern in this field's history that some architectural or training tricks that measurably help at small scale have smaller, negligible, or even reversed effects at large scale, and conversely that some effects are only visible at large scale at all (this second failure mode is the direct ablation-methodology analogue of "emergent capabilities" in the capabilities literature — an intervention's effect can itself be a function of scale, not a fixed quantity).

### 4.2 Why scale can flip a conclusion: mechanisms worth naming specifically

- **Interaction with the loss landscape's regime.** A change that helps primarily by improving optimization stability or convergence speed in a small, noisy-gradient, early-training-dominated regime may buy little once the model is large enough, and trained long enough, that its optimization dynamics are already comparatively well-behaved for other reasons (e.g., simply because at larger scale, per-step gradient noise relative to signal is different, changing which interventions are load-bearing).
- **Interaction with capacity.** A regularization-flavored change (anything that constrains model capacity or representational flexibility in exchange for some other benefit — a compression scheme, a routing constraint, an architectural bottleneck) can look like a clear net win at small scale, where capacity is the binding constraint and any efficiency gain from the change outweighs the capacity cost, and can look like a net loss at large scale, where capacity is comparatively abundant and the same efficiency gain no longer offsets the same capacity cost, because the model had capacity to spare either way.
- **Interaction with data scale.** Some effects are bottlenecked by how much data the model has seen relative to its size, not by parameter count in isolation — a change validated at a token budget and \(N\) that don't reflect the target run's \(D/N\) ratio (see the Chinchilla-optimal-ratio discussion in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`) may be answering a subtly different question than the one the frontier run's actual \((N,D)\) point poses.
- **Interaction with other components of the recipe that also change with scale.** Batch size, learning rate, and warmup length all change systematically as you scale up (`004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md`, Sections 2 and 4), and an ablation's effect can be entangled with the specific hyperparameter regime it was tested under — a technique that looks neutral or negative under a small-scale-appropriate batch size and LR might behave differently under the large-batch, differently-tuned regime a frontier run actually uses, independent of parameter count per se.

### 4.3 Mitigations, and their honest limits

None of the following *solves* the transfer problem; each reduces (some of) the risk at a real, quantifiable additional cost, and a staff-level answer should be explicit that this is a risk-reduction budget allocation, not a guarantee:

- **Multi-point scaling curves rather than single-point comparisons.** Instead of comparing variant A vs. B at one small scale, run the comparison at several scales (e.g., 100M, 300M, 1B, maybe 3B) and check whether the *gap* between A and B is stable, growing, or shrinking as scale increases. A gap that's shrinking as scale grows is a much stronger warning sign than a gap that's stable or growing — this at least gives a trend to extrapolate from, rather than a single point, though extrapolating a trend fit on 3-4 points across less than one order of magnitude of additional scale out to 2-3 more orders of magnitude is still a substantial, uncertainty-laden extrapolation.
- **Principled scale-transfer methodology (μ-parameterization / "Tensor Programs" line of work).** Rather than empirically hoping a hyperparameter or architectural choice transfers, μP (maximal update parameterization) derives specific *scaling rules* for how initialization variance, learning rate, and certain architectural constants should change with width, such that the optimal hyperparameters found at small width provably remain (approximately) optimal at large width, under the paper's theoretical framework. This converts part of the transfer problem from "hope it holds" to "apply a derived scaling rule and get a theoretical guarantee for the specific quantities the theory covers" — genuinely valuable, but its guarantees are scoped to the specific quantities (width-scaling of a defined set of hyperparameters) the theory addresses; it does not, by itself, resolve the transfer question for an arbitrary architectural change (e.g., a new attention variant or MoE routing scheme) outside that scope.
- **A staged confidence-building pipeline: small ablation → medium-scale "canary" run → full commit.** Rather than jumping directly from a 1B-scale ablation to the full frontier run, a lab with sufficient budget can insert an intermediate-scale confirmation run (e.g., a few tens of billions of parameters) that is still far cheaper than the frontier run but large enough to catch at least the more severe non-transfer failure modes before the full budget is committed. This is a real, used pattern — but it is itself a cost/risk tradeoff, not a free additional safety net: every intermediate confirmation stage adds real calendar time and real compute cost, competing directly against the org's overall time-to-ship and compute-budget pressures, so the decision of *how much* intermediate confirmation to insert is itself a judgment call under the same cost-asymmetry logic (Section 5).
- **Preferring changes with a plausible mechanistic story for why the effect should be scale-invariant (or should if anything strengthen with scale), over changes validated only by "it worked empirically at small scale, no clear reason given."** This is a softer, judgment-based mitigation rather than a technique — a change whose benefit is argued to come from, say, reducing serving cost via a well-understood cache-compression mechanism (MLA-style; `003_Model_Architecture_Decisions_At_Pretraining_Time.md`) has a clearer story for why the benefit should persist (or grow) at scale than a change whose benefit is a purely empirical small-scale loss-curve delta with no accompanying mechanistic account of *why* it should hold at 500x the parameter count.

## 5. Cost asymmetry as the organizing principle behind all of the above

Every mitigation in Section 4.3 costs real compute and real calendar time, and none of them provides certainty — which means the actual operational decision a lab faces is a **budget-allocation problem across a spectrum of decreasing risk and increasing cost**: spend nothing extra and accept the raw small-scale-ablation risk; spend a bit more on a multi-point scaling curve; spend meaningfully more on an intermediate canary run; or, for changes with a strong theoretical scale-transfer story (μP-covered quantities), spend comparatively little and lean on the theory. The right amount to spend is itself a function of (a) how large and irreversible the frontier commitment is (a change to the core attention mechanism or the dense-vs-MoE decision, both effectively unchangeable after the run starts, deserves more validation spend than a data-mixture percentage that can be adjusted stage-by-stage per `005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md`), and (b) how far outside the validated range the target scale sits (a change validated up to 10B being deployed at 70B is a smaller extrapolation than the same change validated at 1B being deployed at 500B+). Making this cost/risk tradeoff explicit — rather than treating "we ran an ablation" as automatically sufficient evidence, or conversely treating the transfer problem as unsolvable and therefore ignorable — is the actual research-methodology skill this file is about, and is precisely the kind of judgment a staff research engineer is expected to exercise and be able to articulate on demand.

## 6. Statistical power at small scale: how many seeds is actually enough

Section 2.2 asserts that seed variance matters; it is worth making this quantitative, because "run a couple of extra seeds" is not by itself a rigorous stopping rule, and a staff-level ablation practice should be able to say *why* a given number of seeds is enough for a given claimed effect size.

### 6.1 The basic estimator

If a single training run's final loss (at a fixed token budget) has standard deviation \(\sigma\) across seeds — itself something that has to be measured empirically for a given model/data setup by running several seeds of the *same* configuration — then the standard error of the mean over \(k\) seeds is \(\sigma/\sqrt{k}\), and the standard error of the *difference* between two \(k\)-seed means (variant vs. baseline, assuming independent, comparable variance for both) is \(\sigma\sqrt{2/k}\). A claimed effect of size \(\Delta\) is only distinguishable from noise at a conventional threshold (say, requiring the effect to exceed roughly 2 standard errors of the difference, a rough stand-in for a two-sided test at conventional significance) when:

\[
\Delta > 2\sigma\sqrt{2/k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k > \frac{8\sigma^2}{\Delta^2}
\]

```python
import math

def min_seeds_for_effect(sigma: float, effect_size: float, z: float = 2.0) -> int:
    """Minimum seeds per arm (variant and baseline) needed to distinguish
    `effect_size` from noise at a z-standard-error threshold, given the
    empirically measured per-run standard deviation `sigma` at this scale."""
    k = (2 * (z ** 2) * (sigma ** 2)) / (effect_size ** 2)
    return math.ceil(k)
```

### 6.2 Why this bites harder than intuition suggests

Two properties of this formula are easy to underweight. First, required seed count scales as \(1/\Delta^2\) — halving the effect size you need to reliably detect *quadruples* the number of seeds required, which matters directly for exactly the kind of claim frontier decisions are made on ("this change is worth adopting for a ~1-2% loss improvement," a small effect size relative to typical seed-to-seed loss variance at small proxy scale). Second, \(\sigma\) itself is not free to estimate — it requires its own multi-seed baseline sweep before you can even size the comparison correctly, which is a real, often-skipped cost in a resource-constrained ablation program; skipping it and running a single seed per arm because "we don't have budget for variance estimation" means the resulting comparison's evidentiary strength is simply unknown, not merely weak.

### 6.3 The practical compromise

Frontier labs virtually never run enough seeds to reach textbook statistical rigor for every ablation — compute is scarce even at proxy scale, and running 10+ seeds per configuration to detect a 1% effect reliably is itself expensive when compounded across a large ablation program covering many candidate changes. The practical compromise generally used: reserve rigorous multi-seed sizing for the small number of *large, expensive, hard-to-reverse* decisions (architecture-family choices, load-balancing mechanism, optimizer family), where Section 5's cost-asymmetry argument justifies the extra proxy-scale spend, and accept looser, single-or-double-seed evidence for smaller, cheaply-reversible decisions (a data-mixture percentage adjustable stage-by-stage, per `005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md`) — explicitly trading statistical rigor for ablation throughput exactly where the downside of being wrong is smaller.

## 7. Writing the decision record: making the uncertainty explicit rather than laundering it away

A practice worth naming directly because it is a genuine differentiator of mature research organizations: when an ablation result is used to justify a frontier-scale commitment, the decision record accompanying that commitment should state, explicitly and in writing, not just the result but its evidentiary limits — the proxy scale(s) tested, the extrapolation gap to target scale, the seed count and estimated variance (Section 6), which of Section 4.3's mitigations were and weren't applied, and a stated confidence level rather than a bare "we validated X." This matters for two concrete reasons beyond due diligence for its own sake. First, if the target-scale run underperforms expectations, a decision record that honestly stated its extrapolation risk up front lets the team distinguish "the bet was reasonable given what was knowable, and didn't pay off" from "the process itself was sloppy" — a distinction that matters enormously for whether the org's ablation methodology needs to change or whether this was simply the field's structural residual risk (Section 5) materializing. Second, and more subtly, a decision record that is forced to state the extrapolation gap explicitly (e.g., "validated at 1B and 8B, target is 400B, a 50x further extrapolation than our largest tested point") tends, in practice, to surface exactly the kind of "should we actually spend more on a canary run first" conversation that an informally-communicated "the ablation looked good" verdict does not — making the uncertainty visible is itself a mechanism that improves the underlying decision, not just a paperwork exercise.

## 8. A worked case study: sizing the validation spend for a hypothetical MoE routing change

Concretely tying Sections 4-7 together. Suppose a candidate MoE load-balancing change shows a 2% loss improvement at 1B activated parameters (roughly matching the scale in `..\..\OpenSource\007_DeepSeek_V3.md`'s own architecture family, several orders of magnitude below its 37B-activated/671B-total target), with a measured per-seed standard deviation of \(\sigma \approx 0.008\) nats/token at that scale from a small variance-estimation sweep.

```python
seeds_needed = min_seeds_for_effect(sigma=0.008, effect_size=0.02 * 3.2)  # ~2% of a ~3.2 baseline loss
# -> a small, affordable number of seeds per arm at 1B scale, given sigma << effect_size here
```

If \(\sigma=0.008\) and the absolute effect size is \(0.064\) (2% of a baseline loss around 3.2 nats/token), `min_seeds_for_effect` returns a very small integer — the effect is large relative to noise at this scale, so a modest seed count comfortably clears the statistical bar. That statistical clearance, however, only answers "is this a real effect at 1B," not "does it hold at 37B-activated/671B-total" — which is exactly Section 4's transfer question, not a question Section 6's seed-count machinery touches at all. Given the change targets the MoE load-balancing mechanism specifically (an effectively irreversible, training-and-serving-infrastructure-wide commitment, per `003_Model_Architecture_Decisions_At_Pretraining_Time.md` Section 1.1), Section 5's cost-asymmetry logic argues for *not* stopping at the statistically-clean 1B result: the appropriate next spend is a multi-point scaling curve (1B, and at least one point an order of magnitude or more higher) checking whether the 2% gap is stable, and — given the scale of the eventual target — very plausibly an intermediate canary run before full commitment, exactly the staged pipeline described in Section 4.3 and worked through operationally in `008_Interview_Questions_Part2.md`, Q33 and Q39. A statistically well-powered 1B result and a *scale-transfer-validated* result are different claims, and the case study's point is that it is easy to have rigorously established the former while having done nothing at all to establish the latter.

## 9. A taxonomy of ablation questions, and why they don't all deserve the same validation budget

Not every question that gets called "an ablation" carries the same transfer risk, and conflating them is a common source of either wasted validation spend or, worse, under-validated large commitments. It is worth explicitly sorting candidate changes into rough categories before deciding how much of Sections 6-8's machinery to apply to each:

| Category | Example | Reversibility once committed | Typical transfer risk | Suggested validation depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core attention/architecture family | MHA vs. GQA vs. MLA; dense vs. MoE | Effectively irreversible mid-run | High — capacity and communication-pattern interactions (Section 4.2) | Multi-point scaling curve + intermediate canary run |
| Load-balancing / routing mechanism | Auxiliary-loss vs. bias-controlled MoE balancing | Very costly to change mid-run | High — control-loop dynamics can behave differently under different token-per-step and communication regimes | Multi-point curve + canary; explicit decision record (Section 7) |
| Optimizer family | AdamW vs. Lion vs. Muon | Costly; entangled with most other tuned hyperparameters | High, and additionally judged against a strong incumbent with its own frontier-scale track record | Very high bar (per `004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md` Section 5.3); rarely adopted on ablation evidence alone at first attempt |
| Learning-rate/batch-size operating point | Peak LR, warmup length, target batch size | Adjustable within a run only within limits; largely fixed by schedule choice | Moderate — critical-batch-size and warmup-sensitivity effects shift with scale (`004_...md` Section 4) | Proxy sweep plus in-run trajectory monitoring (Section 3) as a live check |
| Data-mixture percentages | Fraction of code/math/web tokens in the main-pretraining mixture | Adjustable stage-by-stage, especially late (`005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md`) | Lower — effects are more continuous and correctable mid-course | Single-seed or lightly-replicated proxy comparison, revisited during annealing |
| Context-extension/positional-scaling parameters | YaRN ramp boundaries, RoPE base frequency | Correctable in a later dedicated stage without touching the main run | Lower — validated directly against long-context benchmarks at the target length, not just loss | Proxy-scale check plus direct long-context evaluation (`003_...md` Section 3.2) |

The pattern worth internalizing: validation depth should track **(reversibility) × (transfer risk)**, not the raw magnitude of the measured small-scale effect size — a change with a huge measured effect at small scale but low transfer risk and easy reversibility (a data-mixture tweak) does not need the same canary-run treatment as a modest-effect-size but high-transfer-risk, irreversible architectural bet.

## 10. Common pitfalls in practice, stated as failure patterns rather than abstract warnings

- **Comparing at mismatched compute rather than mismatched wall-clock or mismatched token count**, without being explicit about which axis is actually held fixed — a variant that's slower per step (e.g., due to added routing or communication overhead) can look worse in a wall-clock-matched comparison and look better (or neutral) in a FLOPs-matched comparison; reporting "variant A beats variant B" without stating which axis was held fixed is close to meaningless.
- **Reading a single loss number at the end of a short proxy run as if it were the converged, final answer**, when the proxy run's token budget doesn't actually correspond to a compute-matched or trajectory-stabilized point (Section 2.2) — this is the single most common way an ablation looks decisive at small scale and reverses at any larger comparison.
- **Silently changing more than one variable between the "control" and "treatment" configurations** — a new attention variant that also happens to require a different learning rate for stability is not a clean ablation of "attention variant" unless the LR is separately re-tuned for both arms; otherwise the measured effect is a bundle of the architectural change and an LR mismatch, and cannot be attributed to either cleanly.
- **Treating a proxy-scale scaling-law fit and a proxy-scale architectural ablation as though they carry the same evidentiary weight**, when a scaling-law fit (Section 3, `002_...md`) is at least checking a smooth, well-studied empirical regularity with decades of precedent for holding across some range, while an arbitrary architectural ablation has no comparable prior track record for extrapolating cleanly — the same "N proxy points, extrapolate" *procedure* does not carry the same *confidence* for both kinds of claims.
- **Underestimating how much a "small" intermediate canary run still costs**, and therefore skipping it under time pressure while telling oneself the 1B-scale evidence was "basically as good" — Section 5's entire point is that this is a real, quantifiable risk tradeoff being made, not a free simplification, and it should be named as a tradeoff in the decision record (Section 7) rather than implicitly assumed away.

## 11. Instrumentation: what to actually log during an ablation, and why each item matters

A surprising amount of ablation-methodology failure traces back not to the comparison logic but to simply not having logged the right signal to make the comparison trustworthy after the fact. A reasonably complete instrumentation set for a proxy-scale ablation run includes:

- **Full loss curve, not just a final number** — logged at fine enough step granularity to support the trajectory-shape analysis in Section 3, and to distinguish "converged to a stable value" from "still improving when the run was stopped," which materially changes how a final-loss comparison should be read.
- **Per-seed results, not pre-averaged** — averaging seeds before storage discards exactly the variance information Section 6's power calculation needs; store every seed's trajectory and compute statistics on read, not on write.
- **Wall-clock and FLOPs consumed, tracked separately** — a variant that changes per-step compute (added routing, a more expensive attention mechanism) needs both numbers to disentangle "this variant is better per FLOP" from "this variant is better per wall-clock-second," which, per Section 10's first pitfall, are genuinely different claims.
- **Gradient-norm and gradient-noise-scale statistics over the course of training** — not just final loss — since a variant that reaches similar final loss via a much noisier or more marginal optimization trajectory is a weaker candidate for scale-transfer than one that reaches the same loss cleanly, even if the two look identical on final loss alone.
- **Hyperparameters actually used for each arm, explicitly, including anything auto-tuned** — so that a later reviewer (or the same researcher, months later) can tell whether the comparison held everything fixed correctly (Section 2.2) without having to reconstruct it from memory or from a script that may have since changed.
- **The proxy scale(s) tested and the intended target scale, recorded together** — so the extrapolation gap (Section 7's decision-record practice) is visible directly alongside the result, not something a reader has to infer separately.

## 12. A minimal data model and portfolio-scoring sketch for an ablation program

Frontier labs typically run many candidate ablations concurrently against a single upcoming frontier commitment, and have to decide which to actually carry forward into the more expensive validation stages (Sections 6-8) given limited canary-run budget. A simple way to make that portfolio decision explicit, rather than purely a matter of individual researcher conviction, is to score each candidate on exactly the axes this file has built up — measured effect size, evidentiary strength (seed count relative to Section 6's requirement), and cost-asymmetry category (Section 9) — and rank accordingly:

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class AblationCandidate:
    name: str
    effect_size: float          # measured loss delta at proxy scale (positive = improvement)
    sigma: float                # measured per-seed std dev at proxy scale
    n_seeds_run: int            # seeds actually run per arm
    reversibility_score: float  # 0 (fully reversible, e.g. data mixture) .. 1 (irreversible, e.g. attention family)
    canary_run_cost_flops: float

def evidentiary_strength(c: AblationCandidate) -> float:
    """Ratio of seeds actually run to seeds required for a 2-sigma-of-difference
    detection of the claimed effect (Section 6) -- >= 1.0 means adequately powered."""
    required = min_seeds_for_effect(c.sigma, c.effect_size)
    return c.n_seeds_run / max(1, required)

def canary_priority_score(c: AblationCandidate) -> float:
    """Higher score = higher priority for scarce canary-run budget.
    Rewards large, well-evidenced effects; penalizes candidates whose
    reversibility is low (i.e., irreversible bets need MORE scrutiny,
    not less, before committing scarce canary budget -- so a low
    evidentiary_strength on a high-reversibility_score candidate is
    weighted as more urgent to resolve before the frontier run, not less)."""
    strength = evidentiary_strength(c)
    urgency = c.reversibility_score * (1.0 / max(0.1, strength))
    return c.effect_size * urgency / max(1.0, c.canary_run_cost_flops ** 0.1)
```

The specific scoring function is illustrative, not a formula any lab is known to use verbatim — the point worth taking from it is structural: **canary-run budget should be allocated preferentially toward candidates that combine a large irreversible commitment with currently-weak evidentiary strength**, not toward whichever candidate simply has the largest raw measured effect size. A large effect size on an easily-reversible, low-stakes change (a data-mixture percentage) is comparatively low-priority for scarce canary budget precisely because being wrong about it is cheap to correct later (`005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md`'s staged curriculum already provides natural, cheap correction points); a modest effect size on an irreversible architectural bet with only single-seed evidence is exactly the case that should be escalated for more validation spend before the frontier run locks it in.

## 13. This methodology's fingerprints in the public record

None of the labs covered elsewhere in this collection publish their internal ablation logs, so nothing here should be read as a disclosed account of any specific lab's canary-run process — but several publicly documented facts are consistent with, and best explained by, exactly the staged-validation pattern this file describes, and it's worth naming them as illustrative evidence that the pattern is real rather than purely theoretical:

- **DeepSeek's auxiliary-loss-free MoE load-balancing mechanism was carried across two model generations (V2 to V3) rather than introduced fully-formed** (`..\..\OpenSource\006_DeepSeek_V2.md`, `..\..\OpenSource\007_DeepSeek_V3.md`) — V2 shipped with a more conventional auxiliary-loss-based balancing approach, and the bias-controlled mechanism appears at V3. This sequencing is consistent with a lab that validated the MoE architecture family's basic feasibility first, and introduced the higher-risk, more novel load-balancing mechanism (exactly the category Section 9's taxonomy flags as deserving the deepest validation) only once a mature in-house MoE training stack already existed — i.e., de-risking one axis (MoE feasibility) before stacking a second, harder-to-reverse bet (a genuinely new balancing mechanism) on top of it.
- **Llama 3's explicit statement that 8B and 70B training continued well past a conventional stopping point because loss curves had not saturated** (`..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md`, Section 5) is a direct, disclosed instance of Section 3's trajectory-monitoring practice being used as a live, in-run decision signal — the decision to keep training was made *by watching the trajectory*, not by pre-committing to a fixed token budget derived purely from an offline scaling-law fit and then stopping regardless of what the curve was doing.
- **GLM's usage drift toward the `[gMASK]`-dominant, causal-generation-like regime by the GLM-4 generation, despite the original GLM objective's more general multi-span design** (`..\..\OpenSource\010_GLM4.md`, Section 2) is a real-world instance of exactly the scale/deployment-transfer question this file is about, playing out at the level of an entire training objective rather than a narrow architectural detail: a design validated and published as a general unified objective evidently did not carry the same practical weight at later, larger-scale, product-oriented iterations, and the lineage's own usage pattern shifted in response — a useful reminder that transfer risk (Section 4) applies to high-level objective choices (`001_Pretraining_Objectives_Overview.md`) and not only to the narrower architectural/optimizer ablations this file's examples otherwise emphasize.
- **The near-universal disclosed pattern of short-native-context pretraining followed by a small, separate context-extension stage** (DeepSeek-V2, Llama 3.1, Qwen2.5 — see `005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md`, Section 3) is itself evidence of the cost-asymmetry logic in Section 5 being applied consistently across independent labs: every one of them chose to treat long-context capability as a cheap, late, separately-validated add-on rather than a property to bet the entire main run's context length on from step zero, which is exactly the conservative, reversibility-aware posture Section 9's taxonomy recommends for a costly-to-reverse architectural parameter.

## 14. A closing checklist

As a compact summary of this file, before signing off on carrying any candidate change into a frontier commitment, a staff research engineer should be able to answer each of the following, specifically, rather than in the abstract:

1. At what scale(s) was this validated, and how large is the extrapolation gap to the target scale, stated as an order-of-magnitude number?
2. How many seeds were run per arm, and does that meet or fall short of the Section 6 requirement for the claimed effect size, given the measured (not assumed) variance at the tested scale?
3. Which category (Section 9's taxonomy) does this change fall into, and does the validation depth actually applied match what that category's reversibility and transfer-risk profile calls for?
4. Is there a plausible mechanistic story for why the effect should hold (or strengthen) at larger scale, or is the evidence purely an empirical small-scale delta with no accompanying explanation?
5. Has an intermediate canary run been run, and if not, is that omission an explicit, acknowledged risk-acceptance decision (Section 7) or an implicit one nobody has actually stated out loud?
6. Is the comparison confounded by anything in Section 10's pitfall list — mismatched compute/wall-clock axis, an unstabilized proxy-run token budget, more than one variable changed at once, or an implicit assumption that scaling-law-style extrapolation confidence applies equally to an arbitrary architectural claim?

A candidate change that can be answered cleanly on all six points has earned a meaningfully higher level of confidence than one that can only gesture at "the ablation looked good" — and being able to walk an interviewer, or a real internal reviewer, through exactly this list for a specific proposed change is the concrete, demonstrable form of the research-methodology judgment this entire file is trying to develop.

## 14a. When to stop validating and ship: the diminishing-returns side of the cost asymmetry

Section 5 frames validation spend as a budget-allocation problem, which cuts both ways — it is possible to over-invest in validation just as it is possible to under-invest, and a mature research organization should be able to recognize both failure modes. Endless additional canary runs, additional seeds, additional intermediate scales all consume the same calendar time and compute budget that could otherwise go toward the frontier run itself, or toward the next candidate improvement in the pipeline entirely. The diminishing-returns curve here is real: the *marginal* risk reduction from a third or fourth intermediate-scale confirmation point, once two have already shown a stable, non-shrinking effect, is typically much smaller than the risk reduction bought by the first confirmation point beyond the initial small-scale ablation. A staff-level judgment call includes recognizing when a candidate change has already cleared a reasonable bar for its risk category (Section 9) and further validation is defensive theater rather than genuine risk reduction — particularly under a hard external deadline, where the opportunity cost of additional validation calendar time (a competitor's model shipping first, a product commitment slipping) is itself a cost that belongs in the same decision, not an afterthought weighed only informally. There is no formula that resolves this tradeoff cleanly; naming it explicitly, and being able to justify why a given validation program stopped where it did rather than continuing indefinitely, is again exactly the kind of judgment this file is trying to make legible rather than left implicit.

## 14b. Experiment-tracking infrastructure as a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have

Everything in Sections 6-12 — per-seed logging, trajectory-shape comparisons against a fitted extrapolation, portfolio-level prioritization across many concurrent ablation candidates — presupposes an experiment-tracking system capable of storing, querying, and comparing large numbers of small training runs' full metric histories, hyperparameters, and metadata (proxy scale, target scale, canary status) in a structured, queryable way. This is easy to take for granted in a mature organization and easy to underestimate in a newly-formed one (directly relevant to the scenario worked through in `008_Interview_Questions_Part2.md`, Q39): a lab running dozens of concurrent proxy-scale ablations against an upcoming frontier commitment, without a system that can answer "show me every ablation candidate currently below the Section 6 evidentiary-strength threshold, sorted by reversibility score," is effectively running this entire methodology by memory and spreadsheet, which scales very poorly exactly when the number of concurrent candidates and the stakes of the eventual frontier decision are both largest. Building this infrastructure is therefore properly counted as part of the *cost* of doing rigorous ablation methodology at all, not a separable concern — an organization that has not budgeted for it should expect its actual validation rigor to fall well short of what this file describes in practice, regardless of how well its research staff understands the methodology in principle.

## 15. Connection to the rest of this module

This file's concerns are not separable from the other five in this module — they are the methodology *underneath* every decision described elsewhere: the compute-optimal \((N,D)\) fit in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md` is itself a scale-transfer extrapolation subject to everything in Section 4 here; the dense-vs-MoE and attention-variant decisions in `003_Model_Architecture_Decisions_At_Pretraining_Time.md` are exactly the kind of large, irreversible commitments Section 5's cost-asymmetry logic says deserve the most validation spend, and Section 9's taxonomy is a direct elaboration of that same file's decision-making framing; the optimizer and hyperparameter choices in `004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md` (batch size, LR, and the Lion/Muon-vs-AdamW question in particular) are directly subject to the "does this transfer to frontier scale" problem, which is exactly why the field's conservatism there was framed as a rational response to cost asymmetry rather than an unwillingness to consider new evidence; and the multi-stage curriculum in `005_Curriculum_And_Multi_Stage_Pretraining.md` — long-context extension, annealing — is itself validated, when it is validated at all before being committed to a frontier run, via exactly the proxy-scale and trajectory-monitoring methodology described in this file. Treat this file as the connective tissue explaining *how a lab earns the right to be confident* in every other decision this module describes, and, just as importantly, how much residual uncertainty remains even after that process, no matter how careful it is.

## 16. Where this methodology's authority runs out

It is worth closing on an explicit limit rather than implying this file's framework resolves the underlying problem. Everything above is a set of practices for *managing* the cost-structure tension named in Section 1 — reducing, prioritizing, and making visible the residual risk of scale-transfer failure — not a method for eliminating it. Even a lab that runs every mitigation in Section 4.3, sizes every ablation per Section 6, writes a careful decision record per Section 7, and stages a canary run per Section 8's case study can still have its frontier run underperform the validated expectation, for the simple reason that no amount of proxy-scale evidence is logically equivalent to target-scale evidence — it is, at best, a well-reasoned, well-documented, appropriately-humble bet. The honest, staff-level position to hold (and to state plainly in an interview rather than implying false certainty either direction) is that this entire methodology exists precisely *because* frontier-scale ground truth is unaffordable to obtain more than once per commitment, and the field's collective practice is better described as continuously improving its risk-adjusted decision-making under that constraint than as having solved the underlying scale-transfer problem itself. That residual, irreducible uncertainty is not a gap in this file's treatment of the topic — it is the topic's actual, structural conclusion, and recognizing it as such is itself the deepest form of the research-methodology judgment this file has been building toward.

## 17. Quick-reference glossary for this file's core terms

- **Proxy scale** — the (much smaller than target) parameter/token scale at which an ablation or scaling-law fit is actually run, chosen for affordability rather than representativeness.
- **Canary run** — an intermediate-scale confirmation run, more expensive than a proxy ablation but far cheaper than the full frontier run, inserted specifically to catch scale-transfer failures before full commitment (Section 4.3, Section 8).
- **Scale-transfer risk** — the risk that a conclusion validated at proxy scale does not hold, in direction or magnitude, at the target frontier scale (Section 4).
- **Cost asymmetry** — the structural fact that being wrong about a large, irreversible frontier commitment is far more expensive than the incremental validation spend that could have caught the error beforehand, which is the underlying justification for every mitigation in this file (Section 5).
- **Gradient noise scale / critical batch size** — the batch-size regime boundary past which additional samples per batch stop buying proportional training-efficiency gains, discussed fully in `004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md` Section 4 and referenced here as an example of a scale-sensitive quantity that itself needs re-estimation, not a fixed constant, across proxy and target scales.
- **IsoFLOP profile** — a family of runs at varying \(N\) with \(D\) set so that \(6ND\) is held constant, used to trace out the compute-optimal frontier (`002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md` Section 4.1) and, by extension, one of the standard proxy-grid designs this file's ablation methodology reuses.
- **Evidentiary strength** — this file's Section 6/12 shorthand for whether a measured effect at proxy scale is adequately powered (enough seeds relative to measured variance and claimed effect size) to be trusted as a real effect at all, prior to any question about whether it transfers to target scale.
- **Decision record** — the written artifact (Section 7) accompanying a frontier commitment that states not just the ablation result but its evidentiary limits, explicitly, so that outcomes can later be attributed to a reasonable bet not paying off versus a genuinely sloppy process.
- **Trajectory monitoring** — reading the *shape* of a loss curve so far against a fitted extrapolation, rather than reading absolute loss at a fixed step, as a leading indicator usable while a run is still in progress (Section 3).
- **Reversibility score** — an informal measure of how cheaply a given decision can be corrected after training has begun; the primary axis, alongside transfer risk, that should drive validation-spend allocation (Section 9, Section 12).
- **Portfolio prioritization** — the practice of ranking many concurrent ablation candidates against a shared, scarce pool of canary-run budget, rather than evaluating each candidate's validation spend in isolation (Section 12).
- **Diminishing-returns stopping rule** — the (necessarily judgment-based) point at which additional validation spend no longer buys a proportional reduction in scale-transfer risk, and continuing to validate becomes a cost with no offsetting benefit (Section 14a).
- **Experiment-tracking infrastructure** — the structured logging and querying system that makes per-seed, per-scale, per-candidate ablation data actually usable at the volume a real ablation program generates; treated in this file as a cost of doing rigorous methodology at all, not an optional convenience (Section 14b).
