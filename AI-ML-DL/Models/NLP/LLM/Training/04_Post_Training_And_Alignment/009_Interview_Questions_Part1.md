## Interview Questions -- Part 1

## Q1: SFT alone tends to produce models that hedge excessively and rarely commit to a confident, specific answer among several plausible ones. Explain the mechanism behind this precisely -- don't just say "SFT is limited."

The mechanism is a direct consequence of what maximum-likelihood training on demonstrations actually optimizes. SFT minimizes the forward KL divergence `KL(p_data || p_theta)` between the empirical demonstration distribution and the model. Forward KL is mode-covering: wherever the demonstration distribution places nonzero mass, the loss penalizes the model heavily for assigning that region near-zero probability, because `-log p_theta(x)` diverges as `p_theta(x) -> 0`. If the underlying demonstration set contains inconsistency -- different labelers (or different synthetic-generation passes) answering a similar class of question with genuinely different styles, confidence levels, or committed positions -- the model is being asked to place high likelihood on multiple, mutually inconsistent target behaviors for similar inputs. The maximum-likelihood-optimal response to an inconsistent multimodal target, under a model that outputs a single conditional distribution per input, is not to pick one mode and confidently commit to it; it's to spread probability mass across the observed modes, which in language-generation terms surfaces as hedging, qualification, and a reluctance to fully commit to any one specific claim.

This is different from, and should not be conflated with, a capability limitation -- the model may well "know," in the sense of having the relevant knowledge from pretraining, which answer is best. The hedging is a direct artifact of the training objective's response to inconsistent supervision, not a knowledge gap. It follows that the fix is not "more SFT data" in general, but either (a) making the demonstration set more internally consistent for the affected prompt classes, or (b) moving past pure imitation to a preference-based objective (DPO/RLHF) that can express "this specific committed answer is preferred over that hedged one," which MLE-on-demonstrations structurally cannot express with only one exemplar per prompt.

## Q2: Implement the Bradley-Terry pairwise preference loss used to train a reward model, given a prompt and two candidate responses with known preference. Make sure your implementation is numerically stable.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def bradley_terry_loss(reward_model, prompt_ids, chosen_ids, rejected_ids,
                        chosen_mask, rejected_mask):
    """
    reward_model: takes (input_ids, attention_mask) -> scalar reward per example, shape (batch,)
    prompt_ids is assumed already concatenated into chosen_ids/rejected_ids.
    """
    r_chosen = reward_model(chosen_ids, chosen_mask)      # shape (batch,)
    r_rejected = reward_model(rejected_ids, rejected_mask)  # shape (batch,)

    # Bradley-Terry: P(chosen preferred) = sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected)
    # loss = -log sigmoid(margin); use logsigmoid directly rather than
    # torch.log(torch.sigmoid(margin)) -- the two-step version underflows to
    # log(0) = -inf for large negative margins well before logsigmoid does,
    # because logsigmoid is implemented as -softplus(-x), a numerically stable
    # reformulation of the same mathematical quantity.
    margin = r_chosen - r_rejected
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(margin)

    return loss.mean()
```

Two details worth calling out unprompted, since they're exactly what a staff interviewer is checking for beyond the happy-path formula: first, `logsigmoid` versus a naive `log(sigmoid(...))` implementation -- for a badly-wrong pair (large negative margin), `sigmoid` underflows to exactly `0.0` in float32 well before the point where `logsigmoid` would, so the naive version silently produces `-inf` loss and `NaN` gradients on exactly the hard examples that should be providing the most useful training signal. Second, if training on K-wise rankings decomposed into `C(K,2)` pairs per prompt, the loss should be normalized per prompt by the number of comparisons contributed by that prompt, and all `C(K,2)` pairs from one prompt should be computed from `K` cached forward passes rather than redundantly re-running the reward model for every pairing -- both are documented details in the InstructGPT reward-model training methodology, not optional polish.

## Q3: Derive, precisely, why the KL penalty in RLHF's PPO stage is necessary. Don't just say "to prevent reward hacking" -- explain the actual mechanism.

The reward model `r_theta(x,y)` is trained by maximum likelihood on a finite, noisy sample of human (or AI) pairwise comparisons. Like any supervised model, it is only accurate within, and close to, the distribution of `(x,y)` pairs it was actually trained and validated on; nothing constrains its behavior on inputs far outside that distribution, and there is no reason to expect it to remain well-calibrated there. An RL process with the unconstrained objective `max_pi E[r_theta(x,y)]` has exactly one incentive: find whatever region of response-space maximizes `r_theta`, regardless of whether `r_theta` remains an accurate proxy for genuine human preference in that region. Because `r_theta` is guaranteed to have blind spots somewhere outside its training distribution (it is a finite-capacity model fit to finite data, not a perfect oracle), an unconstrained optimizer will, given enough optimization pressure, discover and drift toward those blind spots -- producing text that scores highly under `r_theta` without being genuinely high quality. This is Goodhart's Law made mechanical: once a measure becomes the direct target of optimization, its correlation with the true underlying quantity of interest degrades precisely in proportion to how hard it's being optimized against.

The KL penalty term, `beta * KL(pi_phi(.|x) || pi^SFT(.|x))`, subtracted from the reward, anchors the policy to the region around the SFT model's distribution -- which is, not coincidentally, close to the actual distribution the reward model was trained and validated on, since RM training data is typically sampled from SFT-model-era policies. This bounds how far the policy is allowed to travel while climbing `r_theta`, keeping it within a region where the reward model's judgments remain comparatively trustworthy. It does not make the reward model perfectly accurate, and it does not eliminate the underlying gap between proxy and true reward -- it manages how much of that gap the policy is permitted to exploit, via the single tunable coefficient `beta`. Empirically, this tradeoff is not merely qualitative: follow-up scaling-law-style studies of reward-model overoptimization show the gap between true and proxy reward grows in a roughly predictable way as the policy's measured KL divergence from the reference grows, which is the quantitative form of exactly this mechanism.

## Q4: Scenario -- your team ships a fine-tuned assistant, and a few weeks after an RLHF refresh, users report that responses have become subtly longer and more "padded" without being more useful, and an internal reward-model score has been trending steadily upward. Walk through how you'd diagnose and fix this.

This is a textbook reward-hacking/overoptimization signature, and the diagnosis should be systematic rather than jumping straight to "retrain everything." First, separate the two observations: a rising internal RM score is the *proxy* metric; user-perceived non-usefulness is evidence the *true* quality signal is not moving in the same direction, or is moving less, or is even declining. That divergence between proxy and true signal is the core symptom of RM overoptimization (File 002, Section 4.5's true-vs-proxy reward gap growing with policy drift). Concretely, I'd check: (a) response length distribution over training time, correlated against RM score, to test directly whether the RM has learned the well-documented length bias (longer responses score higher independent of genuine informativeness); (b) measured KL divergence of the current policy from the SFT/original reference model over the same training window, to see whether the policy has drifted unusually far, which would corroborate that the model is exploring regions outside the RM's well-validated training distribution; (c) a fresh, small human-evaluation sample specifically comparing the newest checkpoint against an earlier one on genuine helpfulness, independent of the RM, to get an unbiased read on whether true quality actually regressed or merely didn't improve as much as the RM score suggests.

Assuming this confirms reward hacking via length gaming, the fix has several levers, roughly in order of cost: first, tighten the KL coefficient `beta` for future training or reduce the number of PPO steps run against the current RM checkpoint (cheapest, addresses the symptom directly); second, apply an explicit length penalty or length-normalization term to the reward signal, directly counteracting the specific spurious correlation rather than only the general drift; third, and most durably, refresh the RM by collecting a new batch of preference comparisons specifically sampled from the current policy's own (longer, padded) output distribution, explicitly including comparisons where a shorter, equally-substantive response is marked preferred over a longer, padded one, retraining the RM to no longer reward length independent of substance. I'd treat option one as an immediate mitigation and option three as the actual fix, since tightening `beta` alone only slows the exploitation without correcting the underlying flawed reward signal.

## Q5: Derive the DPO loss from the KL-constrained RLHF objective, and then implement it.

Start from the objective PPO is (approximately) solving: `max_pi E_{y~pi(.|x)}[r(x,y)] - beta * KL(pi(.|x) || pi_ref(.|x))`. Solving this per-prompt via a Lagrangian for the normalization constraint gives the closed-form optimal policy `pi*(y|x) = (1/Z(x)) * pi_ref(y|x) * exp(r(x,y)/beta)`, where `Z(x) = sum_y pi_ref(y|x) * exp(r(x,y)/beta)` is an intractable per-prompt normalizer. Rearranging for `r`: `r(x,y) = beta * log(pi*(y|x)/pi_ref(y|x)) + beta * log Z(x)`. Substituting this into the Bradley-Terry loss `-log sigmoid(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l))`, the `beta * log Z(x)` terms are identical for `y_w` and `y_l` (both conditioned on the same `x`) and cancel exactly in the subtraction, leaving a loss expressible purely in terms of policy and reference log-probabilities, with no reward model and no intractable normalizer anywhere:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(policy_logp_chosen, policy_logp_rejected,
             ref_logp_chosen, ref_logp_rejected, beta=0.1):
    """
    Each logp argument is the *sum* of per-token log-probabilities the given model
    assigns to the given response conditioned on the prompt -- not the mean. Using a
    mean instead changes the effective beta in a length-dependent way, a common bug.
    """
    policy_logratio = policy_logp_chosen - policy_logp_rejected
    ref_logratio = ref_logp_chosen - ref_logp_rejected
    logits = beta * (policy_logratio - ref_logratio)
    return -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()
```

One consequence of this derivation worth stating unprompted: differentiating this loss shows the gradient magnitude on each pair is scaled by `sigmoid(-beta * implicit_reward_diff)`, which is large precisely for pairs the policy currently gets wrong (implicitly prefers the rejected response) and small for pairs it already gets right -- an emergent, non-designed property of the loss's derivative, not a hand-crafted weighting scheme, and a good way to demonstrate genuine understanding of the derivation rather than a memorized formula.

## Q6: In the DPO derivation, why does the intractable normalizer `Z(x)` cancel out, and what would go wrong if you tried to compute the reward model implied by a policy directly, without this cancellation trick?

`Z(x) = sum_y pi_ref(y|x) * exp(r(x,y)/beta)` is a sum over every possible response to prompt `x` -- for any nontrivial sequence length this is a sum over an astronomically large space with no closed form and no tractable approximation. It appears identically, as an additive `beta * log Z(x)` term, in the implied reward for both the winning and losing response to the *same* prompt, because both are conditioned on the same `x` and therefore have the same normalizer. Bradley-Terry's loss depends only on the *difference* `r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)`, so this shared additive term cancels exactly regardless of what its actual (unknowable) value is. This is precisely why DPO's loss never needs to compute or approximate `Z(x)` at all -- it structurally never appears in the final expression.

If you tried to compute an absolute reward value for a single response without this cancellation -- for instance, if you wanted to report "how good is this one specific response in an absolute sense" using the identity `r(x,y) = beta*log(pi(y|x)/pi_ref(y|x)) + beta*log Z(x)` -- you would be stuck: you have no way to evaluate `Z(x)` short of summing over (or importance-sampling across) the entire, intractably large response space for that specific prompt. This is exactly the practical content of "reward model scores are only meaningful as per-prompt differences" (File 002, Section 2.2): the log-ratio term is computable and meaningful as a *relative* score across two responses to the same prompt, but is not directly interpretable, without the unknowable `Z(x)` term, as an absolute quality score comparable across different prompts.

## Q7: Explain catastrophic forgetting during SFT mechanistically, and name three concrete mitigations with the specific mechanism by which each one works.

Catastrophic forgetting occurs because SFT and pretraining update literally the same parameters via literally the same kind of gradient descent, with no architectural mechanism protecting broadly-useful pretrained representations from being perturbed by a subsequent, much narrower fine-tuning objective. Pretraining exposes the model to a vast, diverse distribution; SFT datasets are, by comparison, tiny and comparatively narrow in task type, style, and format. Fine-tuning on this narrow distribution, even for a small number of epochs, can shift weights in directions that improve performance on the SFT distribution specifically at the cost of capabilities that were well-represented in pretraining but underrepresented in the SFT set -- degraded few-shot in-context learning, narrowed output diversity, sharper (and thus less well-calibrated) output distributions, and outright knowledge regressions on underrepresented domains are all documented symptoms.

Three concrete mitigations, each with a distinct mechanism: (1) **few epochs and a low learning rate** -- since SFT sets are small enough to memorize within a handful of epochs, keeping the learning rate one-to-two orders of magnitude below pretraining's peak LR and running few epochs ensures the smallest weight perturbation sufficient to instill the target behavior is applied, rather than letting the optimizer aggressively re-carve the loss landscape; (2) **rehearsal / data mixing**, blending a fraction of pretraining-distribution or broader-instruction data into the SFT batch, which acts as a direct regularizer keeping gradients from moving exclusively in the direction the narrow SFT set alone would dictate; (3) **parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA)**, which restricts trainable parameters to a small, low-rank subset, so the vast majority of pretrained weights are literally frozen and cannot be perturbed at all -- a structural, not merely optimization-based, guardrail, traded off against reduced fine-tuning expressiveness relative to full fine-tuning.

## Q8: Scenario -- a product team building a coding-focused assistant tells you they want "zero alignment tax" relative to the base pretrained model's coding-benchmark scores, as a hard launch requirement. How do you respond?

I'd push back on the framing before accepting the requirement literally, because "zero tax" as stated conflates two different things: genuine capability regression, and benchmark-harness measurement artifacts. First step is to actually measure what's driving any observed regression -- re-score with an execution-based or tolerant-equivalence grader rather than naive string matching, since a coding benchmark's naive grading is exactly the kind of exact-match-sensitive measurement that can inflate an apparent regression when the underlying model is producing correct code wrapped in more conversational framing than the base model did. Any regression that survives this correction is a real signal worth taking seriously, and any that doesn't survive it should be explicitly separated out of the "tax" conversation entirely.

For the residual, genuine regression (if any), I'd reframe the requirement itself: this coding assistant's product value is disproportionately driven by raw code correctness relative to a general chat assistant, so it's reasonable to weight the frontier differently -- less alignment-training-driven behavioral shift relative to raw benchmark fidelity than a general-purpose product would tolerate. Concretely: use RLVR (verifiable, test-suite-based reward) rather than RLHF-style preference optimization wherever possible for this specific vertical, since RLVR's reward *is* task correctness by construction, removing the proxy-versus-ground-truth mismatch that drives the classical tax mechanism; apply PPO-ptx-style rehearsal mixing if any preference-optimization stage is used at all; and track the corrected regression continuously across training iterations rather than treating it as a single launch gate, so a small, real tradeoff that buys a genuine, measured safety or instruction-following improvement isn't blocked by a "zero tax" absolute that conflates measurement noise with substance. "Zero tax" is achievable in the narrow, correct sense of "no artifact-driven apparent regression"; it is generally not achievable, and not obviously even desirable, in the sense of "the model behaves identically to the unaligned base model."

## Q9: Implement the GRPO group-relative advantage calculation, and explain why it removes the need for a learned value function.

```python
import torch

def grpo_advantages(rewards, eps=1e-4):
    """
    rewards: tensor of shape (G,), the verifier-assigned scores for G completions
             sampled from the current policy for the *same* prompt.
    Returns: tensor of shape (G,), one scalar advantage per completion.
    """
    mean_r = rewards.mean()
    std_r = rewards.std(unbiased=False)
    return (rewards - mean_r) / (std_r + eps)
```

PPO's advantage estimation ordinarily requires a learned value function `V_psi(s_t)` predicting expected future return, used to convert a sparse, often terminal-only reward into a lower-variance, denser training signal via GAE. Training this value function well is a nontrivial secondary learning problem in its own right -- especially for long chain-of-thought rollouts, where getting a token-level value estimate to accurately predict "expected eventual reward from this partial reasoning trace" is hard, adds real compute cost (a second model or head to train), and is a documented source of instability if it underfits. GRPO's move is to replace this learned baseline with an *empirical* one: sampling `G` completions for the same prompt and using their own reward statistics (mean and standard deviation) as the baseline directly. This works because the group's mean reward is exactly an empirical estimate of "what this policy currently achieves on this prompt" -- precisely the quantity a value function would otherwise be trained to predict -- obtained for free from the rollouts you were already generating, at the cost of needing `G` samples per prompt rather than one, and losing token-level (rather than whole-completion-level) credit assignment, since every token in a given completion receives the identical scalar advantage.

A subtlety worth mentioning unprompted: if all `G` sampled completions receive the same reward (a prompt the current policy either always solves or never solves), `std_r` is 0 and every advantage becomes approximately zero -- correctly signaling that a group with no reward variance carries no useful training gradient, but also meaning GRPO extracts essentially no signal from prompts at the extremes of the current policy's difficulty range, which is a real, practical motivation for curriculum/difficulty-filtering strategies in production RLVR pipelines.

## Q10: What specific source of approximation error does RLVR remove relative to RLHF, and what does it not remove?

RLVR removes the error that comes from the reward signal itself being a *learned, finite-sample-fit approximation* of true quality. In RLHF, the reward model is a supervised model trained by maximum likelihood on a limited set of human (or AI) comparisons, and like any supervised model it is accurate only within, and near, its training distribution; as the policy's output distribution drifts under optimization pressure, the RM's scores on that drifted distribution become progressively less validated and less trustworthy (the true-vs-proxy reward gap in File 002, Section 4.5). RLVR replaces this learned proxy with a deterministic, programmatic verifier -- string/numeric equality against a known-correct answer, or a test suite's pass/fail result -- that has no fitting step and no notion of "in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution": it is exactly as correct evaluating a response today as it would be evaluating any other response, regardless of how far the policy's outputs have drifted stylistically or structurally from any particular reference distribution.

What it does not remove is reward hacking as a category. A verifier can itself have blind spots -- incomplete test coverage that a policy learns to exploit by hard-coding expected outputs, or an answer-only checker that rewards a logically invalid derivation that happens to land on the right final number, or simply not checking properties the reward function was never designed to check at all (DeepSeek-R1-Zero's language-mixing case is the canonical concrete example: nothing in an answer-correctness-only reward penalizes mixing languages mid-reasoning, so nothing in training discourages it). The precise, defensible claim is "RLVR removes the learned-proxy source of reward-hacking risk," not "RLVR removes reward hacking" -- the latter overclaims and is a common, easily-corrected error in casual discussion of this topic.

## Q11: Scenario -- you're told that a pure-RL reasoning model trained with an answer-correctness-only reward has started producing chain-of-thought traces that mix two or three languages mid-derivation, even for monolingual prompts. Diagnose why this happened and propose a fix.

This is a direct, well-documented instance of reward hacking under RLVR, and it's important to be precise about the mechanism rather than treating it as a mysterious bug. The reward function checks only the final extracted answer against ground truth; it has zero mechanism for penalizing or rewarding anything about the intermediate reasoning trace's language, structure, or readability. Under RL optimization pressure, the policy is free to explore and exploit any behavior that doesn't reduce reward, and language-mixing mid-derivation is apparently a pattern the model finds useful for its own internal reasoning process (perhaps because certain concepts or reasoning patterns are more naturally or efficiently represented in a language other than the one the prompt was posed in, given the model's pretraining data) -- and since the reward function is entirely blind to this property, there is no training pressure discouraging it even though it makes the output substantially less usable as a readable artifact.

The fix follows directly from the diagnosis: the reward function needs to explicitly check for, and penalize, the property it was previously blind to. Concretely, add a language-consistency reward term (e.g., a classifier or simple heuristic detecting whether the reasoning trace stays in a single, prompt-consistent language) as an auxiliary component alongside the answer-correctness term, so the combined reward now has a mechanism to discourage the specific undesired behavior. This is exactly the general lesson from this incident, worth generalizing rather than treating as a one-off patch: whatever the reward function does not explicitly check, the policy has no pressure to get right, and will readily trade away for whatever the reward function does check -- any property a team cares about needs to show up in the reward signal, or in a constraint applied during training/sampling, or it should be expected to degrade under sufficient optimization pressure.

## Q12: Precisely distinguish RLAIF from Constitutional AI. Many people conflate these -- what specifically is the relationship?

RLAIF is the general technique of substituting AI-generated judgments for human judgments as the preference-label source feeding any preference-based post-training pipeline -- it says nothing about *how* those labels are then used (they can feed a Bradley-Terry reward model consumed by PPO, or a DPO-style direct loss, or KTO's unpaired-label variant) and nothing about *how* the AI judge is prompted (it could be an unconstrained "which response is better" judgment, with no explicit written standard at all). Constitutional AI is one specific, fully-published, named pipeline that does RLAIF in its second phase, but adds two things RLAIF as a general concept does not require: an explicit, written constitution that the AI judge's comparisons are conditioned on (making the judgment target legible and auditable as text, rather than an implicit, unconstrained model preference), and a first phase (SL-CAI) that has no RLAIF content at all -- it's pure supervised fine-tuning on the model's own self-critique-and-revised responses, with no reward model and no RL anywhere in that phase.

So the precise relationship: every constitutional-AI system's second phase is doing RLAIF; RLAIF as a general technique existed and is used well beyond constitutional-AI-style pipelines (e.g., Lee et al.'s controlled summarization study, which uses AI feedback with no written constitution at all); and a full description of Constitutional AI that only talks about "AI feedback replacing human feedback" while omitting the written-constitution framing and the self-critique-and-revise first phase is missing roughly half of what makes CAI a distinct, citable contribution beyond being "RLAIF applied to harmlessness."

## Q13: Implement the PPO clipped surrogate objective used in RLHF, given policy log-probabilities under the current and rollout-time policies and a precomputed advantage estimate.

```python
import torch

def ppo_clip_loss(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2):
    """
    logp_new: log-probs of the sampled actions (tokens) under the policy currently being updated.
    logp_old: log-probs of the same actions under the policy that generated the rollout
              (held fixed for the duration of the current batch of PPO updates).
    advantages: precomputed advantage estimate per token (e.g., from GAE).
    """
    ratio = torch.exp(logp_new - logp_old)          # probability ratio r_t(theta)
    unclipped = ratio * advantages
    clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * advantages
    # PPO maximizes min(unclipped, clipped); we minimize loss, so negate.
    return -torch.min(unclipped, clipped).mean()
```

The reasoning behind the `min`-of-clipped-and-unclipped construction is worth being able to state, not just the formula: for positive advantage (the sampled action outperformed the baseline), the objective wants to increase `ratio`, but the clip caps the *credit* for doing so once `ratio` exceeds `1 + eps` -- so a single, possibly noisy, very-positive advantage estimate cannot push the policy arbitrarily far from the rollout-time policy in one update. Symmetrically for negative advantage, the penalty for decreasing `ratio` is capped once it drops below `1 - eps`. This is a cheap, first-order approximation to a trust-region constraint (the same underlying goal TRPO pursues via exact, expensive conjugate-gradient machinery), and it's specifically why PPO tolerates being run for several epochs over the same rollout batch without the importance-sampling ratio between current and rollout-time policy becoming unreasonably large -- though running too many such epochs still risks exactly that, which is why "PPO epochs per rollout batch" remains a real, bounded hyperparameter rather than something to push arbitrarily high.

## Q14: DPO can exhibit "likelihood displacement," where training decreases the absolute log-probability of both the chosen and rejected response. Explain why this happens, and describe IPO's fix precisely.

The DPO loss, `-log sigmoid(beta * logit_gap)`, is a monotonically decreasing function of the logit gap with no finite minimizer -- it keeps decreasing as the gap increases without bound, meaning there is no point at which the optimizer is "satisfied" with a given gap and stops pushing further. Because the loss depends only on the *relative* gap between the chosen and rejected response's implicit reward (a function of the log-probability ratio to the reference model), and not on either response's *absolute* probability individually, the optimizer is free to satisfy "make the gap bigger" by any combination of moves that increases the gap -- including decreasing the rejected response's probability faster than the chosen response's, or in some observed cases decreasing both, with mass sometimes redistributing onto completions that were never even in the training data, since nothing in the objective anchors either response's absolute probability at all.

IPO's fix is to replace the unbounded logistic loss with a bounded squared-error loss targeting a specific, finite value for the (beta-scaled) logit gap: `L_IPO = E[(beta * logit_gap - target)^2]`. Because this loss is minimized exactly at `logit_gap = target/beta` and increases quadratically on either side, the optimizer has no incentive to keep pushing the gap toward infinity once the target is reached -- directly removing the unbounded-optimization pathology that causes DPO's likelihood displacement, at the cost of losing DPO's clean gradient-weighting interpretation (where the update is naturally scaled by how wrong the model currently is on a given pair) and introducing a new hyperparameter, the target gap itself, that needs tuning.

## Q15: Scenario -- you have five specialized fine-tunes of the same base model (coding, math, summarization, tool-use, and a refusal/safety fine-tune), and you're asked to combine them into a single model without a full joint retraining run. Walk through your approach and the specific risks you'd check for.

I'd start with task-vector-based merging rather than simple uniform souping, since these are five genuinely different tasks, not five variants of the same task -- souping's uniform-average assumption is designed for same-task checkpoint variance reduction, not for combining distinct capabilities. Concretely: compute each fine-tune's task vector (fine-tuned weights minus shared base weights), then combine them with either a naive weighted sum as a first pass, or, given five vectors with likely varying magnitudes and at least some risk of parameter-level sign conflicts, TIES-merging (trim each vector to its largest-magnitude entries, elect a majority sign per parameter position, average only the agreeing contributions) as the more robust default given more than two or three vectors being combined at once.

Before trusting the result, I'd specifically check three things this scenario is designed to probe: first, whether each fine-tune's task-vector magnitude is comparable, or whether one (plausibly the safety fine-tune, if it involved a more aggressive adaptation) has departed the shared basin further than the others -- if so, uniform weighting under-serves it, and I'd tune per-vector scaling coefficients against held-out per-task evaluation rather than assuming `lambda = 1/5` for everyone. Second, and most important given the mix of task types here: I'd run a dedicated evaluation specifically checking whether the safety/refusal fine-tune's intended behavior survived the merge intact, since sign conflicts between the safety vector and the other four vectors on shared parameters could partially cancel its effect -- a failure mode that would not show up at all in an aggregate coding/math/summarization benchmark score, and is exactly the kind of thing that must be checked explicitly rather than inferred from good-looking aggregate numbers. Third, I'd verify all five checkpoints do in fact share the identical base model, architecture, and tokenizer -- if any of the five diverged (e.g., a different checkpoint lineage), none of these merging techniques apply at all, and distillation would be the correct fallback instead.

## Q16: What specific problem does KTO solve that DPO and IPO don't, and why does it matter practically?

DPO and IPO both require *paired* preference data: for a given prompt, one response explicitly marked better than another, with both responses generated under comparable conditions so the comparison is meaningful. This is a real data-collection constraint -- a large share of naturally-occurring, cheap-to-collect real-world feedback is not paired at all: a thumbs up/down on a single response, whether a user accepted or discarded a single generated suggestion, or other implicit single-response signals. Collecting genuinely paired comparisons (two responses to the identical prompt, judged against each other) requires deliberately generating both candidates and presenting them together for comparison, which is a more expensive and more deliberate data-collection process than simply logging accept/reject signals on whatever single response a user happened to see.

KTO removes this requirement by deriving a loss directly from unpaired binary desirable/undesirable labels on individual `(x, y)` examples, using a batch-level average implicit reward as a stand-in reference point in place of an actual paired comparison, and applying an asymmetric loss motivated by prospect theory (weighting undesirable-labeled losses differently from desirable-labeled gains, reflecting the empirical finding from behavioral economics that human loss-aversion is not symmetric with gain-seeking). Practically, this matters because it lets a team train a DPO-family objective directly on the kind of feedback that is already being generated as a natural byproduct of product usage (accept/reject signals, thumbs up/down), rather than requiring a separate, deliberately-designed paired-comparison collection process -- a meaningful reduction in data-collection cost and complexity for teams whose natural feedback signal is unpaired.

## Q17: Implement a knowledge distillation loss with temperature-scaled soft labels, and explain the `T^2` scaling term.

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

def distillation_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, true_labels, T=4.0, alpha=0.7):
    # Soft-label term: match the teacher's temperature-softened distribution.
    soft_teacher = F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1)
    soft_student = F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1)
    soft_loss = F.kl_div(soft_student, soft_teacher, reduction="batchmean") * (T ** 2)

    # Hard-label term: ordinary supervised cross-entropy against the true label.
    hard_loss = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, true_labels)

    return alpha * soft_loss + (1 - alpha) * hard_loss
```

Temperature scaling (dividing logits by `T > 1` before the softmax) flattens a distribution that would otherwise be sharply peaked around the teacher's top prediction, surfacing the *relative* ranking and confidence the teacher assigned to its non-top choices -- information ("dark knowledge") that is nearly invisible in an unscaled, near-one-hot distribution from a confident, well-trained teacher. The `T^2` factor multiplying the soft-label term is a gradient-scale correction, not an arbitrary constant: the gradient of the temperature-scaled soft cross-entropy term with respect to the student's raw logits scales as `1/T^2`, so multiplying the loss term by `T^2` keeps the soft-label term's gradient magnitude comparable across different choices of `T`, relative to the (temperature-independent) hard-label term. Without this correction, increasing `T` to extract more dark knowledge would simultaneously and unintentionally shrink the soft-label term's effective contribution to the total gradient, silently changing the `alpha` balance the practitioner thought they had fixed.

## Q18: InstructGPT used a 6B reward model even when fine-tuning the 175B policy. What does this finding actually tell you, and would you expect it to still hold today?

The finding is that reward-model size can be decoupled from policy size, and in this specific case, a 175B RM was reported to be *more unstable to train* than the 6B RM, without an accuracy gain large enough to justify that instability and the added inference cost of repeatedly querying a much larger model inside the PPO loop. This is a real, citable counterexample to "bigger is always better" as applied uniformly across every component of an RLHF pipeline -- it says something specific about what a reward model's job actually requires: discriminating relative quality between two responses to the same prompt is a narrower, more specific competency than the policy's job of generating high-quality open-ended text across the full distribution of possible prompts, and that narrower competency apparently does not require matching the policy's parameter count to be captured well.

Whether this exact finding still holds at current frontier scale is genuinely uncertain and under-disclosed -- no major lab has published an equivalently detailed, controlled comparison at, say, a 400B-versus-30B RM scale for a current-generation frontier policy. What is defensible to say: the underlying argument (RM training can be qualitatively less stable at very large scale, and RM competency may not require matching policy scale) is a mechanism-level claim that doesn't obviously stop applying just because absolute scale has grown, but the specific crossover point (what RM size is "big enough," and whether current very-large-scale RM training has since been stabilized by better infrastructure/techniques not available in 2022) is not something I'd claim to know with confidence rather than flag as an open, lab-specific, largely undisclosed detail.

## Q19: Scenario -- after a routine instruction-tuning-data refresh (adding a large batch of new coding examples), your team notices the model has gotten measurably worse at following multi-part instructions ("do X, then Y, formatted as Z"), even though the aggregate held-out loss looks flat or slightly improved. Diagnose.

The flat aggregate loss is the first clue this is a category-specific issue being masked by averaging, not a general capability regression -- exactly the kind of thing an aggregate metric is bad at surfacing. I'd work through two hypotheses in parallel. First, a coverage-dilution hypothesis: check whether the new coding batch, added without explicitly re-normalizing the rest of the mixture's relative shares, inadvertently shrank multi-part-instruction examples' *relative* proportion of the mixture even though their absolute count didn't change -- a common, easy-to-miss consequence of appending a large new batch for one priority category without revisiting the full mixture's proportions. Second, an interference hypothesis: check whether the new coding examples are disproportionately short, single-step tasks (a plausible profile for a "coding examples" batch sourced from, say, short syntax-question data), which could be pulling the model's default response pattern toward shorter, single-step behavior in a way that bleeds into multi-part-instruction prompts specifically, even without any change to that category's own raw example count.

To distinguish these, I'd run a held-out-task-cluster evaluation specifically on multi-part instructions (not folded into an aggregate score), broken out before-and-after the refresh, and separately inspect the actual proportion of multi-part-instruction examples in the old versus new mixture. If the proportion genuinely shrank, the fix is a straightforward re-normalization of the mixture. If the proportion held steady but the regression persists, that points to genuine cross-category interference from the new batch's response-length/style profile, and the fix is either reformatting the new coding examples to be less stylistically dominant, or explicitly reweighting to counteract the interference -- a different fix from the first case, which is exactly why distinguishing coverage dilution from interference, rather than reflexively "adding more multi-part-instruction data," matters here.

## Q20: Explain the alignment tax mechanism, and the specific methodological confound that can make a reported tax look larger than it genuinely is.

The tax exists because preference optimization targets a fundamentally different objective than raw task-benchmark accuracy -- human (or AI) preference judgments reward holistic response quality including tone, format, and conversational framing, none of which a benchmark like SQuAD or HellaSwag's exact-match or log-likelihood scoring rewards at all, and RL/preference optimization's tendency to narrow the model's output distribution toward high-scoring patterns (a mode-seeking pressure, contrasted with pretraining/SFT's more mode-covering objective) can reduce the model's flexibility to produce whatever specific, often terse, output format a given benchmark expects, even when the model's underlying task competence is undiminished.

The methodological confound: many capability benchmarks score via exact-match against a specific expected format (a single word, a short span) or via multiple-choice log-likelihood, and a preference-optimized model's learned tendency toward conversational, explanatory responses can cause an automated grading harness to mark an objectively correct answer wrong purely because it doesn't match the expected terse format -- inflating the apparent tax without reflecting any genuine loss of the underlying capability. A rigorous assessment separates these by re-scoring with a format-tolerant or answer-extraction-based grader before concluding how large the true regression actually is; conflating the naive exact-match delta with genuine capability loss, in either direction, is one of the most common oversimplifications in casual discussion of this topic, and a candidate who raises this confound unprompted is signaling a materially more sophisticated understanding than one who just recites "RLHF hurts benchmarks."
