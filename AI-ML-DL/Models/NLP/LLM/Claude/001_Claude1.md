## Claude 1 (2023)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

Claude 1 was Anthropic's first publicly available model, released in March 2023 (with an earlier limited-access period in 2022 for select partners such as Notion, Quora, and DuckDuckGo). It arrived roughly four months after ChatGPT's public debut, positioning Anthropic as the second frontier lab to ship a broadly usable conversational LLM. Two versions were shipped: **Claude** (the full model) and **Claude Instant** (a smaller, faster, cheaper variant) — the first instance of the tiered product strategy Anthropic would formalize later with the Claude 3 family.

Anthropic was founded in 2021 by a group of former OpenAI researchers (including Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei) explicitly as an **AI safety research company** — the founding thesis was that transformative AI was coming whether or not safety-focused labs built it, and that having a lab racing at the frontier while prioritizing alignment research was safer than ceding frontier capability entirely to labs without that focus. This is not incidental branding: it is the reason Claude 1 was framed publicly around "helpful, honest, and harmless" (HHH) behavior rather than around raw capability benchmarks, and it is the reason Anthropic's technical reports for Claude models read closer to safety papers than architecture papers.

This founding thesis directly explains Anthropic's disclosure policy, which applies to every model in this series and should be stated plainly once here rather than re-litigated per file: Anthropic does not publish parameter counts, layer counts, attention variants, tokenizer details, training compute, or hardware topology for any released Claude model. This is a deliberate policy choice, publicly justified on two grounds Anthropic has articulated in its own posts and papers: (1) architectural and scale disclosures function as a capabilities roadmap that accelerates the field's fastest-moving (and least safety-focused) actors toward the frontier, working against the "safety-focused lab at the frontier" thesis; and (2) Anthropic has historically preferred to compete on and publish about *alignment methodology* (Constitutional AI, RLHF, interpretability, model evaluations) rather than architecture, since that is the axis on which it wants the field's incentives to shift. The practical consequence for anyone studying Claude technically is that there is no leaked or confirmed parameter count, layer count, or attention mechanism for any Claude model as of this writing — everything below that isn't a benchmark score, a stated context length, or a documented alignment technique is inference, and is labeled as such.

Claude 1's core technical antecedent is Anthropic's own research: **Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback** (Bai et al., December 2022), published a few months before Claude 1's public release and foundational to how Claude 1 was aligned. Constitutional AI (CAI) is covered in depth in Section 6; the short version is that it replaced a large fraction of human-labeled harmlessness preference data with AI-generated critiques and preferences derived from a small written set of principles (a "constitution"), making the harmlessness side of RLHF more scalable and more auditable than pure human-feedback-only approaches.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

**Stated plainly: Anthropic has disclosed no architectural details for Claude 1** — no parameter count, no layer count, no attention mechanism, no positional encoding scheme, no tokenizer, no activation function. There is no official technical report analogous to the GPT-3 or PaLM papers that lays out these numbers. Everything in this section is informed inference from public, indirect signals — treat it as such, not as fact.

What can be reasoned about:

- **Decoder-only Transformer as the near-certain family.** Every publicly known frontier LLM from this era (GPT-3, PaLM, LLaMA, Chinchilla) is a decoder-only, causally-masked Transformer trained with a next-token or next-token-adjacent objective. Anthropic's own research output (papers on scaling laws, interpretability work on Transformer circuits) is entirely framed around this architecture family. There is no public signal suggesting Claude 1 departs from it; the overwhelmingly likely inference is a standard decoder-only Transformer, plausibly with mixture-of-experts *not* being used at this stage (MoE only became a plausible inference for frontier labs' *later* models, and there's no indication of it here).
- **Context window as the only hard architectural constraint that is disclosed.** Claude 1 launched with a 9,000-token context window (with Claude Instant matching), later extended. A 9K window is modest by later standards but was competitive with GPT-3.5-era models at the time. A context window of this size is comfortably servable with standard learned or fixed positional embeddings (absolute position embeddings, or an early rotary/ALiBi-style scheme) — it does not, by itself, require anything unusual in positional encoding, unlike Claude 2's jump to 100K (see `002_Claude2.md`).
- **No disclosed multimodality.** Claude 1 is text-only in and text-only out; there is no vision encoder to reason about at this stage, unlike Claude 3 onward.
- **RLHF/CAI reward modeling implies at least two model artifacts around the policy model**: a preference/reward model (trained on comparison data, here substantially AI-generated per Constitutional AI) and the policy model itself, updated via a PPO-style RL step. This is inferred from Anthropic's published Constitutional AI and "Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with RLHF" papers describing the pipeline in the abstract, not from any Claude-1-specific disclosure — but Claude 1 is explicitly the model this pipeline was built to produce.

### 3. Scale — Parameters, Data, Compute

Not disclosed, in any form — no parameter count, no token count for pretraining, no FLOPs figure. Anthropic has never published a scale figure for Claude 1 comparable to OpenAI's "175B" for GPT-3 or Google's disclosed PaLM sizes.

The only informed inference available at this stage comes from the **Claude vs. Claude Instant product split** and pricing at launch: Claude Instant was priced and marketed as materially faster and cheaper than the full Claude model, with the tradeoff being somewhat lower quality on complex reasoning tasks. A large latency and price gap between two models in the same family, offered as a deliberate choice rather than a hardware accident, is generally consistent with (though not proof of) a meaningfully smaller parameter count and/or a distillation relationship for the "Instant" tier — this is the same tiering logic Anthropic would later make explicit and public with Haiku/Sonnet/Opus. At the Claude 1 stage, though, Anthropic said nothing about *how* Instant achieved its speed (smaller dense model, distillation, more aggressive quantization, or serving-side optimizations are all consistent with the observed behavior, and there is no way to distinguish between them from public information).

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

Anthropic has not published training-cluster details for Claude 1: no accelerator type, no cluster size, no parallelism strategy, no training duration. What can be said is generic to any lab training a GPT-3-class model in 2022–2023, not specific to Anthropic:

- Training a model at this capability tier in this era required a large cluster of accelerators (GPUs, at the time predominantly NVIDIA A100-class parts across the industry; TPUs were a Google-specific option Anthropic would not have had native access to before its later compute partnerships) sustained over weeks to months.
- At minimum, **data parallelism** (sharding batches across replicas) is required at any nontrivial scale, and once a single model's parameters and optimizer state exceed single-accelerator memory, **tensor parallelism** (sharding individual matrix multiplies across devices) and/or **pipeline parallelism** (sharding layers across devices) become necessary, typically combined with **ZeRO-style optimizer-state sharding** (as in DeepSpeed or FSDP) to keep memory per device tractable. Whether Claude 1 in fact required tensor/pipeline parallelism (versus fitting comfortably with data parallelism plus optimizer-state sharding) depends entirely on its parameter count, which is undisclosed — so this is presented as a generic requirement for models "at this capability tier," not a confirmed fact about Claude 1's training job.
- Checkpointing, mid-run failure recovery, and loss-spike/instability mitigation (e.g., restart from a healthy checkpoint after a divergence, gradient clipping, careful learning-rate warmup) are standard practice for any run of this duration on this much hardware, mentioned in the general LLM-training literature of the period (e.g., OPT's and BLOOM's public logbooks document this kind of instability explicitly) — Anthropic has not published an equivalent logbook for Claude 1.
- Anthropic's compute has been publicly reported (in press coverage, not Anthropic technical papers) to come primarily from major cloud/hardware partners, most notably Google Cloud in this early period, with the Amazon and later, much larger, Google/AWS investments coming after Claude 1. This is a corporate/financial fact, not a technical disclosure of the training run itself.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

Not disclosed in composition or size. Anthropic has never published a data mixture breakdown, a token count, or a cutoff date methodology for Claude 1 comparable to, e.g., GPT-3's documented five-source mixture. The safe, generic inference is that pretraining used a standard **causal (autoregressive) next-token prediction objective** over a large corpus of predominantly web text, plausibly supplemented with books, code, and reference text — this mirrors every other frontier LLM of the era and is consistent with Claude 1's demonstrated ability to write code, follow multi-step instructions, and produce fluent long-form prose, but Anthropic has not confirmed a specific mixture, a specific filtering methodology, or a specific training-token count.

What is known with more confidence, because it is a *behavioral* claim Anthropic has made publicly, is a knowledge cutoff: Claude 1's knowledge was understood to extend to roughly early-to-mid 2021 at launch, consistent with an already fairly old pretraining snapshot even at release time — itself a small data point suggesting substantial lead time between data collection/pretraining and public release.

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

This is the section where Claude 1 is most documented, because Constitutional AI and the HHH framing are Anthropic's own published research contribution and the central technical story of this model. Two papers matter here: **"A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment"** (Askell et al., 2021, which introduced and operationalized the helpful/honest/harmless framing Anthropic uses to this day) and **"Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback"** (Bai et al., December 2022, which Claude 1 is the direct product of).

**The HHH framing.** Anthropic defines a good assistant response along three axes: *helpful* (does what the user actually wants, is responsive to intent, doesn't hedge unnecessarily), *honest* (doesn't state falsehoods, expresses calibrated uncertainty, doesn't deceive), and *harmless* (doesn't help with harmful requests, doesn't produce content that causes real-world harm). These three properties are in genuine tension — a maximally cautious model is harmless but not helpful, and Anthropic's stated research goal has always been to find training methods that push the Pareto frontier of all three simultaneously rather than trading one off for another. Claude 1's public persona ("helpful, honest, and harmless") is a direct, literal restatement of this internal research framing turned into a product description.

**Constitutional AI, mechanically, in two phases:**

- **Phase 1 — Supervised learning via self-critique and revision (SL-CAI).** Start from a helpful-only model (already instruction-tuned, e.g., via standard SFT on human demonstrations, to be a capable assistant but without harmlessness training yet). For a set of prompts designed to elicit harmful, unethical, or otherwise undesirable completions, sample an initial response from this model. Then, prompt the *same model* to critique its own response against one of a small set of natural-language principles drawn from a written "constitution" (e.g., a principle like "choose the response that is least likely to be viewed as harmful or offensive to a non-western audience," or one invoking values like avoiding deception or unnecessary judgmentalism). The model produces a critique of its own output, then produces a revision that addresses the critique. This critique-then-revise step can be applied repeatedly (chaining multiple principles/revisions). The resulting (prompt, revised-response) pairs are then used as supervised fine-tuning data — the model is fine-tuned to directly produce the *revised* response given the original prompt, without needing the critique step at inference time. This phase alone measurably reduces harmful outputs relative to the helpful-only starting point, and crucially requires **zero additional human harmlessness labels** — the supervisory signal comes from the model applying written principles to itself.
- **Phase 2 — Reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RL-CAI).** Starting from the SL-CAI model, generate pairs of responses to new (typically red-teaming-style) prompts. Instead of asking human raters which response is better/less harmful (the standard RLHF harmlessness-labeling step), prompt a separate LLM instance (a "feedback model") to compare the pair against the same constitutional principles and produce a preference label — effectively **AI feedback replacing human feedback** for the harmlessness axis specifically (helpfulness preference data in this pipeline was still substantially human-labeled). These AI-generated preference pairs train a preference/reward model exactly as human preference pairs would in standard RLHF, and that reward model is then used to fine-tune the policy via reinforcement learning (Anthropic's papers describe this as broadly PPO-style RL against the learned reward model, consistent with contemporaneous RLHF practice). The name "RL-CAI" is Anthropic's own term for this AI-feedback RL stage.

**Why this design, and what it buys:** Human labeling of harmful content is expensive, slow, psychologically taxing for annotators, and produces preference data whose quality depends heavily on rater guidelines and consistency. By anchoring both the SL and RL stages to an explicit, inspectable, natural-language constitution rather than implicit rater judgment, Anthropic gets three things simultaneously: (1) **scalability** — AI-generated critiques and preferences can be produced far faster and cheaper than human ones once you have a capable-enough helpful model to generate them; (2) **transparency/auditability** — because the constitution is a written document rather than an implicit pattern in thousands of anonymous human ratings, it is possible to inspect, debate, and revise *which values* the model is being pushed toward, which is a distinctly different (and, Anthropic argues, more accountable) knob than "hire different raters"; (3) **reduced human exposure to harmful content** during the labeling process itself, since AI feedback substitutes for a large share of what would otherwise be human harmlessness annotation. The tradeoff, which Anthropic's own paper is explicit about, is that the harmlessness ceiling is now partly bounded by how well the feedback model itself can apply the constitution — RLAIF inherits any blind spots or biases of the model doing the critiquing, and there is an obvious bootstrapping question (is the critiquing model itself well-aligned enough to be a good judge?) that later interpretability and evaluation work is partly aimed at addressing.

Claude 1 is the first shipped product built on this pipeline (helpful-SFT base, then SL-CAI, then RL-CAI), rather than a hypothetical description — Anthropic has stated this framing publicly in connection with Claude's alignment approach, even without disclosing the base model's architecture or scale.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

- **Constitutional AI itself** — the central contribution, covered in depth above: replacing a large share of human harmlessness-preference labeling with model self-critique (SL-CAI) and model-vs-model AI feedback (RL-CAI) anchored to a written, revisable set of principles.
- **The HHH decomposition** as a concrete, operationalized alignment target (from the earlier Askell et al. 2021 "general language assistant" paper), giving Anthropic's later evaluation and red-teaming work a stable rubric to measure against rather than a vague "be good" objective.
- **Explicit framing of harmlessness training as a red-teaming/adversarial-prompt problem**, i.e., deliberately constructing prompts designed to elicit unsafe behavior as the training signal source for the constitutional critique-and-revise loop, rather than relying solely on naturally occurring harmful prompts in a general instruction-tuning set.
- **A public, if partial, model welfare and disclosure posture** distinct from competitors at the time — Anthropic published the CAI methodology in enough mechanical detail (the paper includes the actual constitutional principles used) that outside researchers could inspect and critique the *values* being trained for, which was a novel level of transparency about alignment methodology specifically (as opposed to architecture, which remained undisclosed).

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

Because architecture and infra are undisclosed, this section is necessarily about the alignment-pipeline challenges Anthropic *has* discussed, rather than systems-engineering challenges Anthropic hasn't:

- **Bootstrapping the critique model.** SL-CAI requires a model already capable enough to meaningfully critique its own harmful outputs against abstract principles — a model too weak to understand "is this response likely to be read as condescending" cannot productively self-revise. Anthropic's published ablations show critique-and-revision quality scaling with model capability, meaning CAI as a technique is itself capability-gated, not a free harmlessness improvement applicable to arbitrarily small models.
- **Constitution design and coverage.** Writing a finite set of natural-language principles that generalizes well across the enormous space of possible harmful prompts is itself an open-ended design problem; Anthropic's constitution mixes fairly abstract principles (drawing on sources like the UN Declaration of Human Rights) with more concrete behavioral instructions, and the paper is explicit that this is an evolving artifact, not a solved specification.
- **Helpfulness/harmlessness tradeoff drift.** A known failure mode of harmlessness-focused RL (whether AI- or human-feedback-driven) is the model becoming needlessly evasive or refusing benign requests that superficially resemble unsafe ones — sometimes called "overcautiousness" or evasiveness in Anthropic's own later evaluation writing. Balancing this against genuine harm-avoidance is described as an explicit ongoing tension in Anthropic's alignment work rather than something CAI fully solved with Claude 1.
- **No public information exists on infrastructure-level training challenges** (loss spikes, hardware failures, cluster scaling issues) for Claude 1 specifically, unlike some other labs' training retrospectives (e.g., Meta's LLaMA/OPT logs, or BLOOM's public logbook) — this is itself consistent with Anthropic's general non-disclosure posture on training-systems engineering.

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

Claude 1 was served exclusively through Anthropic's own API and through a small number of launch partners' products (Notion, Quora's Poe, DuckDuckGo, Robin AI) — there was no open-weights release and no on-premises deployment option at this stage, which is a serving-architecture decision as much as a business one: it means every inference request runs on Anthropic-controlled infrastructure, which in turn is a prerequisite for the kind of usage monitoring and misuse-detection Anthropic's safety posture depends on (a closed-API deployment is much easier to monitor for abuse than distributed open weights).

The Claude / Claude Instant split is itself a serving-cost decision as much as a product one: offering a materially cheaper, faster tier lets Anthropic serve high-volume, latency-sensitive, or cost-sensitive use cases (e.g., simple classification, short completions) without forcing every request through the most expensive model, which is the same economic logic that later, more explicitly, produced the three-tier Claude 3 family. No public details exist on Claude 1's actual serving stack (batching strategy, quantization, hardware target) — inference-time engineering specifics were not disclosed at this stage any more than training specifics were.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

Anthropic's public positioning of Claude 1 leaned on qualitative/product framing (safety, helpfulness, tone) more than a heavy benchmark push relative to how OpenAI marketed GPT-3.5/4 at the time, though Claude was reported at launch to be competitive with contemporaneous GPT-3.5-class models on standard academic benchmarks (e.g., MMLU-style multi-task evaluation) without claiming to exceed GPT-4, which had launched shortly before Claude 1 and was understood at the time to be the stronger model on raw capability.

**Known limitations, as understood at the time:** a knowledge cutoff of roughly early/mid-2021 leaving it unaware of more recent events; a 9,000-token context window that was generous for the era but small by later standards; no vision or other modality input; a documented tendency (shared with essentially every RLHF/RLAIF-trained assistant of this generation) toward occasional over-refusal on benign-but-sensitive-sounding prompts, a direct manifestation of the helpfulness/harmlessness tension discussed in Section 6; and, like all LLMs of this era, susceptibility to hallucination — confident generation of incorrect facts or citations — which Constitutional AI's honesty-oriented principles targeted but did not eliminate.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**Confirmed (publicly stated by Anthropic or directly observable):**
- Released March 2023 (limited partner access in 2022); two variants, Claude and Claude Instant.
- Built on the HHH (helpful, honest, harmless) framing from Askell et al. 2021.
- Aligned using Constitutional AI (Bai et al., Dec. 2022) — SL-CAI (self-critique-and-revise supervised phase) followed by RL-CAI (RL against an AI-feedback-trained preference model), as described mechanically in Section 6.
- Context window of 9,000 tokens at launch.
- Text-only input and output; no vision or other modality.
- Anthropic founded 2021 as an AI-safety-focused research company; this is the stated rationale for its architecture/scale non-disclosure policy.
- Served only via Anthropic's API and select launch partners; no open weights.

**Speculation / informed inference (explicitly not confirmed by Anthropic):**
- Any specific parameter count, layer count, attention mechanism, positional encoding scheme, or tokenizer.
- Any specific pretraining data composition, mixture weighting, or total token count.
- Any specific training cluster size, accelerator type, or parallelism strategy.
- Whether Claude Instant is a smaller dense model, a distilled model, or otherwise related to full Claude — the pricing/speed gap is observed; the mechanism producing it is not disclosed.
- Any total training compute (FLOPs) figure.

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

- Be able to state, without hedging, that Anthropic discloses no architecture or scale for any Claude model, and explain *why* in terms of the founding safety thesis — this is a substantive fact about the company's strategy, not a gap in your knowledge.
- Be able to walk through Constitutional AI mechanically, phase by phase (SL-CAI critique-and-revise; RL-CAI AI-feedback preference modeling and RL), and articulate precisely what problem it solves relative to pure human-feedback RLHF: scalability of harmlessness labeling, auditability of the value specification via an explicit written constitution, and reduced human exposure to harmful content during labeling.
- Be able to state the known bootstrapping limitation of CAI honestly: self-critique quality is gated by the critiquing model's own capability and alignment, so CAI is not a free harmlessness improvement independent of the underlying model's quality — this is a legitimate critique interviewers may probe, and hand-waving past it is a worse answer than acknowledging it directly.
- Be able to connect the helpful/honest/harmless framing to the well-known "alignment tax" / overcautious-refusal failure mode as a real, still-unsolved tension rather than a solved problem — this shows you understand alignment as an ongoing multi-objective optimization, not a checkbox.
- Understand the Claude/Claude Instant split as the earliest instance of Anthropic's now-standard multi-tier serving strategy, and be able to reason about *why* a lab would ship two sizes rather than one even before it became the explicit three-tier strategy of Claude 3.
