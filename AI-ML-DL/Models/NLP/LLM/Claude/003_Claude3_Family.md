## Claude 3 Family (2024)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

Claude 3 launched in March 2024 as a family of three models released simultaneously — **Haiku** (fastest, cheapest), **Sonnet** (balanced), and **Opus** (most capable, most expensive) — rather than as a single model, formalizing and publicly naming the tiering strategy that had existed only informally as the Claude/Claude Instant split in the Claude 1 and 2 generations. All three models share the same publicly stated context window (200,000 tokens standard, with Anthropic noting up to 1 million tokens available to select customers for some tier/version combinations later in the family's life) and, critically, this is **the first Claude generation with vision input** — all three models can accept images (photos, charts, diagrams, screenshots, handwritten notes) alongside text and reason over them jointly.

The three-tier naming (Haiku/Sonnet/Opus, a poetry-form metaphor: short and efficient, structured and versatile, and the most elaborate form, respectively) is itself worth treating as a genuine product/research decision rather than just a marketing flourish, and this file's Section 12 develops the reasoning in more depth. The short version: no single model is Pareto-optimal across the joint objective of (capability, latency, cost) that real deployments actually care about. A customer building a high-volume, latency-sensitive classification pipeline and a customer building an agentic research assistant that reasons over long documents have fundamentally different points on that frontier they want to sit at, and serving both well from one fixed-size model means either overpaying (running Opus-class compute for Haiku-shaped tasks) or under-delivering (forcing complex reasoning through a small, fast model that can't do it well). Releasing three explicitly-differentiated sizes simultaneously, with transparent relative pricing, lets Anthropic capture both ends of that frontier and — as importantly — lets *customers* do their own routing rather than Anthropic guessing at a single one-size-fits-all operating point.

Claude 3 was also the release where Anthropic first started publishing head-to-head benchmark comparisons against GPT-4 and Gemini 1.0 Ultra with Opus specifically positioned as competitive with or exceeding those models on several standard academic and reasoning benchmarks at launch — a more assertive competitive-benchmark posture than Anthropic had taken with Claude 1 or 2.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

**Anthropic disclosed no architecture details for any Claude 3 model** — no parameter counts for Haiku, Sonnet, or Opus individually, no layer counts, no attention mechanism, no confirmation of whether the three tiers are three independently-trained dense models, three sizes of one distilled/scaled family, or something else entirely. This is the same policy discussed in `001_Claude1.md` Section 1, applied across three simultaneously-released models rather than one, which if anything makes the non-disclosure more conspicuous: even the *relative* relationship between the three tiers (same architecture at three scales? different architectures entirely?) is not confirmed.

**What can be reasoned about, clearly labeled as inference:**

- **Vision input requires some image-encoder-to-language-model integration**, and every publicly known approach to this in 2023–2024 followed one of a small number of patterns: (a) a separate pretrained vision encoder (commonly a ViT-style architecture) whose output patch/token embeddings are projected into the language model's embedding space and concatenated with text token embeddings before the shared decoder processes both jointly (the pattern used by, e.g., LLaVA-style academic models and, by most public technical framing, GPT-4V), or (b) a more tightly co-trained multimodal architecture where image and text tokens are treated symmetrically from an earlier stage. Anthropic has not confirmed which pattern Claude 3 uses, nor confirmed a vision encoder architecture, patch size, or image tokenization scheme (e.g., how many "tokens" a given image resolution consumes, though Anthropic's documentation does state practical guidance like recommended image sizes and a rough token-cost-per-image relationship for API pricing purposes — a product-level disclosure, not an architectural one).
- **All three tiers sharing the same stated 200K context window** is itself informative: it suggests the KV-cache-and-positional-encoding engineering discussed in `002_Claude2.md` (Sections 2 and 9) was solved once, at the family level, rather than re-solved per tier — consistent with, though not proof of, the three tiers sharing substantial architectural lineage rather than being unrelated models that happen to share a marketing window size.
- **No confirmation of mixture-of-experts (MoE) or any other sparse-activation scheme** for any tier. By 2024, MoE was a publicly known technique used by at least one other frontier lab for cost-efficient scaling (Mixtral's release and public commentary about GPT-4 possibly using an MoE-style architecture were both circulating by this point), making it a plausible technique *in the abstract* for a lab wanting to serve a fast, cheap Haiku tier — but this is pattern-matching against industry trends, not evidence about Claude 3 specifically, and is flagged here explicitly as unconfirmed speculation, not inference with real evidentiary support.
- **Decoder-only Transformer backbone remains the near-certain baseline assumption**, unchanged from Claude 1/2 reasoning, since there is no public signal of a fundamental departure.

### 3. Scale — Parameters, Data, Compute

Not disclosed for any of the three tiers, individually or relatively. Anthropic has never confirmed parameter counts for Haiku, Sonnet, or Opus.

The most concrete informed inference available comes directly from Anthropic's own published **pricing and stated relative-speed positioning**, which is a real, disclosed data point even though it doesn't translate to an exact parameter count:

- At launch, Anthropic's API pricing (publicly listed, not speculative) placed Haiku as by far the cheapest and fastest per-token, Sonnet in the middle, and Opus as the most expensive, with the price ratios across tiers spanning roughly an order of magnitude or more from Haiku to Opus on a per-token basis (exact published figures have changed over time with subsequent price adjustments and should be checked against Anthropic's current pricing page rather than treated as fixed; the qualitative ordering and the roughly order-of-magnitude spread is the durable point).
- A sustained, large, *designed* price and latency spread across models marketed explicitly around a capability tradeoff (Anthropic's own materials describe Haiku as targeting "near-instant" responses for simple tasks and Opus as targeting the most complex reasoning and analysis) is consistent with real differences in either parameter count, activated compute per token (if any tier uses sparsity/MoE), or both. This remains an inference from pricing/latency behavior, not a confirmed architectural fact — the same epistemic caveat as Claude 1/2's Instant-vs-full reasoning, just with three points on the curve now instead of two.
- Anthropic's own qualitative positioning statements (e.g., describing Opus's benchmark performance as at or near the frontier for its release window, and Haiku's as strong for its speed/cost class rather than for its absolute capability) are themselves a form of relative-scale signal, even without numbers attached.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

Not disclosed for any Claude 3 tier. Beyond the generic distributed-training considerations already discussed for Claude 1/2 (data/tensor/pipeline parallelism as needed, optimizer-state sharding, multi-week+ runs, checkpointing for fault tolerance), releasing three tiers simultaneously raises one additional generic infrastructure question worth naming explicitly: **whether the three tiers were trained as genuinely independent runs, or whether smaller tiers were produced via some form of distillation from a larger, already-trained teacher (e.g., Opus), or via pruning/scaling-down of a shared architecture.** All three are established, publicly documented industry techniques in 2024 for producing a family of models at different scales without paying the full independent-pretraining cost three times over; Anthropic has not confirmed using any of them for Claude 3 specifically, and none should be asserted as fact. Anthropic has separately disclosed (in later public statements, e.g. regarding its 2024 compute partnerships) substantially expanded compute access via Amazon and Google around this period, which is a corporate/financial fact consistent with training three frontier-class models in the same generation, but again says nothing about the specific training recipe used for any one of them.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

Not disclosed in composition or size for any tier. The addition of vision input is the one concrete, disclosed capability change with a clear data-side implication: training a model to reason jointly over images and text requires a training corpus containing substantial paired image-text data (captioned images, documents with embedded figures/charts, screenshots with associated descriptions, OCR-style text-in-image data, and so on) in addition to whatever text-only corpus underlies the language-modeling objective — this is a structural necessity of any multimodal LLM regardless of the specific model, not a Claude-3-specific disclosure. Anthropic has not published the composition, size, or sourcing of this multimodal data for Claude 3. The underlying language-modeling objective is presumed, as with Claude 1/2, to be standard causal next-token prediction, now presumably extended so that image-derived tokens/embeddings are part of the same sequence the causal objective is computed over (the specific mechanism for how image content participates in that loss — e.g., whether there is a captioning-style auxiliary loss on image tokens specifically, versus image tokens purely serving as context for predicting subsequent text tokens — is not disclosed).

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

Claude 3's alignment approach is understood to be a continuation and refinement of the Constitutional AI-based pipeline established with Claude 1 (see `001_Claude1.md` Section 6 for the full mechanical description of SL-CAI and RL-CAI, not repeated here), now applied across three model sizes and extended to cover multimodal inputs — i.e., the harmlessness and honesty evaluation surface now includes image-based prompts (e.g., a request accompanying an image that would be benign as text alone but concerning combined with the image, or vice versa), which is a genuinely new alignment surface relative to Claude 1/2's text-only scope, though Anthropic has not published a detailed multimodal-specific extension of the constitution comparable in detail to the original 2022 CAI paper's text-only principles.

Anthropic's Claude 3 model card (a public document, more detailed than prior release announcements) discusses evaluation across categories including harmlessness, honesty and calibration, and notably highlights **reduced unnecessary refusals** relative to Claude 2 as an explicit, named improvement — directly addressing the overcautious-refusal failure mode flagged as an open tension in `001_Claude1.md` Section 6 and `002_Claude2.md`. This is a meaningful public data point: it indicates Anthropic's internal alignment work between Claude 2 and Claude 3 specifically targeted the helpfulness side of the HHH tradeoff (reducing false-positive refusals on benign prompts) rather than only continuing to push harmlessness further, suggesting an explicit attempt to move along the Pareto frontier discussed conceptually in Section 6 of the Claude 1 file rather than trading one property for another.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

- **The three-tier simultaneous release itself** is the primary structural novelty of this generation from a strategy-of-research-and-deployment standpoint — treated by Anthropic as a first-class product architecture decision, not just three separately-scheduled releases, and this file's Section 12 treats the decision itself as worth defending on its research/business merits.
- **First disclosed vision capability** across an entire Claude generation simultaneously (not staged one tier at a time), meaning the multimodal training and evaluation methodology had to be validated across three different capacity points at once.
- **The Claude 3 model card's explicit "reduced unnecessary refusals" framing** is itself a small methodological contribution worth naming: it is a public commitment to measuring and reporting the over-refusal failure mode as a tracked metric, not just capability benchmarks, which was not as prominent a public framing in Claude 1/2 materials.
- No fundamentally new alignment *technique* (comparable in novelty to Constitutional AI's original introduction) was publicly announced with Claude 3 — the alignment story here is continuity and extension (to multimodal inputs, to three tiers, with quantified refusal-rate improvement) rather than a new mechanism.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

Not disclosed specifically. The generic challenges worth naming, given what changed in this generation:

- **Multimodal alignment surface expansion.** Extending harmlessness/honesty evaluation to image+text joint prompts multiplies the space of possible unsafe combinations relative to text alone (an image can recontextualize an otherwise-benign piece of text, and vice versa), which is a genuinely harder red-teaming and evaluation problem than the purely textual case Claude 1/2 faced — Anthropic's model card discusses multimodal-specific evaluation categories, though without disclosing the underlying training-data or critique-generation mechanics used to address it.
- **Maintaining consistent behavioral/safety properties across three simultaneously-released model sizes** is itself a nontrivial engineering and evaluation challenge distinct from single-model alignment: a technique or piece of red-teaming data that reliably steers a large model's behavior does not automatically transfer with equal effectiveness to a much smaller, faster model with less capacity, meaning the alignment pipeline plausibly had to be validated (and possibly tuned) per tier rather than applied once and assumed to generalize — this is a reasonable generic inference from the fact of a simultaneous multi-tier release, not a confirmed Anthropic statement about their internal process.
- **No public logbook-style disclosure exists** (loss curves, hardware failure rates, training-run timelines) for any Claude 3 tier, consistent with Anthropic's ongoing non-disclosure of training-systems engineering specifics.

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

The three-tier structure is, from a serving standpoint, best understood as Anthropic productizing a **model-routing decision** that would otherwise have to be made implicitly by over-provisioning a single model for the hardest tasks in a workload. This file's accompanying Python implementation (`003_Claude3_Family.py`) builds a small, explicit `ModelRouter` class that mirrors the decision problem a real deployment or an agentic system faces: given a task's estimated complexity (and optionally its latency/cost sensitivity), pick the cheapest tier that is still likely to succeed, rather than defaulting every request to the most capable and most expensive option.

This pattern — cheap-fast triage model handling the bulk of a workload, escalating only the subset of requests that need it to a slower, more expensive model — is a serving-cost optimization independent of any specific undisclosed architecture detail, and is exactly the kind of routing logic real production systems built on top of tiered model families implement (sometimes literally, sometimes via a "try the small model first, only call the big one if the small one signals low confidence or fails a check" cascade). Anthropic's own API supports this pattern directly by exposing all three tiers as separate, individually addressable model identifiers with transparent, stable per-tier pricing, rather than a single endpoint with an opaque internal routing decision — putting the routing choice under the caller's control, which matters for cost predictability and for debuggability (an application team can reason about exactly which tier handled a given request).

The 200K-token context window shared across all three tiers means the KV-cache scaling analysis from `002_Claude2.md` Section 9 applies to each tier independently, at whatever its (undisclosed) per-tier architecture actually is — meaning Haiku serving a near-200K-token request still carries a KV-cache memory cost linear in that sequence length, just presumably at a smaller per-layer/per-head footprint than Opus would for the same request, consistent with Haiku's design goal of being usable at high throughput.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

Anthropic's Claude 3 model card publishes comparative benchmark tables across Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus (and against GPT-4, Gemini 1.0 Ultra, and Claude 2.1) spanning: **MMLU** (undergraduate-level multi-subject knowledge/reasoning), **GPQA** (graduate-level science reasoning, a harder and more contamination-resistant benchmark than MMLU), **GSM8K** and **MATH** (grade-school and competition mathematics), **HumanEval** (Python code generation), and multilingual and reading-comprehension benchmarks, along with early vision-specific benchmarks (e.g., chart/document/diagram understanding tasks) reflecting the new modality.

The consistent, disclosed pattern across these tables is **monotonic ordering: Opus > Sonnet > Haiku on essentially every reported capability benchmark**, with Opus at launch reported by Anthropic as matching or exceeding GPT-4 and Gemini 1.0 Ultra on a number of these benchmarks — a genuinely assertive claim relative to Claude 2's more modest competitive framing. Haiku, despite being the smallest/fastest/cheapest tier, was positioned by Anthropic as strong *for its class* — outperforming some competitors' smaller/faster models — rather than simply being "the worst model," reinforcing that the tiers are meant to be evaluated against differently-priced competitor tiers, not against each other in isolation.

**Known limitations as understood at the time:** despite reduced-refusal-rate claims, over-refusal on ambiguous prompts remained a live issue (as it does across the industry generally, not uniquely for Claude 3); vision capability, while new, was understood to have the general limitations shared across contemporaneous multimodal LLMs — weaker performance on fine-grained spatial reasoning, precise counting, and dense small-text OCR in images relative to larger, cleaner inputs; and hallucination, while continually reduced generation over generation per Anthropic's own reporting, was not eliminated at any tier.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**Confirmed (publicly stated by Anthropic or directly observable):**
- Released March 2024 as three simultaneous tiers: Haiku, Sonnet, Opus.
- First Claude generation with vision (image) input, across all three tiers.
- 200,000-token context window standard across the family (with limited, later access to a 1-million-token window for some configurations, per Anthropic's own subsequent announcements).
- Anthropic-published comparative benchmark tables (MMLU, GPQA, GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, and others) showing monotonic Opus > Sonnet > Haiku ordering, with Opus reported competitive with or exceeding GPT-4/Gemini 1.0 Ultra on several benchmarks at launch.
- Anthropic-stated reduction in unnecessary/over-cautious refusals relative to Claude 2, published in the Claude 3 model card.
- Transparent, differentiated per-tier API pricing, publicly listed, with Haiku cheapest/fastest and Opus most expensive/most capable.
- Continuation of the Constitutional-AI-based alignment approach from Claude 1/2, now covering multimodal inputs.

**Speculation / informed inference (explicitly not confirmed by Anthropic):**
- Any parameter count for any of the three tiers, or their relative parameter-count ratios.
- Whether the three tiers are independently trained, distilled from one another, or otherwise architecturally related.
- Whether any tier uses mixture-of-experts or another sparse-activation scheme.
- The specific vision-encoder architecture, image tokenization scheme, or image-to-language integration method.
- Any training data composition, size, or FLOPs figures for any tier.
- Any specific multimodal-alignment training technique beyond the general continuation of Constitutional AI implied by Anthropic's public materials.
