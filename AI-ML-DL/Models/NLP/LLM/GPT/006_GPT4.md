## GPT-4 (2023)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

GPT-4 was released by OpenAI on March 14, 2023, first to ChatGPT Plus subscribers and API waitlist customers, then broadly through 2023. It is the first GPT-series model OpenAI shipped with essentially zero public disclosure of architecture, parameter count, training compute, or training dataset composition -- a deliberate break from GPT-2/GPT-3's relatively open technical reports. The GPT-4 Technical Report (arXiv:2303.08774) states this explicitly: OpenAI chose not to disclose "further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, training method, or similar" citing "the competitive landscape and the safety implications of large-scale models."

Strategically, GPT-4 marks the moment the GPT line shifted from research artifact to deployed product platform. It shipped simultaneously with: a multimodal (text+image) input capability, a formal 60+ page "System Card" documenting safety evaluation and red-teaming, integration into Microsoft Bing Chat (reportedly running a GPT-4-class model months before the public announcement, under the codename "Prometheus"), and a tiered context-length product (8K and 32K token variants at different price points). This is also the point at which OpenAI began explicitly competing on standardized professional/academic exams as a marketing and evaluation strategy -- simulated Bar Exam, LSAT, GRE, AP exams, and olympiad-level science and math problems -- signaling a positioning shift toward "AI as credentialed professional capability" rather than purely a language-modeling benchmark story (MMLU, HellaSwag, etc., still reported, but exam framing dominated the launch narrative).

The competitive context matters for interpretation: GPT-4 launched a few months after ChatGPT's explosive consumer adoption, amid intensifying capital investment across Google (PaLM, later Gemini), Anthropic (Claude), and a wave of open-weight efforts (LLaMA leaked the same month). Non-disclosure of architecture should be read partly as a response to this environment, not merely a safety stance.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

**Nothing about GPT-4's architecture is officially confirmed beyond**: it is multimodal (accepts text and image inputs, produces text outputs), it is available in at least two context-length configurations (8,192 and 32,768 tokens), and it is presumed -- by strong inference from the entire GPT lineage and from behavior (autoregressive generation, causal attention artifacts, tokenizer continuity) -- to be a decoder-only, causal-attention Transformer trained with a next-token prediction objective. OpenAI's technical report contains no architecture diagram, no layer count, no hidden dimension, no attention-head count, and no statement about positional encoding scheme (whether learned absolute embeddings as in GPT-2/3, or a rotary/ALiBi-style relative scheme).

**The image-input pathway** is functionally described (users can attach images and ask questions about them) but not architecturally described. The plausible design space, based on contemporaneous multimodal LM research (Flamingo, PaLI, LLaVA-style approaches published around the same period), is: a separately trained vision encoder (e.g., a ViT-style patch encoder) that produces a fixed or variable number of visual embeddings, which are then linearly projected into the LLM's token embedding space and spliced into the input sequence alongside text token embeddings, with the decoder-only stack attending over the combined sequence causally. This is a reasonable architectural guess grounded in how the field solved this problem elsewhere -- it is not a claim about GPT-4 specifically. Note that GPT-4's image capability was announced at launch but not made broadly available until months later, consistent with a bolted-on or late-integrated modality rather than something trained jointly from step zero (contrast this with GPT-4o, covered separately, where OpenAI explicitly claims joint end-to-end training across modalities).

**The Mixture-of-Experts rumor.** In mid-2023, SemiAnalysis (Dylan Patel and Gerald Wong) published a widely circulated report claiming GPT-4 is a sparse mixture-of-experts model: 8 experts, each with roughly 220B parameters, for a total of roughly 1.8T parameters, with 2 experts activated per forward pass (yielding roughly 280B active parameters per token, including some shared/always-on parameters), trained on roughly 13T tokens, using 16-way tensor parallelism and 8-way pipeline parallelism (128-way total model parallelism) across a very large A100 cluster, with the training run estimated at roughly 90-100 days. George Hotz independently stated similar 8x220B / 2-active-experts figures in a public podcast appearance around the same time, which is often cited as corroboration. **None of this has been confirmed by OpenAI.** It should be treated as informed industry rumor from analysts with a track record of semiconductor/compute supply-chain reporting, not as a leak with primary-source documentation, and not as something OpenAI has ever acknowledged, denied, or commented on directly. It is repeated so often in secondary sources that it has taken on a false patina of fact; this document flags it explicitly as unconfirmed every time it is invoked.

### 3. Scale -- Parameters, Data, Compute

No parameter count, dataset size, or training FLOP count has been officially published. The oft-cited numbers (~1.8T total / ~280B active parameters, ~13T training tokens) are the SemiAnalysis/Hotz rumor above, not confirmed figures -- repeated here only for completeness, with the same caveat.

What is confirmed or attributably sourced:
- Sam Altman stated in public interviews (e.g., at an MIT event, reported by multiple outlets) that GPT-4 training cost "more than $100 million." This is a compute-cost data point from OpenAI's own CEO, not a rumor, though it is a rounded, informal figure, not an audited number.
- The GPT-4 technical report's headline research claim is about *predicting* scale behavior rather than disclosing it: OpenAI reports building infrastructure and optimization methods with "remarkably predictable behavior" across scales, and shows a plot predicting GPT-4's final loss from a series of smaller models trained with the same methodology using 1,000x to 10,000x less compute. This tells us OpenAI ran a substantial internal scaling-law-fitting program before committing to the full run -- a strong (if indirect) signal that the full run's compute budget was large enough to justify serious amortized risk-reduction R&D, but it does not tell us the actual number.

**Reasoning about scale under uncertainty (the actually answerable part of this question):** given a >$100M training cost, contemporaneous 2022-era A100 cluster pricing (roughly $1-2/GPU-hour at the fleet-operation level for a well-utilized owned cluster, higher for on-demand cloud), and typical large-run utilization rates (Model FLOPs Utilization commonly 30-50% for dense transformer training at this scale), one can back out a plausible order of magnitude of total training FLOPs in the low-to-mid 1e25 range -- consistent with, but not proof of, a model with several hundred billion to low-single-digit-trillion total parameters trained on multiple trillions of tokens. This kind of cost-to-compute-to-scale triangulation is exactly the skill a staff-level systems interview is probing for: the ability to reason quantitatively about frontier training runs from public financial and hardware-market signals when the vendor discloses nothing, while being explicit about the width of the resulting error bars.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

OpenAI has disclosed almost nothing GPT-4-specific here either, but several adjacent facts are confirmed and worth anchoring on:
- OpenAI's compute partnership with Microsoft Azure was public well before GPT-4, including Microsoft's description of building a dedicated large-scale GPU supercomputer for OpenAI (widely reported as tens of thousands of A100 GPUs connected via high-bandwidth InfiniBand networking, in Azure's "AI supercomputer" announcements around 2020-2022). It is reasonable to infer GPT-4 was trained on this or a successor generation of that infrastructure, but OpenAI has not confirmed a specific cluster configuration, GPU count, or generation (A100 vs. early H100 access) for the GPT-4 run itself.
- The technical report's "predictable scaling" work implies a mature internal MLOps stack: automated launch and monitoring of many smaller-scale training runs across a compute range spanning 3-4 orders of magnitude, a fitted scaling-law function (loss and, notably, some downstream capability metrics like HumanEval pass rate) used to forecast the large run's outcome *before* spending the compute, and presumably a rollback/kill-criterion process if the large run's observed loss trajectory deviated from the predicted curve. This is a meaningfully different engineering discipline from "just launch the big run" -- it is closer to a staged capital-investment process with a forecasting gate, similar in spirit to how chip design houses do early SPICE simulation before committing a tape-out.
- Standard large-scale training engineering for a model in this parameter/token range (whether the true architecture is dense or the rumored sparse MoE) would require some combination of tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, and data parallelism (3D parallelism), ZeRO/FSDP-style optimizer and gradient state sharding to fit optimizer states in aggregate GPU memory, activation checkpointing to manage memory at this depth, and mixed-precision (bf16 with fp32 master-weight accumulation) training. If the MoE rumor is accurate, expert-parallelism (routing tokens to experts placed on different device groups, with all-to-all communication for dispatch/combine) would be an additional, nontrivial systems requirement layered on top of 3D parallelism -- MoE training at this scale is meaningfully harder to load-balance and network-provision for than dense training, which is one of the more interesting (if unconfirmed) systems angles of the rumor.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

The training objective is presumed to be standard autoregressive next-token prediction (cross-entropy over the model's tokenizer), consistent with every model in the GPT lineage; nothing in public materials suggests OpenAI departed from this for GPT-4's base pretraining stage.

Dataset composition is undisclosed. The technical report states only that the model was trained on "a large dataset of text and code, as well as publicly available data, including data licensed from third-party providers," and that the pretraining data has a knowledge cutoff (initially reported around September 2021 for the original release, later revised for subsequent snapshots such as GPT-4-0613 and GPT-4-turbo). No mixture proportions, deduplication methodology, filtering approach, or total token count are published. The presence of strong code-generation ability (HumanEval performance reported in the technical report) confirms a substantial code component in the mix, consistent with all GPT-3.5/4-era models. Given OpenAI's stated use of "data licensed from third-party providers," it is confirmed (in the sense that OpenAI itself states it) that at least part of the corpus was not purely scraped public web text -- but which providers, and what fraction of tokens, is not disclosed.

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

This is the best-documented part of the GPT-4 release, via the System Card and technical report, and is worth treating as the confirmed centerpiece of this entry.

- **RLHF**, as established in InstructGPT, remained the core alignment technique: human-labeled comparison data used to train a reward model, followed by RL (PPO) fine-tuning of the policy against that reward model.
- **Rule-Based Reward Models (RBRMs)**: OpenAI describes using a set of zero-shot GPT-4 classifiers ("rule-based reward models") as an *additional* signal during RL fine-tuning, specifically for safety-relevant behaviors -- e.g., correctly refusing disallowed requests, refusing in an appropriately-worded style, and *not* over-refusing borderline-but-acceptable requests (a documented failure mode of naive safety RLHF is a model that becomes needlessly evasive). An RBRM is given the model's output plus a rubric and produces a reward signal used alongside the human-preference reward model. This is a genuinely distinct technique from vanilla RLHF: it substitutes model-graded, rule-conditioned judgments for some of the human-labeled reward signal specifically in the safety domain, which is cheaper to scale and can be targeted precisely at known failure categories.
- **Adversarial red-teaming program**: OpenAI contracted more than 50 external experts across domains including cybersecurity, biological and chemical weapons risk, international security, and misinformation, to adversarially probe the model pre-release. Findings fed back into additional RLHF/RBRM training passes and mitigations before general availability.
- **Iterative deployment and post-launch adjustment**: OpenAI describes GPT-4's release as part of an "iterative deployment" philosophy -- shipping with monitoring and the expectation of continued fine-tuning/mitigation updates (visible in the sequence of dated snapshots: gpt-4-0314, gpt-4-0613, gpt-4-1106-preview, gpt-4-turbo, etc., each with adjusted behavior).
- A reported and specifically interesting side effect: the technical report shows that pre-RLHF ("base") GPT-4 is well-calibrated on multiple-choice evaluation (predicted confidence tracks empirical accuracy closely), while post-RLHF GPT-4 is measurably *miscalibrated* -- the alignment procedure improves instruction-following and safety behavior at a cost to probability calibration. This is one of the clearest, most citable public demonstrations of an "alignment tax" in a frontier model.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

- **Predictable scaling as a first-class engineering deliverable**: rather than a scaling-law paper for its own sake, OpenAI operationalized scaling-law fitting as pre-launch risk management -- forecasting both loss and select downstream capability metrics (e.g., HumanEval pass rate) from small-scale runs at 1,000x-10,000x less compute, and validating the forecast against the actual GPT-4 result. Publishing this (without publishing the underlying scale) is itself a notable communication strategy: OpenAI shares the *methodology* for de-risking frontier runs while withholding the *inputs and outputs* of that methodology for GPT-4 itself.
- **Rule-Based Reward Models** as a scalable complement to human-labeled RLHF specifically for safety behaviors, addressing the known over-refusal / under-refusal tradeoff.
- **Steerability via system messages**: GPT-4 introduced materially improved adherence to a persistent "system" instruction that shapes persona, tone, and constraints across a conversation, a capability OpenAI markets explicitly as "steerability" and treats as a controllable dimension distinct from raw task capability.
- **Exam-based evaluation as a capability narrative**: GPT-4's launch popularized standardized professional/academic exams as a capability benchmark for LLMs at a scale beyond prior academic NLP benchmarks (MMLU, etc.), a framing choice that significantly shaped how the field and the public discussed subsequent frontier releases.
- **Formal System Card as a release artifact**: publishing a dedicated, lengthy safety-and-risk document alongside a capability report set a template OpenAI and (to varying degrees) competitors have followed for subsequent frontier releases.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

GPT-4-specific engineering failure modes are not disclosed. What can be stated with confidence, extrapolating from general large-scale distributed training literature applicable at this compute/cluster scale (not GPT-4-specific confirmed detail):

- **Hardware failure rate at scale**: clusters of the size implied by a >$100M run (plausibly O(10,000) accelerators) experience frequent individual GPU/NIC/node failures during a multi-month run; robust checkpointing and fast job-restart/resume tooling is a hard requirement, not an optimization, at this scale.
- **Loss spikes and training instability**: dense transformer training at this depth/width commonly exhibits periodic loss spikes from data anomalies, numerical instability in mixed precision, or optimizer state corruption after a restart; mitigation commonly involves gradient clipping, careful learning-rate warmup/decay schedules, and (increasingly common industry practice by this era) selective rollback-and-skip of anomalous batches.
- **If the MoE rumor is accurate**, expert load balancing (avoiding a small subset of experts absorbing most tokens, which wastes the sparsity benefit and can starve undertrained experts) and all-to-all communication overhead for token routing across devices would be the dominant additional systems challenges relative to a dense model of similar active-parameter count.
- **Alignment-side challenge, confirmed**: the calibration degradation from RLHF noted above is a genuine, documented training challenge OpenAI reports without fully solving in the GPT-4 report -- it is presented as an open tradeoff, not something engineered away.

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

Confirmed product facts: GPT-4 shipped in 8K and 32K context-length variants at different (32K materially more expensive) pricing; it was noticeably slower per generated token than GPT-3.5-turbo at launch, consistent with either a larger dense model or a larger *active* parameter count under sparse activation (the MoE rumor's ~280B active-parameter estimate would be squarely consistent with the observed latency/cost profile relative to GPT-3.5, though this is inference from behavior, not confirmation of cause). Image input required a separate multimodal-enabled endpoint/rollout that lagged the initial text-only general availability by months, consistent with the modality being integrated somewhat separately from the core text model rather than shipped atomically. Deployment was via OpenAI's API and Microsoft Azure OpenAI Service, with rate limits and context-length tiers used as the primary product-segmentation and load-management levers.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

Reported results (OpenAI's own technical report numbers): simulated Uniform Bar Exam around the 90th percentile of test-takers (a figure that itself attracted later academic scrutiny and some pushback on methodology/percentile-source accuracy), strong performance across LSAT, SAT, GRE, and numerous AP exams, and large jumps over GPT-3.5 on MMLU, HellaSwag, and HumanEval. Known limitations, several explicitly acknowledged in OpenAI's own report: GPT-4 still hallucinates confidently, still has a fixed pretraining knowledge cutoff with no built-in real-time knowledge, still has a bounded context window that constrains long-document and long-conversation tasks relative to the 32K ceiling, and (as noted) is measurably less calibrated post-RLHF. A limitation specific to this document's purpose: because OpenAI discloses no architecture, weights, or training data, independent verification of any capability or safety claim is impossible -- all evaluation is necessarily black-box, API-mediated, and subject to OpenAI's own choices about what to test and disclose.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**CONFIRMED (stated directly by OpenAI in the technical report, System Card, or official communications):**
- Multimodal input: accepts text and images, outputs text.
- Two context-length product variants: 8,192 and 32,768 tokens.
- RLHF used for post-training, augmented with Rule-Based Reward Models (RBRMs) specifically for safety-relevant behavior shaping.
- External adversarial red-teaming program (50+ domain experts) prior to release.
- "Predictable scaling" methodology: forecasting final loss and some capability metrics from runs using 1,000x-10,000x less compute.
- Post-RLHF calibration is measurably worse than pre-RLHF (base model) calibration.
- Training data includes licensed third-party data in addition to public web data and code; exact composition undisclosed.
- Training cost publicly stated by Sam Altman as "more than $100 million" (informal, not an audited disclosure).
- No parameter count, layer/head/dimension configuration, positional encoding scheme, dataset size, or total training FLOPs has ever been officially disclosed.

**SPECULATION / UNCONFIRMED (never acknowledged by OpenAI):**
- The 8-expert, ~220B-per-expert, ~1.8T-total-parameter, ~280B-active-parameter sparse MoE architecture, ~13T training tokens, and specific parallelism configuration (16-way tensor x 8-way pipeline). Source: SemiAnalysis (Dylan Patel/Gerald Wong) report, mid-2023, corroborated informally by George Hotz in a public podcast statement. No primary documentation, no OpenAI acknowledgment or denial.
- Any specific GPU count, cluster generation (A100 vs. H100), or network topology used for the GPT-4 training run specifically (the Microsoft/Azure large-cluster partnership is real and public, but its specific configuration for GPT-4 is not confirmed).
- The internal design of the vision-input pathway (patch encoder + linear projection into a shared embedding space is a reasonable inference from contemporaneous multimodal LM research generally, not a GPT-4-specific confirmed design).
- Any estimate of total training FLOPs derived from the cost figure (order-of-magnitude reasoning presented in Section 3 is analytical inference, not disclosure).

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

- Be ready to reconstruct, from first principles, how you would estimate a frontier model's training compute given only a dollar figure and public hardware pricing/utilization assumptions -- this is precisely the reasoning-under-uncertainty exercise this model forces, and it demonstrates systems fluency independent of any leaked number.
- Articulate the RBRM design precisely: it is not "RLHF with extra steps," it is substituting a rubric-conditioned zero-shot classifier for human labels in a domain (safety refusal behavior) where the desired behavior is specifiable as a rule, which is both cheaper to scale and more precisely targetable than a general human-preference reward model -- and be able to discuss its failure modes (a classifier trained on rules can itself be gamed or can encode the rule-writer's blind spots).
- Be able to discuss the over-refusal/under-refusal tradeoff explicitly: safety RLHF that is too aggressive produces a model that refuses benign requests (a real, measured regression the RBRM approach targets), which is a concrete example of reward misspecification with user-visible product consequences.
- Have an opinion, and be able to defend it, on whether MoE at the scale rumored for GPT-4 is architecturally plausible given known 2022-era training infrastructure constraints (expert parallelism, all-to-all bandwidth requirements, load-balancing losses) -- this tests whether you understand MoE systems tradeoffs deeply enough to evaluate a rumor rather than just repeat it.
- Understand why "predictable scaling" is a genuinely different research contribution from a scaling-law paper: it converts scaling laws from a descriptive post-hoc fit into a pre-commitment forecasting and risk-gating tool for an unprecedented, unrepeatable, extremely expensive single run.
- Be explicit, unprompted, about the calibration-degradation-under-RLHF finding -- it is one of the cleanest empirical demonstrations in the public literature of RLHF's alignment tax, and it is a good anchor for a broader conversation about the tension between instruction-following/safety training and epistemic properties of the base model.
