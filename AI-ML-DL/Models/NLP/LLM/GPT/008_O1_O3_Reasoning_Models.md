## o1 / o3 Reasoning Model Series (2024-2025)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

OpenAI shipped o1-preview and o1-mini in September 2024, o1 (full) and o1-pro in December 2024, and announced o3 and o3-mini in December 2024 (with broader availability through the first half of 2025). This series represents a distinct branch of the GPT lineage rather than a numbered successor to GPT-4/GPT-4o: OpenAI explicitly frames it as "a new series of models trained with reinforcement learning to perform complex reasoning," optimized for domains with checkable correctness -- competition mathematics, competitive programming, scientific reasoning -- rather than for general conversational breadth or low latency.

The strategic significance is that it opens a second, largely independent axis of capability scaling. Pretraining-scale improvements (more parameters, more data, more FLOPs at training time -- the axis studied by Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al./Chinchilla) had, by 2024, shown signs of diminishing and increasingly expensive returns on frontier labs' own internal assessments (a claim made publicly by several lab researchers, though the degree of slowdown is itself debated and not something this document takes a side on). OpenAI's o1 announcement blog post ("Learning to Reason with LLMs") presents empirical curves showing model accuracy on hard reasoning benchmarks (AIME, competitive coding) improving smoothly as a function of *test-time* (inference) compute -- i.e., how many reasoning tokens the model is allowed to generate before answering -- as a lever distinct from and additive to pretraining-time compute. This reframes "how do we get a better model" from a single-axis question (spend more on pretraining) into a two-axis question (spend more on pretraining, and/or spend more on inference per query), with the second axis being something a deployment can dial per-request rather than something fixed at training time. This is one of the most consequential framing shifts in frontier LLM research of 2024-2025, and it is the organizing idea of this entire document entry.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

**Base architecture is not disclosed.** OpenAI has not published whether o1/o3 use a new base transformer, a fine-tuned GPT-4o-class checkpoint, a different tokenizer, or a different context-length/attention configuration. The publicly emphasized innovation is a *training-and-inference behavior*, not a stated architectural change: the model is trained (via RL, detailed in Section 6) to, at inference time, generate a long sequence of intermediate reasoning tokens -- explore approaches, check intermediate steps, backtrack, verify -- before committing to a final answer token sequence. Structurally, this can be implemented within an entirely ordinary decoder-only causal transformer: the "reasoning" is just autoregressively generated tokens like any other, occupying context window budget, with no publicly confirmed special architectural mechanism (no disclosed separate "scratchpad" memory, no disclosed recurrent controller) distinguishing it from standard generation. The distinguishing factor OpenAI has confirmed is a *product/serving* mechanism: the raw reasoning token stream is not returned to the user or API caller; instead, a separate, smaller, model-generated *summary* of the reasoning is surfaced (visible in ChatGPT's UI as a "thought for N seconds" collapsible trace), while the full raw chain-of-thought is retained internally (per OpenAI's own stated rationale, discussed in Section 6) and billed to the user as invisible output tokens.

The API additionally exposes a **`reasoning_effort` parameter** (documented for o1/o3-family models as low/medium/high), letting a caller trade latency/cost against expected answer quality by constraining how much internal reasoning the model is allowed to generate -- this is a confirmed, documented product control, and it is the most direct evidence that "how much to think" is treated as a first-class, externally tunable inference parameter rather than a fixed property of the checkpoint.

### 3. Scale -- Parameters, Data, Compute

Undisclosed for both o1 and o3, as with every other model in this document. What is qualitatively new and confirmed at the level of OpenAI's own framing (not a specific number): OpenAI states the *training* compute invested in the RL stage, and the *inference* compute spent per query at high reasoning-effort settings, are both treated as independently scalable investments -- i.e., OpenAI reports being able to trade "train the RL stage longer" and "let the model think longer at inference" against each other and against benchmark accuracy, and shows both as separate scaling curves in the o1 announcement. The o3/ARC-AGI results (see Section 10) make this concrete in cost terms via a rare quasi-independent data point: the ARC Prize Foundation, which administers the ARC-AGI benchmark, published its own measured cost-per-task figures for o3 at different compute/effort tiers when OpenAI gave it early access to evaluate the model -- this is one of the very few places in this entire model series where a third party, rather than OpenAI itself, produced and published a cost/performance figure. It should still be read as specific to that one benchmark and access arrangement, not as a general disclosure of o3's economics.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

Not disclosed, but the *shape* of the required infrastructure is a genuinely informative, confirmable-by-inference departure from prior GPT-series post-training. Standard RLHF (as used for GPT-3.5/4-class instruction tuning) is comparatively inference-light during the RL loop: the policy generates one response per prompt, a reward model (a single forward pass) scores it, and PPO updates the policy -- the dominant cost is still largely attributable to pretraining. RL training that specifically targets long chain-of-thought quality against verifiable rewards, as o1/o3 are described as using, is structurally far more inference-heavy during post-training: for each training prompt, the system must generate multiple (potentially many) full reasoning rollouts, each of which can itself be very long (thousands of reasoning tokens), before any reward or gradient update happens. This means the post-training stage requires large-scale, low-latency generation infrastructure -- essentially production-grade inference serving repurposed as a training-data-generation engine -- alongside the usual GPU training infrastructure for the actual gradient updates. It also requires **verifier infrastructure**: for math, an automated answer-checker (symbolic or exact-match against ground truth); for code, a sandboxed execution environment that can run generated code against test cases at scale, safely and quickly, for every rollout of every training step. This sandboxed-execution-at-training-scale requirement is a genuinely new infrastructure category relative to prior GPT post-training, and is a strong candidate topic for a systems-focused interview discussion even though OpenAI has not disclosed its specific implementation.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

Not separately disclosed from the underlying base model. Presumed standard autoregressive pretraining precedes the RL stage, as with every model in the GPT lineage; the entry's technical content is properly about the *post-training* stage (Section 6), which is where this model family's actual innovation lives.

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

This is the technical core of the o1/o3 story, and several pieces of it are confirmed by OpenAI's own publications, not merely inferred.

**Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).** This is the precise term for what distinguishes o1/o3's RL stage from standard RLHF, and it is worth being exact about the distinction:
- **RLHF** (as used for InstructGPT/GPT-3.5/GPT-4 instruction tuning): a reward *model* is trained to predict human preference between pairs of full model outputs, on tasks where there is generally no single objectively "correct" answer (helpfulness, tone, style, safety-appropriateness). The reward model is itself a learned, imperfect proxy for human judgment, and the policy is optimized against that proxy -- a setup famously vulnerable to reward hacking/over-optimization against the proxy's own blind spots (Goodhart's-law-style failure).
- **RLVR** (the o1/o3 approach, as OpenAI describes it): reward comes from an automated, *objectively checkable* verifier rather than a learned preference model, in domains where "correct" has a ground-truth definition -- a math problem's final numeric/symbolic answer matches the known solution; generated code passes a held-out test suite; a logic puzzle's answer is verifiably right or wrong. Because the reward signal is not a learned proxy for a subjective judgment but a (near-)ground-truth check, the RL stage can push much harder and longer against it with substantially less risk of the specific failure mode where the policy learns to exploit the *reward model's* idiosyncrasies rather than actually getting better at the task (though reward hacking is not eliminated entirely -- a policy can still find degenerate ways to satisfy a verifier, e.g. exploiting a weak test suite, a distinct but related risk).
This distinction -- optimizing the process/trajectory that leads to a verifiably correct outcome, rather than optimizing final-output style against a learned human-preference proxy -- is the single most important technical fact to have crisp and ready to state precisely in an interview about this model family.

**Hidden chain-of-thought, with a stated rationale.** OpenAI has stated two reasons for not exposing the raw reasoning trace to users/API callers: (1) a competitive/product reason -- the raw CoT is considered a valuable artifact of the model's problem-solving process that a competitor could use to distill or reverse-engineer capability cheaply; (2) a monitorability/safety reason -- OpenAI has stated it wants to preserve the ability for the model's internal reasoning to be *unfiltered and honest* (including reasoning about whether to refuse something, or reasoning that might reflect an intermediate incorrect or even policy-violating thought en route to a correct/safe final answer), without training the model to make that internal reasoning itself look good to a human observer -- the concern being that if you optimize the visible reasoning trace to *look* aligned, you may inadvertently train the model to produce reasoning that looks acceptable rather than reasoning that accurately reflects its actual computation, which would make the trace a worse monitoring signal, not a better one. Instead, a separate, smaller model summarizes the hidden trace into the user-visible "thinking" display. This is a genuinely debated design choice in the field: it improves near-term product safety/IP posture at the cost of external auditability of the model's actual reasoning process, and it is directly connected to an active alignment research question -- chain-of-thought *faithfulness* (whether a model's stated reasoning, visible or not, accurately reflects the computation that actually produced its answer) -- studied across the field, not specific to OpenAI.

**Deliberative alignment.** OpenAI published a paper/technique under this name (December 2024, alongside o1) describing training the model to explicitly reason about a safety specification *within its hidden chain-of-thought* before producing a final answer -- essentially, having the model deliberate about whether/how a request interacts with policy, as an explicit reasoning step, rather than relying purely on the input-output shaping that standard RLHF safety tuning provides. This is confirmed via OpenAI's own publication and is a distinct, named alignment technique specific to reasoning models, worth naming precisely rather than folding into "RLHF" generically.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

- **RLVR at frontier scale** as a named, precise alternative/complement to RLHF for domains with checkable correctness.
- **Test-time compute / inference-time scaling** established as an empirically demonstrated, independently dialable axis of capability improvement, distinct from pretraining-scale, with published curves (accuracy vs. reasoning-token budget) supporting the claim.
- **Deliberative alignment**: safety reasoning made an explicit, trained step within the hidden CoT rather than purely an input/output shaping objective.
- **Hidden-but-summarized reasoning** as a specific, named product/safety design pattern for reasoning models, with an explicit, stated (if contestable) rationale distinguishing it from simply not having a CoT at all.
- **Externally exposed reasoning-effort control** (`reasoning_effort`: low/medium/high) as a first-class API parameter, operationalizing test-time compute scaling as something a caller -- not just OpenAI internally -- can tune per request.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

Confirmed-by-implication and reasonably inferred challenges, kept explicitly labeled: **reward hacking within verifiable domains** remains possible even though the reward is "objective" -- a policy can learn to exploit a weak or incomplete verifier (e.g., a code test suite with insufficient coverage, or a math-answer-format matcher that a degenerate output can satisfy without genuine correct reasoning), meaning verifier quality/coverage becomes a first-order engineering investment, not an afterthought. **CoT faithfulness** is an open research concern rather than an engineering problem OpenAI claims to have solved: there is no publicly demonstrated guarantee that the hidden reasoning trace (or its user-facing summary) accurately reflects the actual computation driving the final answer, which matters both for user trust and for any safety argument that relies on being able to "read the model's mind" via its CoT. **Overthinking/cost calibration**: a model trained to generate long reasoning traces for hard problems risks generating unnecessarily long traces for easy ones, wasting latency and cost -- the `reasoning_effort` parameter and continued RL tuning are the confirmed/inferred mitigation levers, respectively (effort-level exposure is confirmed; the internal RL-side mitigation for right-sizing reasoning length is inferred, not disclosed in detail).

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

Confirmed: inference is slower and more expensive than a comparably-sized non-reasoning model, as a direct, acknowledged consequence of generating a long hidden reasoning trace before any user-visible output token appears -- OpenAI bills for these hidden reasoning tokens even though the caller never sees their content, which is a notable and sometimes-criticized economic design point (paying for tokens you cannot inspect). Serving must support a materially different latency profile from a standard chat model: a long "silent" generation period (sometimes many seconds to tens of seconds at high reasoning effort) before the first user-visible token, which has UX implications (ChatGPT surfaces a running "thinking" indicator with the periodically-updated summary specifically to manage this latency perception). The `reasoning_effort` control lets a deployment trade latency/cost against expected accuracy per request rather than needing a separate model checkpoint per latency tier.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

Headline OpenAI-reported results: large jumps over GPT-4o on AIME (competition mathematics), GPQA-Diamond (graduate-level science QA), and Codeforces competitive-programming rating, with o3 in particular reported at a Codeforces rating competitive with top human competitive programmers per OpenAI's own announcement. The most notable *quasi-independent* data point in this entire document series: the ARC Prize Foundation independently evaluated o3 on ARC-AGI (a visual/abstract reasoning benchmark specifically designed to resist memorization) under early access from OpenAI, reporting a large jump over all prior models (roughly 75-88% depending on compute tier, on the semi-private evaluation set) alongside a large increase in per-task inference cost at the highest compute/effort tier -- this is one of the only places any of these four models has a capability claim checked by a party other than the model's own developer, and it is directly relevant to the test-time-compute story since ARC Prize explicitly reported the cost/accuracy tradeoff across effort tiers. Separately, Epoch AI's FrontierMath benchmark (very hard, largely unpublished research-level mathematics problems) showed near-zero scores for prior frontier models and a meaningful (though still low in absolute terms) jump for o3 per Epoch AI's own reporting. Known limitations: cost and latency are real deployment constraints, not merely benchmark footnotes; hallucination is not eliminated by reasoning training and can in some cases manifest as confident-sounding but incorrect *reasoning* rather than just a confident wrong final answer; hidden CoT means external researchers and auditors cannot directly verify whether the model's stated reasoning process matches its actual computation (the faithfulness concern above); and there is active public discussion (not unique to OpenAI, and not a settled empirical finding about o1/o3 specifically) about whether optimizing a model's externally-visible or externally-summarized reasoning could, in principle, train against the wrong target if the summarization or hidden-trace training process incentivizes reasoning that merely *looks* correct/safe rather than reasoning that *is*.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**CONFIRMED (OpenAI's own blog posts, system cards, and named-technique publications):**
- The model is trained via reinforcement learning specifically to improve the quality of extended chain-of-thought reasoning generated before a final answer, using domains with automatically verifiable correctness (math, code, and similar checkable tasks) as the reward signal -- i.e., Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), as distinct from standard RLHF's learned-preference-model reward.
- Raw chain-of-thought is hidden from users/API callers; only a model-generated summary is shown, with OpenAI stating both competitive/IP and monitorability/faithfulness-preservation rationales for this choice.
- Inference is slower and more expensive than non-reasoning models as a direct consequence of generating (billed, hidden) reasoning tokens before the visible answer.
- A `reasoning_effort` parameter (low/medium/high) is exposed via the API, letting callers trade latency/cost against expected accuracy.
- "Deliberative alignment" -- training the model to explicitly reason about safety specifications within its hidden CoT -- is a named, published OpenAI technique introduced alongside o1.
- Test-time compute (reasoning-token budget at inference) is empirically shown by OpenAI to improve accuracy on hard benchmarks along a smooth curve, distinct from and additive to pretraining-scale improvements.
- ARC Prize Foundation's o3 evaluation on ARC-AGI, and Epoch AI's o3 evaluation on FrontierMath, are genuinely third-party-run evaluations (under early access arrangements with OpenAI), not OpenAI self-reported numbers -- a notable exception in this document series.

**SPECULATION / UNCONFIRMED (not stated by OpenAI):**
- Whether o1/o3 use the same base transformer architecture/checkpoint as GPT-4o, a modified architecture, or a different tokenizer.
- The specific RL algorithm used (PPO, a PPO variant, or something else) -- OpenAI has not disclosed the optimization algorithm for the RLVR stage.
- Parameter counts, training data composition, or total training compute for either model.
- The specific internal mechanism (if any) beyond "generate more tokens" that differentiates o1/o3's reasoning generation from ordinary autoregressive decoding -- no disclosed special memory/controller/search mechanism exists in public materials, though this document's companion .py file demonstrates one plausible *inference-time* scaffold (best-of-N with a scoring mechanism) that is explicitly a toy illustration, not a claim about o1/o3's internals.
- Whether o3's reported ARC-AGI/FrontierMath results generalize to a broad capability jump versus being concentrated in benchmark-adjacent domains; both the ARC Prize and Epoch AI evaluations are informative but narrow, single-benchmark data points.

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

- State the RLVR vs. RLHF distinction precisely and be ready to discuss its actual scope of applicability: RLVR only works where you can automatically verify correctness, which excludes most open-ended, subjective, or stylistic tasks -- the reasoning-model approach does not replace RLHF for those tasks, it complements it (and OpenAI's own system cards indicate RLHF-style and deliberative-alignment safety training remain layered on top).
- Be ready to argue both sides of hiding the CoT: it protects against distillation and (per OpenAI's stated rationale) may preserve a more faithful reasoning signal for internal safety monitoring than training the model to produce reasoning that looks good to external observers would -- but it also removes exactly the transparency an external safety researcher or auditor would want, and there is no public proof the hidden trace is actually more faithful than a visible one would be. This tension (transparency vs. monitorability-by-training-non-optimization) is a genuinely unresolved, discussable research question, not a settled matter.
- Understand and be able to explain "test-time compute as a second scaling axis" with enough precision to contrast it against Kaplan/Chinchilla-style pretraining scaling laws: pretraining scaling improves the model once, at training time, for all future queries; test-time compute scaling is a per-query dial, meaning the same checkpoint can be made more or less capable/expensive on demand -- a fundamentally different economic and deployment lever than anything available to non-reasoning GPT-series models.
- Be able to name reward hacking as a risk that persists even under verifiable rewards (weak/incomplete verifiers being exploitable), and connect this to why verifier/test-suite quality is itself now a frontier-lab engineering investment, not a solved commodity.
- Have a clear, sourced answer ready for "how do we know o3 is actually better, given OpenAI reports its own benchmarks" -- and be able to name the ARC Prize Foundation and Epoch AI evaluations specifically as the (rare, narrow) third-party exceptions in this space, while being honest about their limited scope.
- Be able to discuss the infrastructure implication directly: RLVR training is inference-heavy (many long rollouts per prompt) and requires safe, fast, scaled sandboxed code execution for verification -- a different infrastructure profile from classical RLHF post-training, and a legitimate systems-design discussion topic in its own right.
