# Word Embeddings Evolution — Companion Notes

Companion to `002_word_embeddings_evolution.py`. This file's code implements `Word2Vec` (skip-gram, plus a simplified stand-in for CBOW) and `GloVe` from scratch with NumPy, training both on WikiText-2 subsets. FastText is named in the file's docstring but is **not** actually implemented anywhere in the code — it's covered fully below as the third required embedding technique, with an explicit note about that gap.

---

## Word2Vec: Skip-gram and CBOW (2013)

### 1. What Problem It Solved
TF-IDF and n-gram counting both represent words as sparse, "meaningless" symbols — a huge vector with mostly zeros, where every word gets its own dimension unrelated to every other word's dimension. Under that representation, "cat" and "dog" are just as unrelated as "cat" and "bicycle", even though "cat" and "dog" clearly behave more similarly in language. This is a real practical problem: a model trained on sentences about cats has no way to transfer what it learned to sentences about dogs, even though the two situations are semantically close. Word2Vec solved this by learning a **dense** vector for every word (typically 100-300 numbers instead of vocabulary-sized sparse vectors) such that words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors — giving models, for the first time, a computable notion of word similarity and even simple analogical relationships (the famous "king - man + woman ≈ queen" result).

### 2. Architecture — How It Works
Word2Vec is fundamentally based on the **distributional hypothesis**: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. It doesn't try to define meaning directly — it just trains a simple neural network to predict context from a word (or a word from context), and the useful embeddings turn out to be a side-effect of that prediction task.

There are two variants:

**Skip-gram**: given a center word, predict the words in its surrounding context window. If the window size is 5 and the sentence is "the cat sat on the warm mat quietly", and the center word is "sat", the model creates training pairs like (sat → cat), (sat → the), (sat → on), (sat → the), (sat → warm) — one pair per word within 5 positions on either side. Each pair pushes the center word's embedding to be predictive of that particular context word.

**CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)**: the mirror image — given all the context words, predict the single center word. Using the same example, CBOW would take the (averaged) embeddings of "the, cat, on, the, warm" and try to predict "sat".

Mechanically, both variants use two embedding matrices: one for words as centers (the "input" embeddings you actually use afterward) and one for words as contexts/outputs. For skip-gram, the forward pass looks up the center word's embedding, computes a dot product against every word's output vector to get a score for each vocabulary word, turns those scores into probabilities with softmax, and computes how far off that probability distribution is from the actual observed context word (cross-entropy / negative log-likelihood loss). Backpropagation then nudges the center word's embedding, and the output vector of the observed context word, closer together, while implicitly pushing them away from unrelated words (through the softmax normalization).

**This repo's implementation detail worth knowing**: the `Word2Vec` class here computes a **full softmax over the entire vocabulary** for every single training pair (`scores = np.dot(self.output_weights, center_embedding)` over all vocab_size rows). The real historical breakthrough that made Word2Vec fast enough to train on billions of words was avoiding exactly this — using **negative sampling** or **hierarchical softmax** instead of a full softmax (see Training Challenges below). Also worth flagging: the code's `_train_cbow` method doesn't actually implement true CBOW (which averages multiple context embeddings to predict one center word) — it simply swaps the center and context word and reuses the skip-gram training step (`self._train_skipgram(context_word, center_word, learning_rate)`). So while the file's docstring says it demonstrates "Skip-gram and CBOW," the CBOW code path is really a simplified skip-gram variant, not the real CBOW averaging mechanism.

### 3. Model Size & Parameters
The original Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) trained embeddings of **300 dimensions** over a vocabulary of about **3 million words and phrases**, on Google News data — giving roughly `300 * 3,000,000 * 2 (input + output) ≈ 1.8 billion` parameters across both embedding matrices, though only the input embedding matrix (~900 million numbers) is typically kept and used afterward.

This repo's code trains embeddings of only **50 dimensions** (`embedding_dim=50` in `main()`), over a vocabulary built with `min_count=5` from just the first 1,000 training sentences of WikiText-2 (a small fraction of typical vocabulary sizes). Parameter count is reported in the code as `len(vocab) * 50 * 2` (input embeddings + output weight matrix). This is scaled down purely for speed and teachability: with a vocabulary this small and only 3 training epochs over a subset of 10,000 training pairs per epoch, the whole thing trains in seconds rather than the days the original Google News model needed on much larger hardware and data.

### 4. Dataset & What It Was Trained On
The original Word2Vec was trained on **Google News**, roughly **100 billion words** of news text — an enormous corpus that let the model see enough varied contexts for each word to learn stable, meaningful embeddings even for moderately rare words.

This repo trains on WikiText-2, and specifically only the **first 1,000 sentences** of its training split (`build_vocabulary(train_sentences[:1000])`), with training itself further capped to the first 10,000 shuffled (center, context) pairs per epoch, for 3 epochs. WikiText-2 is a reasonable stand-in for learning the mechanics of Word2Vec — you can watch the loss go down and the embeddings start to organize — but with a corpus this small (a few hundred thousand words rather than a hundred billion), don't expect the "king - man + woman ≈ queen"-quality analogies that made the original Word2Vec famous; there simply isn't enough data here for rare, specific relationships like that to be learned reliably.

### 5. Training Process
The objective being optimized is: maximize the probability the model assigns to the true observed (center, context) word pairs, which is the same as minimizing the negative log-likelihood / cross-entropy loss between the predicted softmax distribution and the actual context word. Optimization is plain **stochastic gradient descent**, computed by hand in this code (there's no PyTorch/autograd here — the class implements the forward pass, the softmax, the loss, and the backward-pass gradient updates directly with NumPy).

Concretely, the code: builds all (center, context) pairs from every sentence and window position (`_create_training_pairs`), shuffles them each epoch, updates on the first 10,000 pairs per epoch (a fixed-size subset, for speed) with a learning rate of `0.025` (the same default learning rate historically used for Word2Vec), and repeats for 3 epochs when run from `main()`. Each individual update (`_train_skipgram`) is a single manual gradient-descent step: compute the softmax error vector (`probabilities` minus a one-hot vector for the true context word), then update the output-weight matrix and the center word's embedding by that error scaled by the learning rate.

The original Word2Vec paper also used plain SGD (with a linearly decaying learning rate as training progressed), so this matches the historical approach reasonably well — the big difference from the original isn't the optimizer, it's the missing negative-sampling speedup described below.

### 6. Training Challenges
The single biggest historical challenge for Word2Vec was **computational cost**: a full softmax over a vocabulary of millions of words means every single training step requires computing (and updating) scores for every word in the vocabulary, even though only one of them is the "correct" answer. That's enormously wasteful at scale. The original paper's solution was **negative sampling**: instead of updating against the entire vocabulary, update the true context word's probability upward and only a small handful (5-20) of randomly-sampled "negative" words' probabilities downward, leaving everything else untouched for that step. An alternative solution used in the same paper is **hierarchical softmax**, which arranges the vocabulary in a binary tree so that computing (and updating) a word's probability only costs `O(log(vocab_size))` instead of `O(vocab_size)`.

This repo's implementation does **not** use either speedup — it computes a full softmax over the whole vocabulary on every step, which is exactly why the code only trains on a small vocabulary and a capped subset of pairs; at Google-News-scale vocabulary and data, this naive full-softmax approach would be computationally infeasible with plain NumPy. This is a good thing to be able to point out in an interview: understanding *why* the toy version needs to skip negative sampling to stay simple, while knowing that's the exact piece production Word2Vec depends on for speed, shows real depth.

### 7. Performance & Evaluation
Word2Vec embeddings are evaluated with **word similarity** and **word analogy** benchmarks: cosine similarity between vectors for words expected to be related (e.g., "king" and "queen" should be closer than "king" and "banana"), and analogy tasks like "man is to woman as king is to ___" (solved by vector arithmetic: `vector(king) - vector(man) + vector(woman)` should land closest to `vector(queen)`). The original Word2Vec paper reported strong accuracy on Google's analogy test set (tens of thousands of analogy questions spanning syntactic and semantic relationships), a result that was considered a striking demonstration that simple vector arithmetic could capture real semantic structure.

This repo's code evaluates similarity with `find_similar_words` (cosine similarity between one word's vector and every other word's vector, via scikit-learn's `cosine_similarity`) on a handful of test words like "king", "queen", "good", "bad". Given the tiny training corpus and epoch count used here, expect the similarity rankings to be noisy and only loosely sensible rather than showcasing the famous high-quality analogies — that's an expected and instructive limitation of the scaled-down demo, not a bug.

### 8. Impact — Why It Mattered
Word2Vec was the breakthrough that made dense word embeddings practical at scale and popularized them as a standard first step in almost every NLP pipeline that followed — instead of training a model from scratch on sparse one-hot words, you could plug in pretrained Word2Vec vectors and get a real head start, especially valuable when labeled training data was limited. It also demonstrated, memorably, that vector arithmetic on learned embeddings could capture real semantic and syntactic relationships, which reframed how researchers thought about representing meaning computationally. Its main limitation — every word gets exactly **one fixed vector**, regardless of context, so "bank" (river) and "bank" (finance) share the same embedding — is precisely the gap that later motivated **contextual embeddings** (ELMo, then BERT and transformer-based models), where a word's representation changes depending on the sentence it's in.

### 9. How To Explain This In An Interview
"Word2Vec learns dense word vectors by training a simple neural network to predict context words from a center word — that's skip-gram — or the reverse — that's CBOW — and the embeddings that fall out of that prediction task turn out to capture real semantic similarity, because words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors. I implemented skip-gram with a full softmax and manual gradient updates in NumPy, trained with plain SGD at a 0.025 learning rate, matching the original paper's setup. The historically important optimization I did *not* implement is negative sampling, which the original paper used to avoid computing a full softmax over the entire vocabulary on every step — that's what let Word2Vec scale to Google News' 100-billion-word corpus and 3-million-word vocabulary. I evaluated my embeddings with cosine similarity between related words. Word2Vec matters because it made dense, semantically meaningful word representations practical, and it's the direct ancestor of every embedding-based NLP pipeline that followed, up until contextual embeddings solved its biggest limitation: a fixed vector per word regardless of context."

## GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation (2014)

### 1. What Problem It Solved
Word2Vec learns purely from **local context windows** — it only ever looks at words within a small window (e.g. 5 words) of each other, one window at a time, and never directly uses global statistics like "how often do these two words co-occur across the *entire* corpus." That means Word2Vec can miss or under-use useful signal that only shows up when you look at the big picture — e.g., two words might rarely appear right next to each other but still co-occur very consistently at a document level in a way that's informative. GloVe (Pennington, Socher & Manning, 2014, from Stanford) solved this by explicitly building a **global word-word co-occurrence matrix** for the whole corpus first, and then training embeddings to reproduce the statistics in that matrix — directly combining local context information (each entry is still built from context windows) with global corpus-wide counts (aggregated once, rather than seen one training pair at a time).

### 2. Architecture — How It Works
GloVe works in two stages.

**Stage 1 — build a co-occurrence matrix.** Scan the whole corpus once, and for every pair of words `(i, j)` that appear within some window size of each other, increment a count `X_ij`, typically weighted by how close together they are (closer = more weight). This gives one big sparse matrix of "how often does word i appear near word j across the entire corpus." This repo's code does exactly this in `build_cooccurrence_matrix`: for every word `i` at position `p`, it looks at all words `j` within `window_size` positions and adds `1/distance` to `cooccurrence[i][j]` — so a word 1 position away contributes a full 1.0, a word 3 positions away contributes 1/3, etc.

**Stage 2 — factorize it.** Once you have the co-occurrence counts, GloVe trains word vectors (and separate context vectors, plus a bias term for each) such that the dot product of two words' vectors approximates the log of their co-occurrence count:

```
vector(i) · vector(j) + bias(i) + bias(j)  ≈  log(X_ij)
```

This is trained as a **weighted least-squares regression** problem: minimize the squared difference between the predicted value (dot product plus biases) and the actual log co-occurrence count, for every observed pair, weighted by a function `f(X_ij)` that down-weights extremely frequent pairs (like stopword pairs) so they don't dominate training, while still giving zero weight to pairs that never co-occurred.

A tiny worked example: if "ice" and "cold" co-occur 50 times in the corpus, GloVe wants `vector(ice) · vector(cold) + bias(ice) + bias(cold) ≈ log(50) ≈ 3.91`. Training nudges both vectors (and biases) to get that dot product closer to 3.91, while simultaneously satisfying every other word pair's constraint — the vectors that emerge from solving all these constraints jointly end up placing semantically related words near each other.

This repo's weighting function (`weight = min(1.0, (count / 100.0) ** 0.75)`) and squared-error loss (`weight * (prediction - log(count + 1)) ** 2`) match the real GloVe paper's formula closely: the original paper uses `f(x) = (x/x_max)^alpha` capped at 1, with `x_max = 100` and `alpha = 0.75` as its recommended defaults — exactly the constants used here.

### 3. Model Size & Parameters
The original GloVe models (from the Stanford NLP group) were released at several scales, the largest trained on **840 billion tokens of Common Crawl web text**, producing **300-dimensional** vectors over a vocabulary of about **2.2 million words**; a smaller, commonly used version was trained on Wikipedia 2014 + Gigaword 5 (about 6 billion tokens) with vocabulary around 400,000 words. Like Word2Vec, the "size" is two embedding matrices (word vectors and context vectors) plus two small bias vectors.

This repo's code trains **50-dimensional** vectors (`embedding_dim=50`), with the vocabulary size determined by `build_vocabulary` (the same vocabulary built earlier from the first 1,000 WikiText-2 sentences), and reports parameter count as `len(vocab) * 50 * 2` (word + context embeddings), not counting the small bias vectors. As with Word2Vec, this is scaled down purely to keep the demo fast and inspectable — the co-occurrence matrix itself is built from only the first 500 training sentences (`train_sentences[:500]`), and training only updates on the first 5,000 shuffled co-occurrence pairs per epoch, for 5 epochs.

### 4. Dataset & What It Was Trained On
The original GloVe releases were trained on some of the largest text corpora used for word embeddings at the time: Wikipedia dumps, the Gigaword news corpus, Twitter data (for a separate GloVe-Twitter release), and Common Crawl web text at up to 840 billion tokens.

This repo's code builds its co-occurrence matrix from just the first **500 sentences** of WikiText-2's training split — a minuscule fraction of even the smallest original GloVe training set. WikiText-2 works fine for demonstrating the mechanics (build a co-occurrence matrix, factorize it with weighted least squares) but, exactly as with Word2Vec, don't expect embeddings of the same semantic quality the original, web-scale GloVe vectors achieved — there just isn't enough data here for rare word relationships to be reliably captured.

### 5. Training Process
The loss being minimized is the weighted squared error between the model's predicted `log(co-occurrence count)` and the actual observed value, described above. Optimization in the original GloVe paper used **AdaGrad** (an adaptive per-parameter learning-rate method well-suited to sparse, unevenly-frequent co-occurrence data). This repo's code instead uses **plain SGD with a fixed learning rate** (default `0.05`), updating word vectors, context vectors, and both bias terms by the same gradient-scaled amount on every step (`_train_step`). This is a simplification: AdaGrad would adapt the learning rate per parameter automatically (helpful because some words are far more frequent than others), while the fixed-rate SGD used here treats every parameter update the same way — a reasonable teaching simplification, since implementing AdaGrad by hand adds complexity without changing the core lesson about GloVe's global-co-occurrence idea.

### 6. Training Challenges
Building the co-occurrence matrix itself is the first practical challenge: for a large corpus and vocabulary, the matrix can have billions of non-zero entries, requiring sparse storage (this repo uses `defaultdict(Counter)`, which is fine at this small scale but wouldn't hold up unmodified at web scale). The second challenge is the same one Naive Bayes and n-grams face — extremely frequent word pairs (especially those involving common words like "the" or "of") would dominate training and skew the learned vectors if trained without correction, which is exactly why the weighting function `f(x)` caps the contribution of highly frequent pairs. A subtler challenge is that GloVe needs the co-occurrence matrix built over the *entire* corpus before training can start (unlike Word2Vec, which can stream through sentences one at a time), so memory and preprocessing time scale with corpus size in a way Word2Vec's online approach doesn't.

### 7. Performance & Evaluation
GloVe is evaluated the same way Word2Vec is: word similarity benchmarks (cosine similarity against human-judged similarity scores) and word analogy tasks (vector arithmetic). The original GloVe paper reported that its vectors matched or slightly outperformed Word2Vec on several analogy and similarity benchmarks of the time, while often training faster because factorizing a pre-built co-occurrence matrix can be more efficient than iterating over every individual context window in the raw text multiple times.

This repo's code evaluates GloVe the same way it evaluates Word2Vec — cosine-similarity lookups for a handful of test words via `evaluate_embeddings` and `find_similar_words`. Given the tiny corpus, capped co-occurrence pairs, and few epochs used here, expect noisy, only loosely meaningful similarity results, same caveat as Word2Vec above.

### 8. Impact — Why It Mattered
GloVe showed that you didn't have to choose between local context-window methods (like Word2Vec) and older global matrix-factorization methods (like classic Latent Semantic Analysis) — you could get the best of both by building a co-occurrence matrix from local windows, then factorizing it with a global objective. It became, alongside Word2Vec, one of the two dominant pretrained-embedding options used across NLP throughout the mid-2010s, widely used as a drop-in input layer for downstream models (sentiment classifiers, NER taggers, etc.) before those models had their own learned embeddings. Like Word2Vec, its core limitation — one fixed vector per word regardless of context — is what contextual embeddings (ELMo, then transformer-based models like BERT) solved next.

### 9. How To Explain This In An Interview
"GloVe builds word vectors from global co-occurrence statistics rather than only local context windows like Word2Vec. First you scan the whole corpus once to build a word-word co-occurrence matrix, weighting nearby co-occurrences more heavily than distant ones. Then you train word and context vectors, plus bias terms, so that their dot product approximates the log of the co-occurrence count for every pair, using a weighted least-squares loss that down-weights extremely frequent pairs so common words don't dominate. The original paper optimized this with AdaGrad; in my implementation I used plain SGD with a fixed learning rate for simplicity. I evaluated the resulting vectors with cosine similarity between related words, same as I did for Word2Vec. GloVe matters because it showed you could combine the efficiency of local-context training with the completeness of global corpus statistics, and it became one of the two standard pretrained embedding choices, alongside Word2Vec, before contextual embeddings replaced the whole 'one fixed vector per word' paradigm."

## FastText (2016)

### 1. What Problem It Solved
Both Word2Vec and GloVe assign exactly one vector per whole word, learned only from whatever whole-word tokens they saw during training. That creates two problems. First, **out-of-vocabulary words**: any word not seen during training gets no vector at all (or a generic `<UNK>` vector), which is common for morphologically rich languages, misspellings, or rare technical terms. Second, both methods **ignore internal word structure**: "run", "running", and "runner" are treated as three completely unrelated symbols, even though they obviously share a root and related meaning. FastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017, from Facebook AI Research) solved both problems at once by representing each word not as one atomic unit, but as a combination of its **character n-grams** (overlapping chunks of characters) — so morphologically related words automatically share sub-word pieces, and even a word never seen during training can still get a reasonable vector built from the character n-grams it shares with known words.

### 2. Architecture — How It Works
The core idea: break every word down into overlapping character chunks (typically 3 to 6 characters long), plus the whole word itself as one extra unit, and represent the word's embedding as the **sum (or average) of the embeddings of all those chunks**.

A concrete example: for the word "where" with character n-grams of length 3, and adding boundary markers `<` and `>` to mark word start/end, you'd get n-grams like: `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere`, `re>`, plus the whole-word token `<where>`. Each of those chunks has its own learned embedding vector, and "where"'s final embedding is the sum of all of them. Notice that "where" and "here" would share the n-grams `her` and `ere` — giving the model a mechanism to represent that they're related, purely from shared sub-word structure, without ever being told about English morphology explicitly.

Since the number of possible character n-grams can be enormous, FastText uses a **hashing trick**: instead of storing a unique vector for every possible n-gram, it hashes each n-gram into one of a fixed number of buckets (the original paper uses about 2 million buckets) and shares an embedding per bucket — trading a small amount of hash-collision noise for a bounded, manageable table size.

Once you have the sub-word-based word vectors, the actual training objective is basically the same as Word2Vec's skip-gram with negative sampling: predict context words from a center word, except the center "word" vector used in the prediction is now the *sum of its character n-gram vectors* instead of a single lookup, and gradients flow back into each of those individual n-gram embeddings during backpropagation.

**Note on this repo's code**: `002_word_embeddings_evolution.py`'s docstring says it demonstrates "Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText," but there is no FastText class or subword-level logic anywhere in the file — only `Word2Vec` and `GloVe` are implemented. It's covered here in full because it's one of the three required word-embedding techniques for this series, but there's no code in this repository to point to for it; everything below is the historical/conceptual picture rather than a description of code in this file.

### 3. Model Size & Parameters
The original FastText models released by Facebook use **300-dimensional** vectors, trained with a hash table of roughly **2 million n-gram buckets** (in addition to whole-word vectors for known vocabulary words), trained on large web-scraped corpora (Wikipedia and Common Crawl) across 157 languages in one widely-used release. The total parameter count is comparable to Word2Vec/GloVe's whole-word embedding tables, plus the additional (but capped, thanks to hashing) n-gram bucket table.

Since FastText isn't implemented in this repo's code, there's no repo-specific configuration to report. If it were added following this file's existing pattern (50-dimensional embeddings, vocabulary from a subset of WikiText-2 sentences), the natural extension would keep the same 50-dimensional size but add a hashed n-gram embedding table (e.g., a few thousand buckets, much smaller than the original 2 million, sized to a toy vocabulary) alongside the whole-word table already used by `Word2Vec`.

### 4. Dataset & What It Was Trained On
The original FastText word-vector releases were trained on **Wikipedia** dumps and **Common Crawl** web text across many languages, generally billions of tokens per language for the larger releases — chosen partly because subword modeling shows its biggest advantage on morphologically rich languages (like Finnish, Turkish, or German) and rare/technical vocabulary, both of which benefit from lots of raw text to learn good n-gram statistics.

WikiText-2 would be a perfectly reasonable, if small, stand-in for a teaching version of FastText — English has comparatively simple morphology, so the subword advantage is less dramatic than it would be in a morphologically richer language, but the core mechanics (build word vectors from n-gram sums, train with skip-gram plus negative sampling) would demonstrate the same way, just at a much smaller and faster scale, exactly like the Word2Vec and GloVe demos in this file.

### 5. Training Process
FastText's training loop is architecturally the same as Word2Vec's skip-gram with negative sampling: for every (center, context) pair, compute a score using the *summed n-gram vector* as the center word representation, apply the same negative-sampling loss (push the true context word's score up, a handful of randomly sampled negative words' scores down), and backpropagate the gradient into every individual n-gram embedding that contributed to the sum. In the original paper, this was optimized with plain SGD with a linearly decaying learning rate, matching Word2Vec's own default training setup — FastText's real innovation was the subword representation and the hashing trick for scalability, not a different optimizer or loss function.

### 6. Training Challenges
FastText inherits every computational challenge Word2Vec has (needing negative sampling or hierarchical softmax to avoid a full-vocabulary softmax on every step) and adds one more: summing potentially dozens of n-gram vectors for every single word, for every single training example, is meaningfully more expensive per step than a single embedding lookup. The hashing trick (mapping the effectively unbounded space of possible character n-grams down to a fixed ~2 million buckets) manages this, at the cost of occasional hash collisions where two unrelated n-grams share a bucket and slightly interfere with each other's learned representation — a deliberate, small accuracy-for-speed trade-off.

### 7. Performance & Evaluation
FastText is evaluated the same way as Word2Vec and GloVe — similarity and analogy benchmarks — plus an extra category specific to its subword design: performance on **rare and out-of-vocabulary words**, and on **morphologically rich languages**, where it consistently outperformed Word2Vec and GloVe in the original paper's benchmarks, precisely because those settings are where sharing information across word forms via character n-grams pays off most. On common-word similarity/analogy benchmarks in English, FastText performed comparably to Word2Vec, with its advantage becoming clearer on rarer words and other languages.

### 8. Impact — Why It Mattered
FastText showed that incorporating a word's internal structure (not just treating it as an atomic symbol) meaningfully improves robustness — better handling of rare words, typos, and morphologically complex languages — without abandoning the efficient skip-gram-with-negative-sampling training recipe that made Word2Vec practical. It also made high-quality pretrained word vectors available for 157 languages, extending word-embedding benefits well beyond English. Like Word2Vec and GloVe, though, FastText still assigns a fixed representation regardless of sentence context, which is the limitation that contextual embeddings (ELMo, then transformer-based encoders) addressed next, ultimately leading toward the transformer-based language models that dominate NLP today.

### 9. How To Explain This In An Interview
"FastText extends Word2Vec by representing each word as the sum of its character n-gram embeddings, plus a whole-word embedding, instead of one atomic vector per word. That means morphologically related words like 'run' and 'running' automatically share sub-word pieces, and even words never seen during training can get a reasonable vector built from n-grams they share with known words. Training uses the same skip-gram-with-negative-sampling objective as Word2Vec, just with the center word's vector computed as a sum over its n-gram embeddings, and a hashing trick to keep the n-gram table a fixed, manageable size instead of growing unbounded. It's evaluated the same way as Word2Vec, plus specifically on rare-word and cross-lingual benchmarks, where it clearly outperforms whole-word-only methods. FastText matters because it fixed the out-of-vocabulary and morphology blind spots of Word2Vec and GloVe while keeping the same efficient training recipe, and it's the last major step in the 'one fixed vector per word' family of embeddings before contextual embeddings changed the paradigm entirely."
