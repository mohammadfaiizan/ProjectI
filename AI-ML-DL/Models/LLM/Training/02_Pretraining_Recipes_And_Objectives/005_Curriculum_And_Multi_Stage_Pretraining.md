# Curriculum and Multi-Stage Pretraining

Modern frontier pretraining is a sequence of purpose-built stages, not one undifferentiated run; this file explains what each stage is actually for, mechanistically, and why its compute share is what it is.

## 1. Why "pretraining" is no longer one homogeneous run

Early large-LM pretraining recipes (GPT-2, GPT-3) are close to a single, homogeneous process: one data mixture, one context length, one learning-rate schedule, applied uniformly from step 0 to the end of training (see `..\..\GPT\003_GPT3.md`, Section 5 — GPT-3's data mixture is reweighted by source, but the *mixture itself* doesn't change over the course of training, and there is no dedicated long-context or cooldown stage). By the DeepSeek-V2/V3, Llama 3, Qwen2.5, and GLM-4 generation, the near-universal pattern documented across `..\..\OpenSource\` is a **three-stage structure**:

1. A long **main pretraining phase** at a comparatively short context length, consuming the overwhelming majority of the total token/compute budget, with a data mixture that is roughly stable (module minor scheduled shifts) throughout.
2. A much shorter, dedicated **long-context extension phase**, in which the model's effective context window is stretched from its native pretraining length out to the target maximum (often 32K→128K or further), via targeted continued training on long-sequence data combined with a positional-encoding adjustment.
3. A final, short **annealing / high-quality-data cooldown phase**, in which the data mixture shifts toward curated, high-quality sources and/or the learning rate is decayed to a very small value, squeezing a further, disproportionate quality gain out of a small fraction of the total compute budget.

This file explains the empirical motivation for each stage separately, because each solves a genuinely different problem, and conflating them (treating "multi-stage pretraining" as one undifferentiated idea) is exactly the kind of imprecision a staff interview will probe.

Read section by section, the file works through: why a single homogeneous run stopped being the norm (Section 1); the cost argument for keeping the main run short-context (Section 2, with a numeric illustration in Section 2.1); the extension stage's positional-encoding mechanics (Section 3, with worked wavelength arithmetic in Section 3.2a) and its compute-cost asymmetry and evaluation risk (Section 3.3); the annealing stage's two intertwined mechanisms, its natural pairing with a WSD schedule, and its disclosure gaps (Section 4, with a cross-model comparison in Section 4.5, a contrast against classical curriculum learning in Section 4.6, whole-pipeline risks in Section 4.7, and the boundary with post-training in Section 4.8); a compute-allocation synthesis (Section 5); a note on real-world ordering and overlap (Section 6); a fully worked planning scenario (Section 7); and closing reference material (Sections 8-10).

## 2. Stage 1: main pretraining at short(er) context

There is not much new to say about this stage mechanically beyond the objective mechanics in `001_Pretraining_Objectives_Overview.md` and the scaling/compute-allocation logic in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md` — it is simply the bulk causal-LM training run. The one thing worth stating explicitly here, because it motivates Stage 2's existence: **the context length chosen for this stage is deliberately short relative to the eventual target**, purely for cost reasons. Attention compute (and, absent context-parallel sharding, activation memory) scales quadratically in sequence length for the attention term specifically, so training the entire multi-trillion-token main run at a long context multiplies the cost of that dominant compute stage for the entire run — a cost that would be paid on every one of the trillions of tokens processed, rather than on the comparatively tiny number of tokens processed during a dedicated later stage. This is the direct rationale for the DeepSeek-V2 pattern (pretrained at 4K, then extended — `..\..\OpenSource\006_DeepSeek_V2.md`), Llama 3's pattern (8K initial release, extended to 128K in the 3.1 release — `..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md`), and Qwen2.5's pattern (shorter native context, YaRN-extended to 128K on select sizes — `..\..\OpenSource\009_Qwen2_5.md`).

### 2.1 A worked numeric illustration of the quadratic-cost argument

It's worth putting a rough number on "training at long context throughout would multiply the dominant cost of the entire run," since the claim is load-bearing for the entire rest of this file and shouldn't be taken purely on faith.

```python
def attention_flops_share(seq_len: int, d_model: int, n_layers: int) -> float:
    """Rough share of total per-token transformer FLOPs attributable to the
    attention SCORE computation (Q.K^T and the weighted sum over V), i.e. the
    O(n^2*d) term, relative to the O(n*d^2) dense-matmul term the 6ND
    approximation (002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md, Section 3)
    is built on. This is illustrative, not an exact accounting."""
    attn_score_flops_per_layer = 2 * seq_len * d_model  # per query position, roughly
    matmul_flops_per_layer = 12 * d_model  # per token, from the 12*d^2 per-layer
                                            # parameter count in the GPT-3 arithmetic
                                            # (see ..\..\GPT\003_GPT3.md Section 2),
                                            # doubled for FLOPs per the 2N rule
    total_per_layer = attn_score_flops_per_layer + matmul_flops_per_layer
    return attn_score_flops_per_layer / total_per_layer

for s in [2_000, 8_000, 32_000, 128_000]:
    print(s, attention_flops_share(s, d_model=8192, n_layers=80))
```

At a typical frontier `d_model` (e.g., 8192, in the range of a 70B-class model), the attention-score term is a small fraction of total per-token compute at short context (a few thousand tokens) but grows linearly with sequence length while the dense-matmul term stays fixed per token — so at 128K context the attention term can become comparable to, or exceed, the matmul term, meaning the simple \(6ND\) approximation itself starts to break down (exactly the caveat flagged in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`, Section 3.3) *and*, more importantly for this file's argument, the *absolute* per-token cost of training at 128K throughout is meaningfully higher than training at a few thousand tokens throughout — multiplied across trillions of tokens in the main run, this difference is exactly why no lab pays it for the entire pretraining budget.

## 3. Stage 2: long-context extension

### 3.1 What has to change, mechanically, and why so little training suffices

The central empirical claim behind treating context extension as a *short, cheap, dedicated stage* rather than folding it into the main run is: **the model's core language competence (grammar, world knowledge, reasoning patterns) does not need to be relearned to extend context — only its ability to generalize positional information to distances it saw rarely or never during main pretraining does.** A transformer's attention mechanism itself has no inherent length limit — the QK-dot-product/softmax/weighted-sum computation is well-defined for any sequence length. What actually breaks when you naively run a model beyond its pretraining context length is the **positional encoding**, specifically RoPE's (Rotary Position Embedding) behavior at relative distances it was never trained on.

RoPE encodes position by rotating query/key vectors by an angle proportional to absolute position, using a set of geometrically-spaced frequencies (see the per-model docs, e.g., `..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md` Section 2, for the full rotation-matrix mechanics — not re-derived here). The lowest-frequency components of this rotation have a wavelength long enough that, at the relative distances actually seen during a short-context pretraining run, they never complete even a small fraction of a full rotation — the model has effectively never observed what those components' rotation *pattern* looks like at the longer relative distances a longer context would introduce. Naively extending context length at inference (or continued-training) time without adjustment causes those under-trained low-frequency components to produce **out-of-distribution rotation angles** the model has no calibrated way to interpret, which is the direct mechanistic cause of the well-documented quality collapse when a RoPE-based model is simply run past its trained context length with no adjustment at all.

### 3.2 NTK-aware scaling and YaRN

**Position interpolation / NTK-aware scaling** addresses this by rescaling the rotation frequencies (equivalently, rescaling the effective positions fed into RoPE) so that the *maximum relative distance* the extended context introduces maps back into a rotation-angle range closer to what the model saw during main pretraining — "NTK-aware" specifically refers to scaling different frequency components by *different* amounts (rather than a single uniform interpolation factor across all frequencies), preserving the highest-frequency components' fine-grained resolution (important for distinguishing nearby positions accurately) while compressing the lowest-frequency components more aggressively (since those are the ones responsible for encoding very long-range relative distance, and are the ones that were most under-trained relative to the new target range).

**YaRN** (Yet another RoPE extensioN method — used explicitly in DeepSeek-V2's and Qwen2.5's extension stages, per their respective docs) refines this further: rather than a single blanket interpolation, YaRN applies a **ramp function across the frequency spectrum**, interpolating more aggressively at low frequencies and leaving high frequencies closer to un-scaled, combined with an **attention-temperature adjustment** (a small correction to the softmax temperature in attention, motivated by the empirical observation that a naively-rescaled RoPE alone tends to produce a subtly miscalibrated attention-score distribution, which a small temperature correction compensates for). The practical upshot reported across the labs that use it: a comparatively small amount of continued-training tokens (specifically at the new, longer context length, using this rescaled positional scheme) is enough to recover strong performance at the new maximum length, without needing anything close to the token budget of the main pretraining run.

```python
def yarn_ramp_factor(freq_idx: int, n_freqs: int,
                      low_freq_factor: float = 1.0, high_freq_factor: float = 0.25) -> float:
    """Illustrative sketch of YaRN's per-frequency interpolation ramp: low-frequency
    (long-wavelength, small freq_idx) components get interpolated more aggressively
    (closer to a full compression toward the new context length), high-frequency
    components are left closer to their original scale. Real implementations use a
    smoother ramp parameterized by wavelength thresholds rather than a linear index."""
    t = freq_idx / max(1, n_freqs - 1)
    return low_freq_factor * (1 - t) + high_freq_factor * t
```

### 3.2a A worked numeric illustration of why low frequencies are the problem

It helps to see the wavelength arithmetic directly rather than take "the low-frequency components are under-trained" purely on faith.

```python
import numpy as np

def rope_wavelengths(dim: int, base: float = 10000.0) -> np.ndarray:
    """Wavelength (in token positions) of each RoPE frequency component:
    the absolute-position distance over which that component completes
    one full rotation (2*pi radians)."""
    i = np.arange(0, dim, 2)
    freqs = 1.0 / (base ** (i / dim))
    return 2 * np.pi / freqs

wavelengths = rope_wavelengths(dim=128)
native_context = 8_000
fraction_of_rotation_seen = np.minimum(1.0, native_context / wavelengths)
print("lowest-frequency component sees only",
      fraction_of_rotation_seen.min(), "of a full rotation within native context")
print("highest-frequency component sees",
      fraction_of_rotation_seen.max(), "rotations (capped at 1.0 by min(), actually many)")
```

With `base=10000` and `dim=128`, the lowest-frequency component's wavelength is on the order of tens of thousands of positions — comparable to or larger than an 8K native context — so within that native context it completes only a small fraction of one rotation; the highest-frequency components, by contrast, have wavelengths of only a few positions and complete many full rotations even within a short context. This is the numeric substance behind Section 3.1's claim: the components responsible for encoding *coarse, long-range* relative position are exactly the ones that see the least varied training signal at short context, which is precisely why they are the ones YaRN's ramp (Section 3.2) targets for the most aggressive rescaling.

### 3.3 Why this stage's compute is a small slice of the total budget, and what that implies

Because the extension stage is training on data that is *already* well-represented by the model's existing competence (the model already knows the language; it needs positional recalibration and exposure to genuinely long documents, not new world knowledge) and because the positional-encoding adjustment does most of the structural work of making longer distances interpretable at all, the number of tokens needed to reach strong performance at the new maximum context is empirically far smaller than the main pretraining budget — DeepSeek-V3's disclosed compute breakdown makes this concrete: ~119K GPU-hours for context extension against ~2.664M GPU-hours for the main pretraining phase (`..\..\OpenSource\007_DeepSeek_V3.md`, Section 3), i.e., context extension consumes on the order of 4-5% of pretraining's compute despite taking the model from its native context all the way to its final 128K-token support. This asymmetry is exactly why treating long-context support as "a cheap add-on stage" rather than "train natively at the target length from step zero" is the dominant, and clearly compute-rational, industry pattern.

**What this implies for evaluation**, though, is a genuine open risk rather than a free lunch: because this stage sees comparatively few tokens and a narrower data distribution (long documents specifically, sometimes partly synthetic — e.g., constructed long-context tasks, as Llama 3.1's extension stage uses per `..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md` Section 3), it is not guaranteed that the model's ability to *use* information anywhere within the new window is as uniformly strong as its ability to use information within its original native context — this is exactly why long-context capability claims are validated with dedicated needle-in-a-haystack-style and long-document benchmarks (as flagged in `003_Model_Architecture_Decisions_At_Pretraining_Time.md`, Section 3.2) rather than assumed from the architecture supporting the sequence length at all.

## 4. Stage 3: annealing / high-quality-data cooldown

### 4.1 The empirical observation motivating this stage

Across multiple training reports (Llama 3's explicit "data annealing" step — `..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md`, Section 5 and 7; and more broadly, the pattern popularized by MiniCPM's WSD-schedule-based cooldown), labs found that **deliberately up-weighting a small, curated, high-quality data mixture in the final portion of pretraining, while simultaneously decaying the learning rate to a very small value, measurably improves downstream benchmark performance relative to simply running the original flat data mixture through to the end of training at the same total token budget.** This is a genuinely empirical finding rather than something derivable purely from the objective mechanics in `001_Pretraining_Objectives_Overview.md` — it is reported as a measured benchmark delta in the training reports that use it, and should be treated with the same "confirmed empirical result, not a fully first-principles-derived guarantee" epistemic status as the scaling-law fits in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`.

### 4.2 Two intertwined mechanisms, worth separating

It's easy to conflate "shift the data mixture" and "decay the LR to near zero" into one undifferentiated idea because they're usually done together, but they are mechanistically distinct levers, and a precise answer should be able to separate them:

- **LR annealing to near-zero as a settling mechanism.** A learning rate that is still comparatively large late in training means the model's weights are still being pushed around by relatively large per-step updates, including in response to noisy, lower-quality, or idiosyncratic examples that happen to appear in a given batch — the model never fully "settles" into a low-loss basin, because the optimizer keeps taking meaningfully-sized steps. Decaying the LR to a very small value late in training lets the model take much smaller, more precise steps, converging more tightly into whatever basin the bulk of training has found, with much less risk of a late high-loss batch knocking it into a meaningfully different (and not necessarily better) region.
- **Mixture shift toward curated/high-quality data as a targeted final nudge.** Independent of LR, *what the model is being shown* in this final stretch matters: if the final tokens seen are disproproportionately high-quality (carefully curated instruction-adjacent text, textbook-like or reasoning-dense content, deduplicated and filtered web text, code with verified correctness, etc. — exact composition is lab-specific and often not disclosed in full detail), the model's *most recent* gradient signal — which, especially combined with a decaying LR, has an outsized influence on where training actually ends relative to earlier, larger-step updates — is disproportionately shaped by exactly the kind of data the eventual downstream benchmarks and product use cases care about most, rather than by the full raw pretraining distribution's average composition.

Because the LR is small during this stage, the *data mixture* portion of the intervention doesn't have to be applied for very long or over very many tokens to have an outsized effect relative to its share of the total training budget — this is the same "small slice of compute, disproportionate quality return" structure Section 3.3 describes for context extension, arrived at for a different underlying reason (settling into a better basin near the end of training, rather than positional recalibration).

### 4.3 Why WSD (warmup-stable-decay) is a natural schedule to pair this with

Section 2.3 of `004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md` introduces WSD as an alternative to cosine decay specifically because it decouples "how long do we train" from "what shape is our decay." The annealing/cooldown stage is exactly where that decoupling pays off operationally: under a WSD schedule, the plateau phase can run for as long as the main pretraining budget calls for (extended, if the loss curve still justifies it, per the overtraining logic in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`, Section 6), and *only once that decision is made* does the short, steep final decay — during which the curated-data mixture shift is also applied — get triggered. Under a pure cosine schedule fixed to a pre-committed total step count \(T\), the annealing stage's data-mixture shift and the schedule's decay are forced to happen at a specific, pre-planned point; under WSD, the cooldown stage becomes a genuinely separable, appendable final phase, which is part of why WSD-style schedules have become associated with this annealing pattern specifically in more recent training reports.

### 4.4 What "high quality" means here, and the honest disclosure gap

Every training report that describes an annealing stage is, to varying degrees, non-specific about the *exact* composition of the curated mixture used — this is consistent with the broader pattern (visible throughout the per-model docs in `..\..\OpenSource\` and `..\..\GPT\`) that exact data-mixture percentages are among the least-disclosed details of any frontier training recipe, annealing-stage mixtures included. What is disclosed, qualitatively and consistently across the labs that describe this stage: the shift favors data judged higher-quality by whatever filtering/classification pipeline the lab uses (frequently reusing the same quality-classifier machinery used to filter the main pretraining corpus, applied more selectively), and/or data closer in distribution to the eventual evaluation and product use cases than the average of the full raw pretraining mixture. Treat any specific claimed percentage breakdown of an annealing-stage mixture from a source not explicitly cited as disclosing it as speculative.

The annealing stage is disclosed with real specificity by comparatively few labs (Llama 3 being the clearest public example, and MiniCPM's WSD-schedule work being the clearest methodological source for how to schedule it) — this uneven disclosure is itself consistent with the general pattern (noted throughout this collection's per-model docs) that late-stage data-mixture details are among the least-disclosed specifics of any frontier training recipe.

## 4.6 Contrast with classical curriculum learning

It is worth explicitly distinguishing this file's three-stage structure from the older "curriculum learning" literature (Bengio et al., 2009, and a long line of subsequent work), because the terms are related but not identical, and conflating them is an easy mistake. Classical curriculum learning is about **ordering training examples by difficulty** — presenting easier examples first and harder examples later (or some more sophisticated adaptive ordering), on the theory that this ordering improves optimization dynamics or final generalization relative to a random shuffle, evaluated primarily at a per-example or per-batch granularity. The multi-stage pretraining pattern in this file operates at a much coarser granularity and is motivated by a different mechanism in each stage: Stage 1 to Stage 2's transition is not "harder examples" — it's a change in *sequence length* and *positional-encoding regime*, driven by a cost argument (Section 2.1), not a difficulty-ordering argument; Stage 2 to Stage 3's transition is not "easier vs. harder" either — it's a shift toward *curated quality* combined with an *optimizer-schedule* change (LR annealing), which is about basin-settling and final polish, not about example difficulty at all. Modern frontier pretraining does still use difficulty- or domain-adaptive data-mixture scheduling in a way that has some kinship with classical curriculum learning (e.g., data-mixture reweighting schedules that shift emphasis across domains over the course of a single stage), but that is a narrower, complementary practice sitting *within* Stage 1's data-mixture design, not the same thing as the three-stage main/extension/annealing structure this file describes, which is organized around cost and mechanism, not around a difficulty gradient.

## 4.7 Risks and evaluation across the whole staged pipeline

Beyond the extension-stage-specific evaluation risk already named in Section 3.3, it's worth naming the risks that apply to the staged approach as a whole, since a staff-level treatment should be able to name what could go wrong with this entire strategy, not just praise its compute efficiency:

- **Stage-boundary regressions.** A change applied at a stage boundary (a new data mixture, a new positional-encoding scale, a schedule transition) can, in principle, cause a measurable regression on capabilities the model had already acquired in the previous stage, if the transition is handled carelessly (e.g., an LR that's still too large when the mixture shifts abruptly, causing a larger-than-intended perturbation away from a previously-good region). This is exactly why the annealing stage's LR-decay and mixture-shift are usually paired rather than applied independently (Section 4.2) — a large mixture shift at a still-large LR is a more disruptive perturbation than the same shift at a small LR.
- **Extension-stage narrowness compounding across sub-domains.** If the long-context extension data over-represents certain document types (e.g., books or code repositories) relative to others, the resulting long-context capability may be measurably stronger on the over-represented type and weaker elsewhere — a subtler version of the general evaluation risk in Section 3.3, worth checking specifically across sub-domains rather than only with a single aggregate long-context benchmark score.
- **Annealing-stage mixture overfitting to the evaluation suite.** Because the annealing mixture is explicitly chosen to resemble "the kind of data downstream evaluations and products care about" (Section 4.2), there is a real risk of this tipping from legitimate quality-focused curation into something closer to targeting the specific benchmark suite the lab evaluates against — a distinction that matters for whether reported benchmark gains reflect genuine capability improvement or a narrower form of benchmark-specific optimization, and one a careful evaluator should probe by checking generalization to benchmarks *not* used to guide the annealing mixture's construction.
- **Compounding uncertainty across stages.** Each stage's own evaluation is comparatively cheap to run and is usually run before moving on to the next, but the *combination* of extension-stage and annealing-stage effects on the final model's behavior is not always evaluated as thoroughly as either stage in isolation — an interaction between the two (e.g., the annealing mixture under-representing long documents, partially undoing some of the extension stage's intended effect) is a plausible failure mode that a staged pipeline should explicitly check for, not merely assume away because each stage individually passed its own evaluation.
- **Irreversibility once a later stage begins.** Because each stage builds directly on the checkpoint the previous stage produced, discovering a problem with Stage 1's data mixture only after Stage 2 or Stage 3 has already consumed real compute means that fix generally requires re-running the affected downstream stages, not just patching the discovered issue in isolation — which is part of why the ablation and validation methodology in `006_Pretraining_Ablations_And_Research_Methodology.md` applies to the staging plan itself (how much native context, what extension factor, what annealing mixture) and not only to the underlying architecture and optimizer choices.

## 4.8 Where annealing ends and post-training begins

It is worth drawing this boundary explicitly, since the annealing stage's use of curated, high-quality, sometimes instruction-adjacent data can look superficially similar to supervised fine-tuning, and conflating the two is a real source of confusion. Annealing is still, mechanistically, **pretraining**: it uses the same causal-LM objective (`001_Pretraining_Objectives_Overview.md`) over raw or lightly-curated text, with no explicit instruction/response structure required, and its purpose is to influence the base model's general capability distribution before any task-specific supervision begins. Post-training (SFT, RLHF/RLAIF, preference optimization — covered in this collection's post-training module, not here) begins only once the base model is considered "done," and operates on explicitly structured instruction/response or preference data with a different objective (or a different loss weighting/masking scheme even when nominally still cross-entropy-based) specifically aimed at instruction-following and alignment rather than general language competence. The reason this boundary matters practically: a lab deciding how "curated" or "instruction-adjacent" to make the annealing mixture is implicitly deciding how much of the instruction-following signal to try to bake in during pretraining versus reserve entirely for the dedicated post-training stage — and pushing too far toward instruction-like data during annealing risks blurring a boundary that most training reports (implicitly or explicitly) intend to keep clean, without gaining the more deliberate, feedback-driven control that a proper SFT/RLHF pipeline provides over exactly that same capability.

## 5. Putting the three stages together: a compute-allocation picture

Expressed as a rough compute-share breakdown consistent with the disclosed DeepSeek-V3 figures (Section 3.3) and the qualitative pattern described across Llama 3, Qwen2.5, and DeepSeek-V2: the overwhelming majority of total training compute (typically 90%+) goes to Stage 1; a small single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage goes to Stage 2 (context extension); and Stage 3 (annealing) is smaller still in raw compute terms, precisely because its leverage comes from *where in the training trajectory* it's applied (late, at low LR, with a curated mixture) rather than from *how much* compute it consumes. This is the general shape worth internalizing: **each later stage is cheap in compute relative to the main run, but each is solving a problem that the main run's mixture and context length could not solve on its own** — Stage 1 cannot cheaply teach long-range positional generalization because doing so at full context length throughout would be prohibitively expensive (Section 2); Stage 1's flat, broad-coverage data mixture is not the same thing as "maximally polished toward the eventual evaluation/product distribution," and fixing that gap late, cheaply, and precisely is exactly Stage 3's job. Understanding *why* each stage is structured the way it is — not just being able to name "long-context extension" and "annealing" as buzzwords — is the level of precision a staff interview is checking for.

### 5.1 A worked numeric compute-allocation table

Concretely, for a hypothetical total training-compute budget of \(C\) FLOPs sized via the Chinchilla-style methodology in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`:

| Stage | Rough compute share | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1 (main pretraining) | ~90-95% of \(C\) | Broad language competence, world knowledge, in-context learning, at the lowest achievable per-token cost given the chosen native context length |
| Stage 2 (long-context extension) | ~4-8% of \(C\) (higher for larger extension factors, per Section 7's discussion) | Positional generalization to the target maximum context, via a rescaled RoPE/YaRN scheme plus targeted long-document training |
| Stage 3 (annealing/cooldown) | Typically the smallest remaining share, often well under Stage 2's | Final-quality polish via curated-data mixture shift and LR decay to near-zero, disproportionate to its compute share because of *when* in the trajectory it's applied |

This table's numbers are illustrative, assembled from the qualitative pattern and the one clearly disclosed data point (DeepSeek-V3's ~4-5% figure, Section 3.3) rather than a universal constant — a model with a much larger extension factor (Section 7's 256K-target scenario) should expect to budget toward the higher end of Stage 2's range, and the exact Stage 3 allocation is, as Section 4.4 notes, one of the least-disclosed figures in this entire staged pipeline across the field's published training reports.

## 6. A note on ordering and overlap

The three-stage picture above is a clean idealization; real training reports sometimes describe more granular sub-stages (e.g., a staged sequence of intermediate context-length increases rather than a single jump straight to the final target, as flagged for Llama 3.1 in `..\..\OpenSource\003_Llama3.md` Section 2), and the annealing stage is sometimes described as overlapping with or immediately following the tail of the long-context stage rather than as a fully separate, sequentially-later phase. The three-way conceptual split (main / extension / annealing) is nonetheless the right mental model to reason from in an interview setting — it correctly separates three genuinely distinct problems (broad capability at controlled cost; positional generalization to long range; final-quality polishing via mixture and LR) even when a specific lab's actual implementation blurs the boundaries between them in its exact scheduling.

## 7. A worked scenario: planning the staged pipeline for a hypothetical 70B model targeting 256K context

To make Sections 1-6 concrete, walk through planning this end to end for a hypothetical 70B-parameter model, targeting 256K context for a long-document-analysis product, with a total compute budget already fixed by a prior Chinchilla-style sizing exercise (`002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`).

**Stage 1 planning:** choose a native context length balancing the quadratic-cost argument (Section 2.1) against not making Stage 2's eventual extension factor too large — a common choice is a native length that's a modest fraction of the target (e.g., 8K-16K native for a 256K target, a 16-32x extension factor), rather than an extremely short native length that would require an unusually aggressive Stage 2 rescaling. The overwhelming majority of the fixed compute budget (Section 5's 90%+ figure) is spent here, on the ordinary causal-LM objective (`001_Pretraining_Objectives_Overview.md`) with whatever data mixture and optimizer recipe (`004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md`) has already been validated at proxy scale (`006_Pretraining_Ablations_And_Research_Methodology.md`).

**Stage 2 planning:** given a 16-32x extension factor (larger than several of the disclosed examples in Section 4.5's table, most of which extend by roughly 16x from an 8K native length to 128K), budget somewhat more of the extension-stage compute share than the low end of the disclosed 4-5% range, and plan a staged sequence of intermediate context-length increases (per Section 6's note on Llama 3.1's approach) rather than a single jump directly to 256K, to reduce the risk that any single rescaling step is too large to recover from with the planned token budget. Data for this stage should specifically include long documents in the domains the product targets (long-document analysis, per the stated use case), not merely long documents in general, given Section 4.7's point about extension-stage narrowness compounding across sub-domains.

**Stage 3 planning:** adopt a WSD schedule (Section 4.3) from the start of Stage 1 specifically so that the decision of when to enter the annealing plateau's decay is not pre-committed months in advance — allowing the option to extend Stage 1 further if loss curves justify it (per the overtraining logic in `002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`, Section 6) without having to redesign the schedule. When the decay phase is triggered, pair the LR decay with a mixture shift toward curated data that specifically includes long-document-analysis-adjacent content (not only short-form curated text), to avoid Section 4.7's risk of the annealing mixture inadvertently undoing part of what Stage 2 established for long-context capability.

**Evaluation planning across all three stages:** validate Stage 1 with standard short-context benchmarks; validate Stage 2 specifically with needle-in-a-haystack-style and long-document benchmarks spanning the *full* 256K range, not just the low end of it, and specifically within the target long-document-analysis domain; validate the final, post-annealing checkpoint against both the standard benchmark suite and the long-context suite together, checking for exactly the stage-interaction regression named in Section 4.7 before declaring the pipeline complete.

## 8. A closing checklist

1. Is the native pretraining context length chosen with an explicit cost/extension-factor tradeoff in mind (Section 2, Section 2.1), rather than defaulted to a round number with no stated rationale?
2. Which positional-encoding rescaling scheme (YaRN, NTK-aware interpolation, a base-frequency rescaling) is being used for the extension stage, and has its ramp/interpolation behavior been checked against the actual wavelength arithmetic for this model's RoPE configuration (Section 3.2a), not just copied from another model's published parameters?
3. Is the extension-stage data genuinely representative of the target use case's long-document distribution, or merely "long documents" in a generic sense (Section 4.7)?
4. Has long-context capability been validated with dedicated benchmarks spanning the *entire* target range, not just short-of-target lengths (Section 3.3, Section 7)?
5. Is the annealing stage's data-mixture shift and LR decay being planned together, deliberately, rather than each decided independently by different parts of the team (Section 4.2)?
6. Is a WSD-style schedule being used specifically to decouple the "how long to train" decision from the "what shape is the decay" decision (Section 4.3), or is a fixed-horizon cosine schedule forcing that decision to be made prematurely?
7. Has the annealing mixture been checked for over-fitting to the specific evaluation suite the lab uses, via held-out benchmarks not used to guide its construction (Section 4.7)?
8. Has the final, fully-staged checkpoint been evaluated for stage-interaction regressions (e.g., annealing partially undoing extension-stage gains), not just each stage's own isolated evaluation (Section 4.7)?
9. Is there a clear, deliberate line between how "curated" the annealing mixture is allowed to become and where post-training's dedicated instruction/preference data begins (Section 4.8), or has that boundary been left implicit and untested?
10. If the disclosed cross-model pattern (Section 4.5) is being used as a reference point for this model's own staging plan, has the specific extension factor and target context length been checked against this model's own constraints, rather than assumed to transfer directly from a different lab's disclosed ratios?

## 9. Quick-reference glossary

- **Main pretraining phase** — the long, short(er)-context causal-LM training run consuming the overwhelming majority of total compute (Section 2).
- **Long-context extension phase** — a short, dedicated continued-training stage that stretches the model's effective context window via a positional-encoding rescaling, not a from-scratch retraining at the new length (Section 3).
- **Annealing / cooldown phase** — a final, short stage combining learning-rate decay to near-zero with a shift toward curated, high-quality data (Section 4).
- **NTK-aware scaling** — rescaling RoPE frequency components by different amounts depending on frequency, preserving high-frequency resolution while compressing low-frequency components more aggressively (Section 3.2).
- **YaRN** — a specific NTK-aware-style extension method combining a per-frequency interpolation ramp with a softmax-temperature correction (Section 3.2).
- **WSD (warmup-stable-decay) schedule** — an LR schedule with a constant plateau after warmup, decoupling training duration from decay-curve shape, and pairing naturally with a deferred, deliberately-triggered annealing stage (Section 4.3; full mechanics in `004_Optimizers_LR_Schedules_And_Hyperparameters.md` Section 2.3).
- **Stage-boundary regression** — a measurable capability loss caused by an abrupt, poorly-managed transition between stages (Section 4.7).
- **Extension factor** — the ratio between a model's target maximum context length and its native pretraining context length, used informally throughout this file as a rough proxy for how aggressive a given extension stage's rescaling needs to be (Section 7).
- **Annealing/post-training boundary** — the deliberate line between pretraining's curated-data cooldown and post-training's dedicated instruction/preference-data stages, both of which can superficially resemble each other but serve different purposes with different objectives (Section 4.8).
- **Curriculum learning (classical)** — the older literature on ordering training examples by difficulty within a single training regime, distinct from this file's coarser-grained, mechanism-driven multi-stage structure (Section 4.6).

## 11. Closing summary

The three stages in this file exist because a single, homogeneous training run cannot cheaply solve three different problems at once: broad language competence is cheapest to build at short context; positional generalization to long range is cheap to graft on afterward via a targeted rescaling, but only after broad competence already exists; and final-quality polishing via curated data and a settled optimizer state is cheapest to apply right at the end, when it can have outsized leverage on the final checkpoint without having to be sustained across the entire run. Recognizing this — that each stage is solving a genuinely different problem, at a genuinely different point in the cost curve, for a genuinely different mechanistic reason — is what separates being able to name "long-context extension" and "annealing" as buzzwords from being able to design, defend, and troubleshoot a staged pretraining pipeline under real constraints, which is precisely the level of understanding a staff research engineer is expected to bring to this topic.

A final point worth carrying forward into any related discussion (post-training, evaluation, or systems design): this staged structure is not a fixed, permanent taxonomy. As scaling laws, hardware, and data availability continue to shift, the boundaries between these stages — and even whether three stages remains the right number, rather than two or four — should be expected to keep evolving, exactly as the field's compute-optimal guidance itself evolved from Kaplan et al. to Chinchilla (`002_Scaling_Laws_And_Compute_Optimal_Training.md`). Treat the three-stage pattern described here as the current, well-evidenced state of the art rather than an immutable law of how pretraining must be structured.

A practical closing heuristic to carry forward: whenever a proposed pretraining change looks like it would require paying its cost across the *entire* main-run token budget, ask first whether it can instead be deferred to a small, targeted, late-stage intervention — this single question is what generated the extension and annealing stages in the first place, and is very likely to keep generating new, analogous late-stage interventions as the field's data, hardware, and quality bars continue to evolve beyond the three-stage pattern documented here today.

## 12. A note on what this file deliberately does not cover

Two adjacent decisions are intentionally out of scope here. First, *whether* a given model should target a long context at all, and which attention mechanism it should be paired with, is a decision made jointly with — but logically prior to — the staging question this file addresses; that decision process is covered in full in `003_Model_Architecture_Decisions_At_Pretraining_Time.md`, Section 3. Second, *how much confidence* to place in a specific extension factor, annealing-mixture composition, or WSD decay-trigger point before committing it to a full frontier run — beyond the risks named qualitatively in Section 4.7 — is developed as a full validation methodology, with proxy-scale sizing and staged canary runs, in `006_Pretraining_Ablations_And_Research_Methodology.md`; this file explains what each stage is for and roughly what it costs, that file explains how to earn confidence that a specific staging plan will actually work at target scale before spending the budget to find out.

## 13. One-paragraph summary

If only one idea from this file survives: every stage past the first exists because some capability (long-range positional generalization, final-quality polish) is cheaper to graft on late and narrowly than to pay for across the entire main run, and recognizing *which* mechanism (a cost argument, a positional-encoding limitation, a basin-settling argument) justifies each stage is what separates genuine understanding from reciting "extend context, then anneal" as an unexamined ritual.

Carrying this forward: every one of this file's three stages is itself a candidate for the same scale-transfer scrutiny this module applies everywhere else — a staging plan validated at a smaller model or a shorter target context is an extrapolation, not a guarantee, exactly as much as any architecture or optimizer choice covered elsewhere in this collection.

The three-stage structure documented here is also a useful lens for re-reading any single per-model doc in this collection: whenever a model's disclosed report mentions a context-extension figure or an annealing-style data-mixture shift, that detail is not an isolated footnote — it is that lab's specific resolution of exactly the two cost-driven problems (positional generalization at low cost, final-quality polish at low cost) this file derives from first principles, and reading it that way turns a scattered set of disclosed facts across many docs into one coherent, transferable pattern.

Applying this lens is also a good diagnostic for spotting genuinely novel disclosures versus routine applications of the standard pattern: a lab reporting "we extended context via YaRN at roughly 4-5% of main-run compute" is reporting a routine, well-precedented application of Section 3's pattern, while a lab reporting a fundamentally different approach to either problem (e.g., Mistral's from-scratch sliding-window design that avoids needing an extension stage at all, Section 4.5's table) is reporting something genuinely worth flagging as a distinct architectural choice rather than a minor variation on the standard staged recipe.

That same diagnostic is worth turning on your own future reading of new training reports as the field continues to publish them: expect the main/extension/annealing shape to keep recurring in some form, but do not assume any specific disclosed percentage, extension factor, or mixture description generalizes to a new model without checking it against that model's own stated native context, target context, and compute budget first.

Holding onto that discipline is what turns this file's three-stage pattern from a memorized fact into a genuinely reusable analytical tool — one that keeps working correctly even as the field's specific numbers, ratios, and favored extension mechanisms continue to shift generation over generation.

Treat every new disclosed figure as a data point to slot into this framework, not as a replacement for it.
