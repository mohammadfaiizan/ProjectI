# Data Licensing, Copyright, and Governance

## 1. Why This Topic Belongs in a Training-Systems Interview

Every other file in this folder treats pretraining data as an engineering artifact: something to source, filter, deduplicate, weight, and tokenize. This file addresses a question that sits upstream of all of that and that a staff-level candidate is expected to be able to discuss with precision rather than avoid: under what legal and contractual basis is a lab entitled to use a given piece of text (or image, or audio, or code) to train a commercial model at all, who gets to object, and what does an organization have to be able to prove about its own corpus if challenged. This is simultaneously a legal question, a policy question, and — the part most relevant to an engineering interview — a systems question, because the ability to answer "where did this training example come from and under what terms" at trillion-token scale is a data-infrastructure problem with no purely legal fix.

The honest framing to hold throughout this file is that the legal question is genuinely unsettled in most of the jurisdictions that matter, that reasonable, well-informed people disagree about how existing law should apply to a technology that did not exist when most of the relevant law was written, and that a good interview answer demonstrates the ability to state both sides of a live dispute fairly and to be explicit about what is settled, what is contested, and what is simply unknown (including facts a lab has chosen not to disclose) — rather than either dismissing the debate as manufactured controversy or asserting a confident legal conclusion in either direction. Nothing below should be read as legal advice or as this file's own opinion on how any lawsuit should or will resolve.

## 2. The Fair Use Debate

### 2.1 What "Fair Use" Actually Is, and Why This Is a US-Centric Framing

Fair use is a doctrine of United States copyright law (codified at 17 U.S.C. § 107) that allows certain uses of a copyrighted work without the copyright holder's permission, notwithstanding the fact that copyright otherwise grants the rights-holder exclusive control over copying, distribution, and the creation of derivative works. It is not a bright-line rule with a checklist that produces a deterministic yes/no answer; it is a case-by-case, multi-factor balancing test that a court applies to the specific facts of a specific use. The four factors a court weighs, none of which is individually dispositive, are commonly summarized as: (1) the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial in nature and whether it is "transformative" (i.e., whether it adds new expression, meaning, or purpose rather than simply substituting for the original); (2) the nature of the copyrighted work (e.g., whether it is a highly creative work like a novel versus a more fact-based work); (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion of the work used in relation to the work as a whole; and (4) the effect of the use on the potential market for, or value of, the original work. Because these four factors are balanced against each other rather than mechanically applied, and because "transformative" itself is a judgment call rather than a precisely defined term, fair use analysis routinely produces genuine disagreement among careful, good-faith readers of the same facts — this is a structural feature of the doctrine, not a sign that someone is arguing in bad faith.

It is important to be precise that this entire four-factor framework is specific to US law. Other major jurisdictions have materially different legal mechanisms that address an overlapping but distinct question, and it is a common and consequential mistake to treat "is AI training fair use" as a single global legal question — it is not; it fragments into a separate legal analysis per jurisdiction, each with its own doctrine, exceptions, and (in several cases) purpose-built statutory carve-outs that the US does not have an exact equivalent of. The European Union's Digital Single Market (DSM) Copyright Directive (2019) contains explicit text-and-data-mining (TDM) exceptions: one exception for TDM performed by research organizations for scientific research purposes, and a broader exception (Article 4) that permits TDM more generally but that rightsholders can opt out of by reserving their rights in an appropriately machine-readable way — meaning the EU framework, unlike open-ended US fair use balancing, explicitly contemplates a rightsholder-controlled opt-out mechanism as part of the statutory scheme itself, which is a meaningfully different structure from asking a court to balance four factors after the fact. Japan has a TDM-specific exception in its copyright law (Article 30-4) that is generally described as comparatively permissive toward machine-learning-style analysis of copyrighted works, reflecting a deliberate policy choice by Japanese lawmakers to encourage this kind of use domestically. Other jurisdictions (the UK, various EU member states implementing the DSM Directive with local variations, and numerous countries with neither a fair-use-style open standard nor a TDM-specific exception at all) sit at other points on this spectrum. The practical consequence for anyone reasoning about this space is that a lab's legal exposure, and the legal theory available to a claimant, genuinely differs by jurisdiction — a use that is defensible under a US fair-use analysis is not automatically defensible under EU or Japanese law and vice versa, and a single training corpus assembled from globally-sourced text can in principle implicate multiple, materially different legal regimes simultaneously depending on where the content originated, where the model developer is domiciled, and where the resulting model is deployed.

A few additional jurisdictions are worth naming briefly, precisely because they illustrate how unsettled and how actively contested this fragmentation is even at the level of national policy-making, not just courtroom litigation. The United Kingdom, at various points, has run public consultations on whether to broaden its own comparatively narrow existing TDM exception (which today applies mainly to non-commercial research) into something closer to the EU's Article 4 opt-out model or a US-style fair-use-adjacent standard, without — as of this writing — having settled on and enacted a final position; this is genuinely a live policy question in the UK, not a resolved one, and any specific characterization of "UK law says X" for AI training should be treated with the same caution urged throughout this section. China's regulatory posture toward AI training data is shaped less by a single judicially-interpreted fair-use-style doctrine and more by a mix of copyright law provisions, sector-specific administrative regulation of generative AI services, and government policy priorities around domestic AI development, producing a distinct and less directly comparable framework than the US/EU/Japan comparison above; this file does not attempt to characterize Chinese copyright doctrine in comparable depth, both because it is a materially different legal system and because confident specifics are more likely to be wrong than helpful here. The broader point these two examples reinforce is the same one made about the US, EU, and Japan: every jurisdiction is working through this question somewhat independently, on its own timeline, and a globally deployed model's training corpus can in principle be evaluated under several different, actively-moving legal standards at once.

### 2.2 The Strongest Version of the Argument That Training Is Fair Use

The strongest form of the pro-fair-use argument centers on the "transformative use" factor and proceeds roughly as follows, presented here as an argument to understand, not as a conclusion this file endorses. Training a language model on a copyrighted text does not, in the overwhelming majority of ordinary generation scenarios, cause the model to store or reproduce that work in a form that competes with or substitutes for the original work in the marketplace. Instead, the argument goes, the work is consumed as one of an enormous number of statistical training signals — one document among billions — that collectively shape a set of learned parameters representing general linguistic structure, world knowledge, and stylistic patterns, in a manner the argument's proponents analogize to how a human who has read widely is legally free to write new material informed by that reading without each sentence of the new material constituting an infringement of every book the person has ever read. Under this framing, the "copying" that occurs during training (reading the work into memory to compute a gradient update) is functionally and legally more like the intermediate, non-expressive copying that courts have found protected in earlier technology cases than like republishing or distributing the work itself.

Proponents of this view point to a line of prior technology-related fair-use cases as instructive, non-identical analogues: search-engine full-text indexing and thumbnail-image display cases, where courts found that creating a searchable index of copyrighted material, and displaying compressed thumbnail versions of copyrighted images as part of search results, served a different, transformative purpose (enabling search and information-location) than the original works' purpose, and did not meaningfully substitute for the market for the original works; and separately, prior TDM- and full-text-search-index cases involving large-scale digitization of copyrighted books for the purpose of enabling search and non-expressive analysis (rather than for reading the book itself), which some courts found to be transformative fair use precisely because the use extracted non-expressive information (e.g., word-frequency or search-index data) rather than delivering the expressive content itself to end users. Proponents argue LLM pretraining is analytically closer to this category than to conventional copying, because — again, in the ordinary case — a trained model's weights are not a compressed copy of the training corpus retrievable on demand, and a typical generation does not surface a specific training document's expression back to a user.

### 2.3 The Strongest Version of the Argument That Training Is Not Fair Use

The strongest counter-argument, again presented to be understood rather than endorsed, contests both the framing and the conclusion. First, it argues that regardless of what the model's output looks like, the *input* stage of training requires making a full copy of the copyrighted work (even if only transiently, in the process of tokenizing it and computing gradients from it), and that this copying is done at industrial scale, for a directly commercial purpose, by well-resourced organizations that in many cases did not seek a license or permission before doing so — and that copyright law has never required a copy to be permanently retained or literally reproduced in the defendant's output for the act of copying itself to be legally cognizable. Second, and more consequentially for the four-factor balancing test, it argues that the fourth factor — effect on the market for the original work — can cut against fair use in ways the "just learning general patterns" framing understates: if the resulting model can be prompted to produce text that competes in the market for the original works (for instance, output that convincingly emulates a specific living author's voice and style closely enough to serve as a substitute for commissioning or licensing that author's own work, or that provides functionally the same informational content a licensed subscription or article would have provided), then the use may be undermining exactly the market the rightsholder would otherwise have been able to monetize, which is squarely what the fourth factor is designed to protect against.

A closely related but analytically distinct concern — not one of the four fair-use factors itself, but adjacent to and interacting with the argument above — is verbatim memorization and regurgitation. As discussed in `002_Data_Cleaning_Filtering_And_Deduplication.md` (Section 6, "Memorization and verbatim regurgitation"), duplicated training sequences are disproportionately likely to be memorized and reproduced near-verbatim by a trained model, and this is precisely the scenario in which the "transformative, non-expressive learning" defense is at its weakest: a model that can, when prompted appropriately, output a long, near-exact passage of a specific copyrighted work is not obviously merely extracting generalized statistical patterns from that work — it has, in some operational sense, retained enough of the work's specific expression to reproduce it, which looks considerably more like the kind of copying courts have historically found infringing when a rightsholder can demonstrate it concretely. This is one of the reasons deduplication is discussed elsewhere in this collection not purely as a training-efficiency or quality lever but as a concrete mitigation with copyright-risk relevance: reducing duplicate exposure reduces the empirical likelihood of exactly the regurgitation scenario that undermines the transformative-use argument.

### 2.4 Where This Actually Stands: An Unsettled, Actively Litigated Landscape

As of the time of this writing, the question of whether training generative AI models on copyrighted material constitutes fair use under US law is the subject of multiple active lawsuits filed against multiple AI labs by multiple classes of plaintiffs (including news organizations, book authors, visual artists, and other rightsholders), in multiple courts, and the honest statement is that this is not resolved — neither in a way that establishes "training is fair use" as settled law nor in a way that establishes the opposite. Early rulings and orders in some of these cases (on preliminary motions, summary judgment on particular claims, or narrower procedural questions, rather than final adjudications resolving the entire dispute) have, in at least some instances, distinguished between different sub-questions rather than issuing one blanket answer: for example, some early rulings have treated the question of whether training itself on lawfully-obtained copyrighted text is likely fair use as analytically separate from the question of how the training data was acquired in the first place, with allegations that certain training corpora were assembled in part from data obtained through unauthorized or pirated sources (rather than through legitimate scraping, licensing, or otherwise lawful acquisition) being treated by at least some courts as raising a distinct legal problem — essentially, "was this specific use of already-possessed content transformative" being a different question from "did the defendant have a legal right to possess this content in the first place" — and having a strong argument on the former does not resolve the latter.

Beyond that general shape, this file deliberately declines to assert specific case names, specific rulings, specific damages figures, or specific outcomes with confidence, because the landscape is moving quickly, rulings are being appealed, new suits continue to be filed, and asserting precise case-level facts here risks stating something that is stale or simply wrong by the time it is read. The correct posture for an interview — and the correct posture for this file — is to be able to state the shape of the legal argument on both sides precisely (Sections 2.2 and 2.3), to be explicit that the doctrine itself is US-specific and that other major jurisdictions apply materially different frameworks (Section 2.1), to know that this is being actively fought out in real litigation with mixed and evolving early signals rather than a single resolved answer, and to resist the temptation — in either direction — to present a confident legal conclusion that the underlying law does not yet support. A candidate who says "this is obviously fine, it's just like a human reading books" or "this is obviously illegal, it's just theft" has demonstrated less legal and technical sophistication than a candidate who lays out the four-factor test, the jurisdictional fragmentation, the strongest argument on each side, and the current unsettled state of the litigation.

## 3. Publisher and Creator Opt-Out Mechanisms

### 3.1 robots.txt: The Pre-AI Convention

Long before AI training was a consideration, the web crawling ecosystem converged on a simple, voluntary convention for signaling crawler access preferences: the Robots Exclusion Protocol, universally implemented as a plain-text file named `robots.txt` placed at the root of a website (e.g., `https://example.com/robots.txt`). The file consists of a small set of directives organized by user-agent (the identifying string a crawler sends in its HTTP requests, which the site owner uses to distinguish, for example, a general-purpose search crawler from an image crawler from some other bot), specifying which URL paths that user-agent is permitted or disallowed from accessing, and optionally a crawl-delay hint. A minimal example: a `User-agent: *` block disallowing `/private/` applies to all crawlers that do not have a more specific matching block elsewhere in the file, while a separate, more specific block naming a particular crawler's user-agent string can grant or restrict access differently for that crawler alone.

The critical mechanical fact to understand about robots.txt, and the fact that makes everything downstream in this section make sense, is that it is a voluntary, non-legally-binding convention, not a technical access-control mechanism. Nothing about robots.txt technically prevents a crawler from fetching a disallowed path — the file is simply a plain-text request that well-behaved crawlers, as a matter of established internet etiquette and (for major, reputationally-exposed operators) self-interest, choose to check and honor before crawling. A crawler operated by an actor who does not check the file, or who checks it and disregards it, faces no technical barrier to ignoring it entirely; any consequence for doing so is reputational, contractual (if a specific agreement exists), or, in some disputed cases, potentially a basis for a legal claim under theories like breach of a site's terms of service or (more contested) computer-trespass-style statutes, rather than something robots.txt itself enforces.

### 3.2 Repurposing and Extending robots.txt for AI-Training Opt-Out Specifically

Once AI training on scraped web text became a matter of public attention and dispute, the existing robots.txt mechanism was a natural, already-deployed lever for site owners wanting to express an AI-specific preference, and it has been extended in a straightforward way: several major AI labs' web crawlers identify themselves with their own dedicated user-agent tokens, distinct from that same company's other crawlers used for other purposes (for example, a company operating both a general-purpose search-indexing crawler and a separate crawler used specifically to gather AI-training data can, and in practice does, give each crawler its own distinct user-agent string). This allows a site owner to write a robots.txt rule that blocks only the AI-training-specific crawler while continuing to allow the same company's search-indexing crawler to operate normally — a meaningfully more granular opt-out than "block this company entirely," and one that would not have been possible if a single shared user-agent covered both purposes.

Beyond this repurposing of the existing mechanism, the space has continued to evolve with newer, more AI-training-specific opt-out signaling efforts: proposals for dedicated metadata tags or HTTP headers that a publisher can attach to content specifically to signal "do not use this content for AI training" as a category distinct from "do not crawl this content at all" (since a site owner might be perfectly happy to be indexed for search but unwilling to have that same content used as AI training data — a distinction the original robots.txt vocabulary, built for a pre-AI-training internet, was never designed to express), along with broader content-provenance and consent-signaling standardization efforts aimed at making an AI-training-specific preference machine-readable and, ideally, more consistently respected across crawler operators. Two concrete, real efforts illustrate the direction this is heading without either having become a universal standard: a W3C community effort around a TDM-rights-reservation protocol aimed specifically at letting a rightsholder declare, in a machine-readable way, that TDM/AI-training use of their content requires a license (a direct technical hook for the EU's Article 4 opt-out mechanism described in Section 2.1); and, on the image and media side, the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) — an industry effort, with Adobe among its founding backers, building a cryptographically-verifiable content-provenance standard originally aimed at proving how an image was captured or edited, whose provenance metadata fields have increasingly been discussed as a candidate carrier for AI-training-consent signaling as well, precisely because it already solves the harder problem of attaching tamper-resistant metadata to a piece of media as it moves across the web. As of this writing, this specific corner of the space should be understood as actively evolving rather than mature or standardized: neither of these efforts, nor any other single proposal, has become the one universally adopted "AI training opt-out" standard analogous to how robots.txt itself became a near-universal convention over decades, and different labs, publishers, and standards bodies continue to implement somewhat different, only partially interoperable mechanisms, some of which may converge, be superseded, or fall out of use over time.

### 3.3 The Fundamental Enforcement Gap

Whatever the mechanism — classic robots.txt, an AI-specific user-agent rule, or a newer proposed tag — the enforcement model is identical and worth stating plainly: it is voluntary and reputational, not technical. A robots.txt-style opt-out creates no cryptographic, network-level, or other technical barrier that prevents a non-compliant crawler from accessing the content anyway; it works only to the extent that the entity operating the crawler chooses to check for and respect the signal. This produces a real and consequential gap between a creator's *stated* preference and what actually happens to their content in practice, for at least three distinct reasons worth separating. First, a crawler operator might simply not implement compliance-checking rigorously, whether through negligence, a lag between when a new opt-out mechanism is proposed and when major crawlers update their software to recognize it, or a deliberate choice not to comply. Second, and importantly independent of any single crawler's behavior, content very often reaches a training corpus secondhand — through a third-party aggregator, a dataset assembled and redistributed by an unrelated party (an archived web snapshot, a research dataset built by an academic group, a resold or repackaged corpus), or a downstream pipeline that consumed an earlier crawl before an opt-out signal was ever added to the site — in which case the party ultimately incorporating the content into a training run may never have directly crawled the site at all and therefore never had an opportunity to observe or honor the site's current robots.txt state, even in a hypothetical world where every crawler were perfectly compliant. Third, opt-out mechanisms are inherently prospective and site-controlled: they say nothing about content that was already crawled and incorporated into an existing dataset or already-trained model before the opt-out signal existed, which connects directly to the "already-trained model" remediation problem discussed in Section 5.3 below.

## 4. Licensing Deals With Publishers and Content Providers

Running alongside — and to some extent as a direct response to — the fair-use debate and the limits of opt-out mechanisms, a distinct and growing pattern has emerged across the industry: major AI labs entering direct commercial licensing agreements with news publishers, image and stock-content libraries, and other structured-content providers, paying for the right to use that content as training data (and, in some deals, to surface or reference it in product outputs) rather than relying solely on scraped web data defended under a fair-use argument. This is worth discussing at the level of "this is a real and growing category of transaction happening broadly across the industry" rather than as a specific, fully-documented ledger of deals, because the exact commercial terms of most such agreements — the dollar amounts, exclusivity provisions, and precise scope of permitted use — are generally not made fully public, and any specific figure that circulates in press coverage should be treated with appropriate skepticism about its precision and completeness rather than repeated as a confirmed fact.

The strategic logic on the AI-lab side is straightforward and multi-part. A licensing deal removes legal uncertainty for that specific content source entirely — rather than relying on a fair-use argument that (per Section 2) is genuinely contested and could in principle be resolved unfavorably in litigation years after a model has already shipped, a license simply grants the permission needed up front, converting an open legal question into a closed contractual one for that source. A license also secures *ongoing, fresh* access rather than a one-time snapshot, which matters disproportionately for content that ages quickly and where staleness materially degrades value — news is the clearest example, since a scrape performed once is immediately out of date, whereas a licensing relationship can be structured to provide a continuing feed of new content as it is published. Finally, a licensing relationship can provide access to content that is not obtainable via scraping at all, either because it sits behind a paywall or access barrier, because it is proprietary or exclusive to the rightsholder, or because the licensed version is more structured, higher-quality, or better-curated than what a generic crawl of the same publisher's public-facing pages would yield (for instance, access to a full historical archive, structured metadata, or a cleaner feed than what is visible to an anonymous web crawler).

It is worth being specific that this is not one uniform category of deal but several, tracking the specific content type involved. News and media licensing deals tend to emphasize the freshness/recency point above and, distinctively among the categories here, are the ones most likely to include the attribution/linkback and product-surfacing provisions discussed below, since news value is heavily tied to timeliness and to the publisher's own brand association with a story. Stock-image and stock-media licensing deals tend to emphasize the opposite side of the freshness argument less and the structured-rights-clearance side more — a stock library's entire commercial value proposition rests on having already cleared rights across a large volume of contributor-submitted content, so a licensing deal with such a library gives an AI lab clean rights to a large, pre-cleared volume of image or media content in one transaction, rather than the lab needing to negotiate individually with, or scrape and separately assess, thousands of individual photographers or illustrators. Academic-publisher and specialized-database licensing deals (covering, for example, structured scientific literature or specialized reference material) emphasize a third dimension again — access to content that is often not freely crawlable at all because it sits behind an institutional paywall, meaning a scraping-plus-fair-use strategy was never actually available for that content in the first place, making a license the only realistic access path regardless of how the fair-use debate in Section 2 eventually resolves for content that is more freely web-accessible.

The strategic logic on the publisher or rights-holder side is the mirror image. A licensing deal converts what would otherwise be an uncompensated (and, per Section 2, legally contested) use of their content into a direct, negotiated revenue stream — a straightforward commercial incentive, particularly for publishers whose traditional revenue models (subscriptions, advertising tied to direct traffic) are themselves under pressure from the broader shift in how readers discover and consume information, including discovery mediated by AI products rather than direct visits. Some such deals additionally include provisions for the AI product to surface, attribute, or link back to the publisher's original content when generating a response that draws on it, which introduces its own separate tension worth naming explicitly: attribution and linkback can, in principle, drive referral traffic back to the publisher (a benefit), but if a significant fraction of users are satisfied by the AI-generated summary or answer itself and never click through, the net effect on the publisher's traffic and downstream ad or subscription revenue can be negative even with attribution present — meaning the value of "getting linked" as compensation is itself contested and is a live point of negotiation, not a settled substitute for direct payment. This tension is analytically related to, but distinct from, the fair-use market-effect question in Section 2.3: a licensing deal is a voluntarily negotiated commercial arrangement addressing this concern contractually, whereas Section 2.3's fourth-factor argument is about what happens in the absence of any such agreement.

## 5. Data Provenance Tracking as an Engineering Problem

### 5.1 Why This Is a Systems Problem, Not a Legal Checkbox

It is tempting to treat "know what's in your training data and under what license" as a compliance exercise that a legal team handles after the fact, perhaps by reviewing a list of data sources before a model ships. At the scale of a modern pretraining corpus — multiple trillions of tokens, assembled from dozens of qualitatively different sources including raw web crawls, licensed corpora obtained under the kinds of agreements described in Section 4, user-generated content harvested from a lab's own consumer products, synthetic data generated by earlier models, and code repositories where every individual repository can carry its own distinct open-source license — this framing badly understates the difficulty. Being able to answer, credibly and specifically, "where did this particular piece of training data come from, under what license or terms was it obtained, and can we produce evidence of that if challenged in litigation or by a regulator" is a data-infrastructure requirement that has to be designed into the pipeline from the point of ingestion onward. It is not something that can be reliably reconstructed after the fact once data from many sources has already been merged, deduplicated against itself, and shuffled into training shards.

A reasonable first-cut mental model for what this requires is that every document (or, depending on granularity, every chunk or shard) entering the pipeline should be tagged, as a first-class field carried alongside the content itself, with metadata that at minimum captures: the originating source (which crawl, which licensed corpus, which internal product surface, which synthetic-generation run), a license or terms-of-use category, the collection date and collection method, and a record of what pipeline transformations (filtering, deduplication pass, format normalization) have subsequently been applied to it. A schema sketch, illustrative rather than any specific lab's actual implementation, makes the shape of the requirement concrete:

```
DocumentProvenance {
  document_id: string            # stable identifier, assigned at ingestion
  source_name: string            # e.g. "CommonCrawl-2024-33", "LicensedNewsCorpusX", "internal_product_Y_ugc"
  source_category: enum          # {scraped_web, licensed, user_generated, synthetic, code_repo, ...}
  license_or_terms: string       # e.g. "MIT", "publisher_license_v3", "public_domain", "unknown/unreviewed"
  collection_method: string      # e.g. "http_crawl", "licensed_data_feed", "product_export"
  collection_date: date
  opt_out_status: enum           # {none_observed, opted_out_respected, opted_out_flagged_for_review}
  pipeline_transforms: list      # ordered log of filtering/dedup/normalization steps applied
  contributing_source_ids: list  # if merged/deduplicated against other docs, which ones
}
```

The point of a sketch like this is not that any particular field list is correct or complete — it is to make concrete that provenance has to be modeled as structured, machine-readable metadata attached at the row/document level and propagated through every downstream stage, rather than as a paragraph in a paper describing the corpus in aggregate.

### 5.2 Why Deduplication and Mixture-Shuffling Specifically Threaten Provenance

The reason this is hard in practice, rather than merely tedious, is that the exact pipeline operations covered elsewhere in this folder as data-quality techniques — deduplication (`002_Data_Cleaning_Filtering_And_Deduplication.md`) and mixture weighting/shuffling (`003_Data_Mixture_Weighting_And_Curriculum.md`) — are specifically the operations that, if implemented naively, destroy provenance information as a side effect of doing their intended job well. Deduplication, by design, identifies documents (or near-duplicate documents) across the *entire* corpus regardless of which original source they came from, and the natural, storage-minimizing implementation keeps one representative copy and discards the rest — if that discard step does not separately record which sources also contained the now-deleted duplicate, the information "this content also existed in licensed source X" is permanently lost the moment the duplicate is dropped, not merely inconvenient to look up. Mixture weighting and shuffling then interleave documents from every source into training shards in an order determined by the sampling recipe, not by source — which is exactly the point of shuffling, since training on data ordered by source would introduce unwanted correlation between training order and source identity — but the direct consequence is that a training shard, inspected on its own, contains no inherent signal about which original source contributed any given document unless that information was explicitly retained as metadata traveling with the document through every one of these transformations. Once this metadata is dropped — often for entirely reasonable proximate reasons, such as reducing storage overhead, simplifying the training data loader's interface, or because an early pipeline version simply was not built with provenance as a design requirement — recovering "which original source contributed this exact token span that appears in this specific training shard" after the fact ranges from difficult to flatly impossible, because the merge and shuffle operations are not generally invertible: many different pre-shuffle corpora could, in principle, produce the same post-shuffle shard, and without a persisted mapping there is no way to distinguish which one actually did.

### 5.3 Why a Lab Wants This Capability, Independent of Which Side of the Fair-Use Debate It Sits On

The operational motivations for investing in rigorous provenance infrastructure hold regardless of one's view on the legal questions in Section 2, which is part of why this is worth treating as a genuine engineering discipline rather than a legal department's problem alone. A lab facing a legal discovery request or a regulatory inquiry needs to be able to answer questions about corpus composition with actual evidence, not with a best-effort reconstruction attempt built after the fact under time pressure — and the difference between "here is the metadata log showing exactly what we did" and "we believe, based on our recollection of the pipeline, that this source was probably excluded" is the difference between a credible compliance posture and a fragile one. A lab also needs provenance tracking to audit for a specific opted-out source having slipped through the pipeline unintentionally — for instance, confirming whether content from a site that added an AI-training opt-out signal after an earlier crawl was nonetheless carried forward into a later training run via a cached or aggregated dataset, which is exactly the kind of secondhand-acquisition gap flagged in Section 3.3, and which cannot be checked without a record of which crawl or dataset vintage contributed which documents.

There is a further, genuinely hard and only partially resolved practical question worth stating explicitly rather than glossing over: complying with an opt-out request or a legal demand to stop using a specific source is straightforward for *future* training runs (simply exclude the source going forward) but is a fundamentally different and much harder problem for a model that has *already been trained* on that source. Once a document has contributed to a set of trained weights through however many gradient updates it participated in, there is, as of current publicly known technique, no clean technical mechanism to selectively identify and remove that document's specific influence from the already-trained model — this is not the same kind of operation as deleting a row from a database or a document from a search index, because a trained model's weights are not a lookup structure that maps cleanly back to individual training examples; the influence of any one document is smeared, in a way that is not straightforwardly reversible, across billions of parameters shared with every other training example. Approaches like machine unlearning are an active area of research aimed at approximating this kind of selective removal, but should be understood as an open research problem rather than a mature, reliable, production-grade capability — meaning that in practice, the only fully reliable remediation available today for "this data should never have been in the model" is retraining without that source, which is enormously expensive at frontier scale and is not something a lab can do casually in response to every individual opt-out request. This asymmetry — cheap to prevent going forward, extremely expensive or practically infeasible to undo after the fact — is itself a strong argument, independent of any legal conclusion, for why provenance tracking and opt-out compliance are better handled proactively at ingestion time than reactively after a model has already been trained.

Finally, and with no legal or compliance motivation required at all, rigorous provenance tracking is simply good data science. A research team that can slice its own training corpus precisely by source, license category, collection vintage, and pipeline history can run much more informative ablations about which sources actually drive which capabilities, diagnose quality or bias issues back to a specific contributing source rather than to the corpus in aggregate, and make deliberate, evidence-based decisions about mixture weighting (the subject of `003_Data_Mixture_Weighting_And_Curriculum.md`) rather than guessing. Knowing precisely what is in the corpus is a capability a lab wants for its own research productivity, and the fact that it also happens to serve legal and governance needs is a case of one investment serving two purposes rather than governance imposing a pure engineering tax.

### 5.4 A Concrete Illustration of Heterogeneity: Code Repositories

Source code deserves specific mention because it is the cleanest illustration of why "license category" cannot be a single corpus-wide tag. Unlike a web crawl, where an entire domain or crawl vintage can reasonably be tagged with one license posture, a corpus of scraped source code is, by construction, an aggregation of individually-licensed units at the repository (and sometimes file) level: one repository might carry a permissive license (MIT, Apache 2.0, BSD) that imposes essentially no restriction relevant to training use beyond attribution; a second might carry a copyleft license (GPL, AGPL) whose terms are, at minimum, designed to propagate obligations onto derivative works in ways whose applicability to a trained model's weights or generated completions is itself a genuinely debated question rather than a settled one; a third might have no explicit license file at all, which under default copyright rules generally means the author has reserved all rights rather than granted any, notwithstanding the code being publicly viewable on a hosting platform; and a fourth might be a fork or vendored copy of someone else's code carrying whatever license the original author chose, which the immediate repository's own top-level license file may or may not accurately reflect. A provenance system for a code-heavy corpus therefore needs the license field to be tracked per repository (ideally per file, since a single repository can itself vendor sub-directories under different licenses than its own top-level license), commonly by parsing or matching against a standard identifier scheme such as SPDX license identifiers, rather than assuming a single blanket policy ("we only use permissively licensed code") is self-enforcing simply because it was stated as an intended filtering rule — the rule is only as good as the pipeline's actual ability to correctly classify every ingested repository's license, which is itself a nontrivial detection problem given how inconsistently real-world repositories declare, update, or omit license metadata. This is also the specific area where the memorization concern from Section 2.3 has a particularly concrete manifestation: a model that reproduces a long, distinctive, verbatim span of a specific copyleft-licensed file in its output raises a sharper version of the same regurgitation concern raised for prose text, because the copied span may carry specific, actively-litigated license obligations that a generic prose passage would not.

### 5.5 Synthetic Data Adds a Recursive Governance Layer

Synthetic data — text generated by an earlier model and then used, often after filtering, as training data for a later model — is increasingly a first-class component of frontier pretraining and post-training mixtures, and it introduces a provenance question that is structurally different from anything in the scraped-web or licensed-corpus categories: the "source" of a synthetic example is not a website or a publisher but another model, and that upstream model's own terms of use may impose restrictions on exactly this kind of reuse. Several major providers' terms of service include clauses restricting the use of that provider's model outputs to train a competing model, which means a synthetic-data pipeline that (deliberately or inadvertently) incorporates outputs sampled from another lab's API is not merely a data-quality question but a distinct contractual-compliance question layered on top of the copyright questions discussed throughout this file — and one that is generally governed by ordinary contract law (the terms of service both parties agreed to) rather than by copyright's fair-use analysis at all, since the relevant restriction is contractual, not (necessarily) a copyright claim over the output text itself. A provenance system that treats "synthetic" as a single undifferentiated source category is therefore insufficiently granular for this purpose; it needs to record, at minimum, which upstream model or system generated each synthetic example and under what terms that generation occurred, mirroring the same "first-class metadata carried from ingestion onward" requirement described in Section 5.1, just applied recursively to a data source that is itself a model's output rather than a document scraped from the web.

## 6. "Publicly Available Data Only" as a Deliberate Design Constraint: The Llama 1 Case Study

### 6.1 What the Llama 1 Paper Actually Says and Does

Meta's Llama 1 paper (Touvron et al., 2023) provides one of the clearest documented instances of a lab treating data provenance as a first-order design constraint rather than an afterthought. As detailed in `..\..\OpenSource\001_Llama1.md`, the paper trains exclusively on a seven-source mixture — CommonCrawl (67% of tokens), C4 (15%), GitHub (4.5%), Wikipedia in 20 languages (4.5%), Books consisting of Project Gutenberg plus the Books3 section of ThePile (4.5%), ArXiv (2.5%), and StackExchange (2%) — and the paper is explicit that this sourcing choice is deliberate: every constituent source was selected specifically because it is, in the paper's own framing, "publicly available and compatible with open sourcing," in direct contrast to contemporaneous large models of the same era. GPT-3, PaLM, and Chinchilla/Gopher (DeepMind's MassiveText corpus) were each trained on a large, undisclosed proprietary web-and-books mixture or a corpus that was never itself publicly released, meaning none of those efforts made an equivalent public commitment to sourcing exclusively from data any outside party could, in principle, identify and inspect the provenance of.

### 6.2 The Stated and the Inferred Rationale, Kept Separate

It is worth being precise about which parts of the rationale for this choice come directly from the paper and which are reasonable inference about Meta's broader strategic motivations, since blending the two risks overstating what is actually confirmed. The Llama 1 paper's own framing centers on reproducibility and openness: because every source is publicly available, the training recipe is, in principle, auditable and largely replicable by outside researchers without requiring access to a proprietary dataset they could never obtain — a sharp contrast with a paper describing a training run over an undisclosed private corpus, where even a reader who fully understood every architectural and hyperparameter detail could not actually reproduce the data-side half of the recipe. This directly explains why Llama 1 became, in practice, the substrate the open-model ecosystem built on top of (instruction-tuned derivatives like Alpaca and Vicuna, and the broader "Llama-block" architectural lineage) even though the weights themselves were released under a noncommercial research license rather than a fully permissive one: the paper's data story was legible and reproducible in a way its contemporaries' were not, independent of the separate question of what license governed the released weights.

Beyond that reproducibility rationale, which the paper itself foregrounds, it is reasonable — but should be flagged explicitly as this file's own inference rather than a claim the paper makes in so many words — to read two further strategic considerations into the choice. First, sourcing from data that is publicly available plausibly reduces (though, and this is an important precision, does not eliminate) legal and reputational risk relative to relying on data known to be proprietary or, more pointedly, known to be pirated — a meaningfully different risk posture than a corpus assembled without regard to provenance. Second, a defensible, openly-describable data story is a more valuable strategic asset for a lab whose broader bet is on releasing open weights than it would be for a lab operating a closed-API-only product, where the training data recipe never has to be published in enough detail for outside scrutiny to be possible in the first place — if you are going to publish the recipe at all, having a recipe you are comfortable publishing in detail is a precondition, and Meta's choice of sourcing plausibly reflects that constraint operating in reverse (the data-sourcing decision was made compatible with the eventual disclosure decision, or vice versa) even though the paper does not narrate its own motivations in exactly these terms.

### 6.3 "Publicly Available" Is Not the Same Legal Category as "Licensed" or "Cleared for Commercial Use" — A Common Point of Confusion

This distinction is important enough to state explicitly, because it is a frequent source of confused thinking about this topic: "publicly available" (meaning a member of the public could, without special access, technically obtain a copy of the content) is not the same legal category as "in the public domain" (meaning not subject to copyright at all), nor the same as "openly licensed" (meaning covered by a license that explicitly grants the specific right being exercised), nor the same as "cleared for use in commercial model training" (a use-specific permission that neither public availability nor even an open license necessarily grants, depending on the license's terms). A document being visible on the open web, or a book being posted to a publicly-reachable file-sharing site, says nothing by itself about whether the copyright holder has authorized that particular use of it, and "public availability" was, in the Llama 1 paper's own framing, a description of accessibility and sourcing practice, not an assertion of comprehensive legal clearance for every downstream use.

This distinction becomes concrete rather than merely academic when looking at the Books component of Llama 1's own mixture. As `001_Pretraining_Data_Sources_And_Composition.md` (Section 5.2) notes, the Books slice combines two sources with meaningfully different provenance: Project Gutenberg, whose contents are unambiguously public-domain works, and the Books3 component of EleutherAI's ThePile, which is a shadow-library-derived corpus of book text — a source that later scrutiny treated as considerably more legally contestable than Gutenberg's, notwithstanding that both were, in the literal sense the paper used, "publicly available" at the time the corpus was assembled. This is precisely the gap the distinction above is meant to name: two sources can both satisfy a "publicly accessible" sourcing bar while sitting in very different places with respect to the underlying copyright question, and a paper's stated commitment to public-availability sourcing should not be read as an implicit claim that every included source was equally well-cleared from a licensing standpoint.

### 6.4 The Later Drift: Open Weights and Open Data Recipes Are Not a Package Deal

It is worth noting, as a general pattern rather than a claim requiring new deep research, that later open-weight models have generally disclosed progressively less granular detail about training-data composition even while remaining open-weight, or in some cases more open-weight (in the sense of permissive licensing) than Llama 1 itself. `001_Pretraining_Data_Sources_And_Composition.md` (Section 5.3) documents this concretely for Llama 3, which trained on more than 15 trillion tokens — over a tenfold increase over Llama 1 — but whose paper describes composition qualitatively (more code, more multilingual data, a data-annealing final stage) rather than with a per-source percentage table analogous to Llama 1's seven-row breakdown; the same source file notes a similar or more pronounced disclosure gap for DeepSeek-V3 and, to a lesser extent, Qwen2.5. The observation worth carrying forward here is that "open weights" (the model's parameters are downloadable and usable) and "open, transparent training-data recipe" (the exact composition and provenance of the training corpus is disclosed in detail) are two independent axes of openness, not a package deal that necessarily moves together — Llama 1 scored highly on both axes simultaneously largely because its comparatively modest scale and single-lab-research framing made a fully itemized, publicly-defensible data story both achievable and strategically valuable, while later, larger, more commercially consequential efforts across the industry have generally kept the weights open while disclosing markedly less about the data side, for reasons the underlying papers do not fully explain and that this file, consistent with the source material it draws on, treats as an open question rather than something to resolve with unfounded speculation about any single lab's motive.

## 7. A Related but Separate Question: Copyright at the Output Side

Everything in Sections 2 through 6 concerns the *input* side of copyright: what a lab may lawfully do with copyrighted material when assembling and training on a corpus. There is a second, analytically distinct set of copyright questions on the *output* side — what happens when a trained model generates something — that a staff-level conversation should be able to keep separate from the training-data debate rather than collapsing the two together, since they involve different legal doctrines, different plaintiffs, and in some cases different bodies (courts versus copyright registration offices) entirely.

The first output-side question is whether AI-generated content can itself be protected by copyright at all. In the United States, the Copyright Office's current guidance and a number of court decisions addressing registration disputes have taken the position that copyright protection requires a sufficient degree of human authorship, and that purely machine-generated output, without meaningful human creative control over the specific expressive choices in the final work, is not eligible for copyright registration — meaning content that is generated by a model with minimal human curation may, under this view, not be protectable by anyone, a conclusion with real practical consequences for a company or individual hoping to claim exclusive rights over AI-assisted creative output. This is an active and evolving area of guidance and case law in its own right, with genuine open questions about exactly how much human creative input is "enough," and it should be treated as current-guidance-subject-to-change rather than a permanently fixed rule, and as another example of a US-specific position rather than a global one — other jurisdictions have not uniformly converged on the same human-authorship requirement.

The second output-side question is closer in spirit to the input-side debate but legally distinct: whether a specific piece of AI-generated output infringes a specific copyrighted work, which turns on ordinary substantial-similarity analysis (does the output copy protectable expression from an identifiable prior work) rather than on whether the training process itself was fair use. This is exactly the scenario the memorization discussion in Section 2.3 and the code-license discussion in Section 5.4 point toward: a model could, in principle, have been trained under a defensible fair-use theory and still produce a specific output, on a specific prompt, that infringes a specific work if that output reproduces enough of the work's protectable expression — the two questions (was training fair use; is this particular output infringing) are litigated and analyzed separately, and a strong answer on training-data fair use does not automatically resolve, and is not a defense to, a claim that a specific generated output is itself an infringing copy. Keeping this separation explicit is a good habit for exactly the same reason the acquisition-versus-use distinction in Section 2.4 is worth keeping explicit: collapsing analytically distinct legal questions into one blended intuition is precisely the kind of imprecision a staff-level answer should avoid.

## 8. What a Staff Interviewer Is Actually Probing For

A question in this area is rarely, at the staff level, actually asking whether the candidate has memorized case names or statutory text — it is probing for something more structural: whether the candidate can hold a genuinely contested, high-stakes, and still-unresolved legal and policy question with the same intellectual discipline they would bring to an unresolved technical question, stating the strongest form of each side's argument fairly, being explicit about what is settled law versus what is live litigation versus what is simply the candidate's own reasonable inference, and resisting the pull toward a confident, tidy conclusion the underlying facts do not yet support in either direction. A candidate who dismisses the copyright debate as obviously overblown, or who asserts with confidence that training on scraped copyrighted text is settled to be illegal (or settled to be fine), has revealed a weaker grasp of the actual landscape than one who can lay out the four-factor test, name the jurisdictional fragmentation between US fair use and frameworks like the EU's TDM exceptions, and say plainly that courts are actively working through this and have not converged. Equally, the interviewer is probing whether the candidate understands data governance and provenance tracking as a genuine, difficult, first-class systems-engineering discipline — something that has to be designed into a data pipeline's ingestion and transformation stages from day one because operations as ordinary as deduplication and mixture shuffling will silently destroy the very information a governance function needs, and something that runs into real, only partially solved technical limits once a model has already been trained on a given source — rather than treating provenance as a paperwork exercise a legal team can bolt on after the fact. A strong answer connects both halves: the legal uncertainty is precisely why the engineering discipline of knowing, provably, what is in a corpus and where it came from matters so much, regardless of how any given court eventually rules.
