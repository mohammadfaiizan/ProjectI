# Data Mixture Weighting and Curriculum

## 1. Framing the Problem

Every pretraining corpus is, in practice, a union of heterogeneous sources: filtered web crawl, books, encyclopedic text, source code, scientific papers, forum/Q&A data, multiple natural languages, and increasingly synthetic or model-generated text. Call these sources "domains," indexed `i = 1..k`. Each domain has a natural size in the corpus — a raw token count `n_i` — which, normalized by the total (`N = sum_i n_i`), gives a raw proportion `p_i = n_i / N`, with `sum_i p_i = 1`. If you did nothing deliberate, the default way to draw training examples would be i.i.d. proportional to `p_i`: a domain that is 60% of your available tokens gets sampled 60% of the time, a domain that is 0.5% of your available tokens gets sampled 0.5% of the time.

The central decision this document is about is that model builders essentially never do this. Instead they choose a separate set of **training-time sampling proportions** `q_1..q_k`, which need not equal `p_1..p_k`, and which encode a judgment about the *per-token value* of each domain for the capabilities the final model needs, as distinct from that domain's raw abundance. This single idea — that raw abundance and training-time weight are two independent knobs — is the entire subject of Sections 2 and 3. Sections 4 and 5 then examine two specific, contested empirical claims that sit downstream of mixture design: whether code data has a causal effect on general reasoning, and whether the *order* in which a fixed mixture is presented (as opposed to the mixture itself) matters.

It is worth being explicit up front about why this decision is consequential enough to warrant its own body of research, rather than being a minor implementation detail. A pretraining run is typically a single, extremely expensive, largely irreversible bet: you do not get to retrain a frontier model many times to A/B test mixture choices at full scale, so mixture decisions are made under genuine uncertainty, validated at small scale (where the signal is noisier and does not always transfer) and then committed to at a scale where a mistake costs weeks of cluster time. This is exactly the kind of resource-allocation-under-uncertainty problem a staff interviewer will want to see reasoned about quantitatively, not just described qualitatively — hence the emphasis on the temperature-sampling math in Section 3.

One more framing point belongs here before going further: the choice of how finely to partition the corpus into "domains" in the first place is itself a design decision with real consequences, not a neutral preprocessing step. A coarse taxonomy (e.g., three buckets: web, books, code) hides a great deal of internal heterogeneity — "web" spans everything from carefully written long-form journalism to auto-generated spam — and any reweighting scheme can only act on the granularity it is given; it cannot discover a valuable sub-population buried inside a coarse bucket. A finer taxonomy (splitting web text by measured quality tier, splitting code by programming language, splitting multilingual text by language) gives the reweighting machinery more to work with but multiplies the number of independent weights that must be set or learned, and increases the risk that any individual fine-grained bucket is small enough to run into the over-upsampling/memorization failure mode from Section 2 if boosted. This granularity choice is logically prior to the temperature and automatic-optimization machinery discussed next: those tools reweight whatever domains they are handed, and are only as useful as the taxonomy feeding them.

## 2. Upsampling and Downsampling: The Basic Mechanic and Its Two Failure Modes

Given raw proportions `p_i`, "upsampling" domain `i` means choosing `q_i > p_i`: that domain is drawn more often per training step than its raw share of the corpus would imply. Concretely, if a domain occupies 0.1% of total tokens but is sampled at 2% of steps, then over the course of a long enough training run every document in that domain will be seen many times — the domain is repeated, i.e., trained for more than one epoch, while the rest of the corpus may not complete even a single epoch. "Downsampling" is the converse: choosing `q_i < p_i` for a large domain (canonically, raw web crawl) means that even though the domain is enormous, the training run only ever draws a fraction of it — most of that domain's documents are never seen at all in a single run, because the total token budget is fixed and a smaller slice of the per-step sampling budget is allocated to it.

The motivating intuition is a simple value judgment: not all tokens are equally useful for producing the capabilities you want. A token from a carefully edited Wikipedia article or a peer-reviewed math derivation plausibly carries more signal about the kind of writing, reasoning, and factual density you want the model to reflect than a token from a low-effort forum comment or a template-generated e-commerce page, of which raw web crawl contains enormous quantities. If quality (however operationalized) is unevenly distributed across domains, and if you have a fixed total compute/token budget dictated by your scaling-law-informed training plan, then proportional sampling is very unlikely to be optimal — it wastes budget on abundant-but-low-value tokens at the expense of scarce-but-high-value ones. GPT-3's own training mixture is the canonical early public example of this logic in action: Common Crawl, despite being by far the largest raw source, was sampled at a rate well below its raw share, while smaller curated sources (Wikipedia, curated books corpora, an expanded WebText) were sampled at a rate well above their raw share, on exactly this premise.

This lever has two distinct failure modes when pushed too far in either direction, and a staff-level treatment should be able to name both precisely rather than only gesturing at "getting the mixture wrong."

**Failure mode 1: over-upsampling causes memorization/overfitting on the upsampled domain.** If a small domain is upsampled aggressively enough that the model sees each of its documents dozens or hundreds of times over the course of a run, the model has the opportunity to memorize those documents nearly verbatim rather than learning generalizable structure from them. This is a real overfitting phenomenon in the classical sense — with enough repeated exposure to a fixed, small set of examples and enough model capacity, the loss on that domain can be driven down by rote memorization, which does not transfer to novel documents from the same domain or to the general capability the domain was meant to instill. This is one of the standard concerns raised around highly upsampled domains such as code (many public code-heavy corpora are much smaller than web-scale text corpora, yet code is frequently upsampled significantly beyond its raw share) and around license-sensitive or small proprietary corpora, where verbatim memorization also raises data-leakage and reproduction concerns distinct from pure capability considerations. The practical mitigation is not a single trick but a combination of things: capping the effective number of repetitions/epochs on any one domain, monitoring domain-specific training loss for the tell-tale sign of loss dropping far faster than validation/held-out loss on that domain (a classic overfitting signature), and, where the domain is small only because of scarcity rather than genuine unimportance, seeking additional raw data for that domain rather than repeating the existing set indefinitely.

**Failure mode 2: under-weighting a domain relevant to a target capability leaves that capability underdeveloped regardless of total token count.** This is the mirror-image error: because total training tokens is often the headline number reported and optimized against scaling laws, it is easy to lose sight of the fact that a fixed total-token budget says nothing about the *composition* of those tokens. A model trained on an enormous token count that happens to contain very little mathematics or code will not spontaneously become strong at multi-step quantitative reasoning just because the aggregate token count is large — the relevant capability-specific tokens were simply never shown to the model in adequate quantity, and no amount of additional web text substitutes for that. This is a subtler failure than failure mode 1 because it does not show up as an obvious overfitting signature; the model simply plateaus at mediocre performance on the underrepresented capability, and it can be hard to distinguish "this capability is fundamentally hard for a model of this scale" from "this capability was never given enough representative training signal" without deliberately running a mixture ablation. This is exactly the kind of judgment call that motivates automatic mixture-optimization methods (Section 4): the correct weight for, say, code or math is not knowable purely by inspecting raw corpus sizes, and hand-tuning it via intuition risks systematically under-provisioning capabilities the mixture designer did not think to prioritize.

The two failure modes also interact in a way worth noting explicitly: the obvious-seeming fix for failure mode 2 (just upsample the underrepresented domain more aggressively) is exactly what risks triggering failure mode 1 if pushed too far, and the two do not trade off symmetrically — a domain that is small because it is a narrow, low-diversity capability (e.g., a specific formal task with limited genuinely distinct source material) will hit the memorization ceiling from the repeated-token literature discussed above sooner, at a lower upsampling factor, than a domain that is small only due to an accident of what happened to be crawled but which in principle has abundant unexploited source material available if the corpus builders sought more of it out. Recognizing which situation a given underrepresented domain is in — genuinely data-scarce versus merely under-collected — changes whether the right fix is "upsample harder" or "go find more raw data for this domain before upsampling further," and mixture design that treats all small domains identically without asking this question is liable to walk straight from failure mode 2 into failure mode 1 while trying to fix it.

Both failure modes point to the same underlying fact: mixture weighting is a bias-variance-like tradeoff between "concentrate the model's limited capacity/updates on what you believe is highest-value" and "don't concentrate so hard that you either memorize a small set of documents or starve some other needed capability of signal." There is no context-free correct answer; the right weighting depends on what capabilities the model needs to have at deployment, which is a product/research decision, not something the data alone determines.

**How much repetition is too much? A quantitative anchor.** "Don't over-upsample" is not a fully operational instruction on its own — it helps to have at least an order-of-magnitude sense of where diminishing or negative returns tend to set in. Work on data-constrained scaling (most notably Muennighoff et al., "Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models," 2023) studies exactly this: given a fixed, limited pool of unique tokens, how many times can you repeat that pool before additional repetitions stop helping or start hurting, relative to the counterfactual of having that many additional *unique* tokens instead? The broad finding is that a modest number of repetitions (roughly on the order of up to about 4 epochs over a fixed pool, as a rough heuristic reported in that line of work — treat the exact number as approximate and specific to the setup studied, not a universal constant) costs relatively little compared to training on an equivalent quantity of fresh unique data, but returns degrade increasingly beyond that, and heavily repeated small pools can end up contributing less useful learning signal per additional epoch than continuing to draw fresh tokens from a larger, less-repeated domain would. The practical implication for mixture design is that "upsample the high-value small domain" has an implicit ceiling: past some number of effective epochs on that domain, the marginal training step is better spent elsewhere, and this is a testable, monitorable quantity (via that domain's held-out loss curve) rather than a purely qualitative judgment call.

**How domain value is actually assessed in practice.** Since the entire premise of non-proportional sampling is that some domains are worth more per token than others, it is worth being concrete about how that per-domain value judgment gets made in practice, since "value" is not directly observable. Three complementary signals are commonly used, none of which is fully sufficient alone: (i) *downstream evaluation correlation* — train small ablation models on candidate mixtures and check correlation with target benchmarks (the standard, most direct, and most expensive method, since each candidate mixture requires its own training run, even if scaled down); (ii) *held-out loss/perplexity on a trusted reference set* — use a small proxy model's loss on a curated, trusted validation set (e.g., held-out high-quality text) as a cheaper, if less direct, stand-in for downstream capability, on the premise that lower loss against a trusted reference correlates with the kind of text-modeling quality you want; and (iii) *learned quality classifiers* — train a lightweight classifier (logistic regression or a small fastText-style model, following the pattern GPT-3's own Common Crawl filtering pipeline used, distinguishing curated reference text from raw web documents) to score individual documents or entire domains for inclusion or reweighting, rather than relying on coarse domain-level labels alone. In practice these signals are used together and cross-checked against each other, precisely because no single one is fully trustworthy in isolation — benchmark correlation at small scale may not transfer to large scale (the same transfer-fidelity concern that motivates Section 4's automatic methods), and loss-based or classifier-based proxies can diverge from what downstream users actually value.

## 3. Temperature-Based Mixture Sampling

### 3.1 The core formula

The most common formalization of "upsample small high-value domains, downsample large low-value domains, but do it smoothly and parametrically rather than by ad hoc per-domain hand-tuning" is **temperature-based sampling**. Start from the raw domain proportions `p_1..p_k` (summing to 1). Define a temperature parameter `T > 0`, and set the training-time sampling weight for domain `i` as:

```
q_i(T) = p_i^(1/T) / sum_j [ p_j^(1/T) ]
```

The exponent `1/T` is applied to each raw proportion, and the result is renormalized so the `q_i(T)` still sum to 1 (they are, after all, sampling probabilities over domains). This single formula spans a family of mixtures indexed by one scalar, which is part of its appeal: rather than hand-picking `k` independent weights subject to the constraint that they sum to 1, you pick one number, `T`, and the formula determines all `k` weights from the raw proportions.

**A note on convention, stated explicitly because it is a genuine, recurring source of confusion in the literature.** Some sources write this transform as `q_i ∝ p_i^alpha` with `alpha` directly as the exponent, where `alpha = 1/T`. Under that convention, `alpha < 1` is the "flattening toward uniform" direction (equivalent to `T > 1` in the convention used here) and `alpha > 1` is the "sharpening toward the largest domains" direction (equivalent to `T < 1` here). Different papers and practitioners use `T` and `alpha` inconsistently, and some invert which direction counts as "increasing temperature." There is no universally agreed convention across the literature. The only defensible practice — and the one this document follows — is to define your exponent convention explicitly every time you use it (here: exponent is `1/T`, so larger `T` flattens, smaller `T` sharpens) and never assume a reader or a paper's abstract is using the same convention you are, since silently assuming the wrong direction is an easy way to describe the exact opposite of the intended effect.

This exact mechanic, under the `alpha`-exponent convention with `alpha < 1`, is a long-standing practice in multilingual model training predating most of the pretraining-mixture literature discussed elsewhere in this document — it is the standard way of deciding how much to upsample low-resource languages relative to high-resource ones when training a single shared multilingual model (e.g., in the lineage of multilingual BERT and later multilingual encoders/decoders), where raw web/document counts per language are extremely skewed (a handful of languages like English, Chinese, and a few major European languages dominate by orders of magnitude over most of the world's written languages) and naive proportional sampling would leave a shared multilingual model with essentially no meaningful exposure to lower-resource languages at all. Exact published exponent values differ across efforts and this document will not assert a specific number as universal — the qualitative point that matters is that this is the *same* smooth interpolation-between-proportional-and-uniform mechanic as Section 3.1's `T`, applied to languages as the domain axis rather than to content-type domains like code or books, and it long predates its use for content-domain mixture weighting in general LLM pretraining. It is also, notably, a case where practitioners sometimes want to move *toward* uniformity across many small categories (many low-resource languages) rather than toward suppressing them further — the reverse of the sharpening use-case mentioned for vocabulary/rare-category suppression, which underscores again that the formula itself is direction-neutral and the intended effect must be specified independently of which convention symbol is used.

### 3.2 Limiting behavior, derived carefully

**`T = 1`** recovers exactly the raw proportions: `q_i(1) = p_i^1 / sum_j p_j^1 = p_i / sum_j p_j = p_i` (since the `p_j` already sum to 1). This is proportional/naive sampling — no reweighting at all.

**`T → ∞`** approaches the uniform distribution across domains, `q_i → 1/k` for every domain regardless of how large or small its raw proportion was. The clean way to see this is to take logs. Write `q_i(T) = exp( (1/T) log p_i ) / sum_j exp( (1/T) log p_j )` — this is just the exponential form of the same expression, useful because it turns the exponentiation into a softmax over log-proportions with inverse-temperature `1/T`. As `T → ∞`, `1/T → 0`, so `(1/T) log p_i → 0` for every domain `i` — this holds regardless of how negative `log p_i` is (i.e., regardless of how small `p_i` is), because you are multiplying a fixed finite negative number by something going to zero. Since every exponent `(1/T) log p_i` converges to 0, every numerator term `exp((1/T) log p_i) → exp(0) = 1`. With all `k` numerator terms converging to the same value (1), the softmax necessarily converges to the uniform distribution `1/k` for each domain. This is the mechanism worth being able to state precisely in an interview: it is not that large and small domains "become similar in size," it is that the *log-proportions*, which is what the exponent operates on, get scaled down toward zero uniformly, erasing the very quantity (`log p_i`) that differentiated the domains in the first place.

**`T < 1` (equivalently `alpha = 1/T > 1`)** sharpens the distribution further toward whichever domains already have the largest raw proportions. Since `1/T > 1` in this regime, and raising a number in `(0,1)` to a power greater than 1 makes it smaller (proportionally more so for numbers already close to 0), small `p_i` shrink relatively faster than large `p_i` under the exponentiation, and renormalizing concentrates even more mass onto the largest domains than raw proportional sampling already would. This is the opposite direction from what most pretraining mixture design wants (which is usually to boost small high-value domains), but it does show up deliberately in some other contexts — for instance, in multilingual vocabulary/tokenizer training or subword-frequency contexts where a practitioner wants to deliberately suppress the influence of very rare tokens or very low-resource categories even further below their already-small raw share, rather than boost them. The point is that the same formula is used in both directions depending on the sign of the deviation from `T=1`; whether you want `T>1` or `T<1` is entirely a function of what problem you are solving, not a property of the formula itself.

### 3.3 Worked numeric example

Take four illustrative domains with raw proportions loosely modeled on a real pretraining mixture: a large filtered-web domain, a mid-sized books domain, a small code domain, and a very small math domain.

| Domain | Raw proportion `p_i` |
|---|---|
| Web | 0.700 |
| Books | 0.200 |
| Code | 0.080 |
| Math | 0.020 |

Applying the formula at a few temperatures (renormalized `q_i(T)`, rounded to 3 decimals):

| Domain | `T=1` (raw) | `T=2` | `T=4` | `T=10` | `T=50` (near-uniform) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Web | 0.700 | 0.490 | 0.367 | 0.295 | 0.259 |
| Books | 0.200 | 0.262 | 0.268 | 0.260 | 0.252 |
| Code | 0.080 | 0.166 | 0.213 | 0.238 | 0.248 |
| Math | 0.020 | 0.083 | 0.151 | 0.207 | 0.241 |

(These figures are the actual output of the `temperature_sample_weights` function in Section 3.4 run on this exact input, not hand-derived approximations, and each column sums to 1.0 up to rounding.) The qualitative shape is the important takeaway and is exact in general, not just in this example: as `T` increases from 1 toward large values, Web's share monotonically *decreases* from its raw 0.700 toward `1/4 = 0.250`, while Math's share monotonically *increases* from its raw 0.020 toward `0.250` — in this example Math's sampling share grows more than tenfold, from 2% of steps to nearly 21% of steps, purely as a consequence of the temperature dial, with no change whatsoever to how much math data actually exists. Books, whose raw share (0.200) already sits fairly close to the four-way uniform point of 0.250, first overshoots slightly past uniform (rising to 0.268 at `T=4`) before settling back down toward 0.250 from above as `T` grows further — a reminder that convergence to `1/k` is monotonic in the limit but need not be monotonic domain-by-domain at every intermediate `T` when there are more than two domains, since the relative ranking of "how far below/above the eventual uniform point a domain sits" can invert along the way. Code, an intermediate-sized domain here, moves smoothly from 8% to nearly 25% of steps. By `T=50`, all four values are within a few percentage points of the exact uniform value 0.250, consistent with the exponential-in-`1/T` convergence derived in Section 3.2. The practical reading: temperature is a single dial that trades off "respect the natural data distribution" (`T=1`) against "give every domain an equal voice regardless of how much of it exists" (`T→∞`), and most real mixture designs sit somewhere in between — enough temperature to meaningfully boost small high-value domains like code and math without going all the way to full uniformity, which would over-boost domains that may be small precisely because there is little genuinely distinct high-quality data available in them (over-boosting a small domain runs directly into the memorization failure mode from Section 2). In this toy example, something in the `T=2` to `T=4` range would be a plausible real-world design point: it roughly doubles code's share and quadruples math's share relative to raw availability while still leaving web as the plurality domain, rather than jumping all the way to treating all four domains as equally important regardless of their very different raw sizes and, presumably, very different total quantities of genuinely distinct underlying documents.

### 3.4 Reference implementation

```python
import math

def temperature_sample_weights(raw_proportions: dict, T: float) -> dict:
    """
    Compute temperature-adjusted training-time sampling weights from raw
    per-domain token proportions.

    Convention (stated explicitly, see Section 3.1):
        q_i(T) = p_i^(1/T) / sum_j p_j^(1/T)
    T = 1   -> recovers raw proportional sampling.
    T -> inf -> approaches uniform sampling across domains.
    T < 1   -> sharpens toward the already-largest domain(s).

    Args:
        raw_proportions: dict mapping domain name -> raw proportion p_i.
                          Values need not already be normalized to sum to 1;
                          this function normalizes defensively.
        T: temperature, must be > 0.

    Returns:
        dict mapping domain name -> sampling weight q_i, summing to 1.0.
    """
    if T <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Temperature T must be strictly positive.")

    # Defensive normalization in case raw_proportions are unnormalized counts.
    total = sum(raw_proportions.values())
    if total <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Sum of raw proportions/counts must be positive.")
    p = {k: v / total for k, v in raw_proportions.items()}

    inv_T = 1.0 / T
    # Work in log-space for numerical stability with very small p_i and
    # large 1/T (mirrors the softmax-over-log-proportions view from 3.2).
    log_terms = {k: inv_T * math.log(v) for k, v in p.items() if v > 0}
    max_log = max(log_terms.values())  # stability shift, standard softmax trick
    exp_terms = {k: math.exp(lt - max_log) for k, lt in log_terms.items()}
    denom = sum(exp_terms.values())
    q = {k: e / denom for k, e in exp_terms.items()}
    return q


if __name__ == "__main__":
    raw = {"web": 0.700, "books": 0.200, "code": 0.080, "math": 0.020}
    for T in (1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 10.0, 50.0):
        q = temperature_sample_weights(raw, T)
        formatted = ", ".join(f"{k}={v:.3f}" for k, v in q.items())
        print(f"T={T:>5.1f}:  {formatted}")
```

Running this prints, for example, `T=1.0` reproducing the raw proportions exactly (`web=0.700, books=0.200, code=0.080, math=0.020`), and each successive higher `T` moving every value measurably closer to `0.250`, with `T=50.0` landing within a percentage point or two of exact uniformity given how quickly the log-proportion term is attenuated for proportions in this range. Note the log-space, max-shifted implementation: computing `p_i**(1/T)` directly is mathematically equivalent but can underflow to exactly 0.0 for very small `p_i` and large `1/T` in floating point, silently zeroing out a domain's weight; operating on `(1/T) * log(p_i)` and using the standard softmax numerical-stability trick (subtract the max before exponentiating) avoids that failure mode and is the version you would actually want in a real data pipeline rather than a toy script.

### 3.5 How a target mixture is actually realized in a training loop

A common point of confusion is imagining that a target mixture `q_1..q_k` is realized by first physically reshuffling or resampling the entire corpus offline into a single dataset with exactly those proportions, then training i.i.d. over that fixed reshuffled set. That can be done for modest corpora, but at the scale of a frontier pretraining run it is usually neither necessary nor how it is actually implemented. The more common mechanic is: maintain the domains as separate data sources (separate shards, separate streaming data loaders, or separate sharded files per domain), and at each training step — or, more precisely, when assembling each micro-batch — decide how many examples to draw from each domain according to the target weights `q_i`. Concretely, a domain-weighted data loader either samples a domain index according to the categorical distribution `q_1..q_k` and then pulls the next example from that domain's stream, or, equivalently at the batch level, allocates a fixed fraction `q_i` of each batch's slots to domain `i`.

The consequence that matters and that is easy to get backwards: **`q_i` is a per-step sampling probability (or per-batch slot fraction), not a statement about how many total tokens exist in that domain.** If a domain's target weight `q_i` is set higher than its raw availability `p_i` implies it should get under proportional sampling, the practical effect is that the domain's finite pool of documents gets cycled through repeatedly — once the loader exhausts the domain's available data, it wraps around and starts drawing from the beginning again (this is the operational meaning of "upsampling equals repetition equals more than one epoch on that domain" from Section 2). Conversely, if `q_i` is set lower than `p_i`, the domain's data loader is drawn from less frequently per unit of global training progress; whether this means the domain is fully iterated once over an extremely long run (just spread out, contributing a smaller fraction of gradient steps per unit wall-clock) or whether a strict subsample is taken once and then the loader stops pulling from it depends on the total training token budget relative to that domain's raw size — for the large downsampled domains typical in practice (raw web crawl), the training budget is normally far smaller than the domain's raw size, so in practice only a fraction of the domain is ever touched in a single run, and which specific fraction is touched is itself a data-engineering choice (e.g., a fixed pseudo-random subsample selected in advance, or an online sample-without-replacement stream that would exhaust before the run ends if drawn from long enough). The important invariant to hold onto for an interview answer: the sampling weight controls the *rate* at which gradient signal is drawn from a domain over the course of training, and whether that rate implies "seen many times," "seen exactly once," or "seen only partially" is a separate arithmetic fact that depends on the ratio between that domain's raw size and the total token budget allocated to it under `q_i` — conflating "sampling weight" with "total tokens that exist" is the single most common way to reason incorrectly about mixture mechanics.

One further mechanical wrinkle worth naming: modern training pipelines almost always **pack** documents into fixed-length sequences (e.g., 4K, 8K, or longer token blocks matching the model's context length) by concatenating multiple, generally unrelated documents back-to-back (separated by a special end-of-document token) until a block is full, rather than padding every sequence out to the context length individually, since padding would waste enormous amounts of compute on padding tokens the model does not need to attend to. This means the notion of a "domain" at the level of an individual training example is really a property of variable-length spans concatenated *within* a packed sequence, not a property of whole fixed-length training examples — a single packed sequence fed to the model at one step can, and often does, contain fragments from several different domains simultaneously if domain-weighted sampling is done at the document level and packing happens afterward. Whether packing is done within-domain (pack a sequence's worth of documents from a single sampled domain, then move to the next domain for the next sequence) or across-domain (fill each packed sequence opportunistically from whichever documents the domain sampler yields next, mixing domains within a single sequence) is itself an implementation choice with second-order effects on things like attention-mask handling (whether attention is allowed to cross document boundaries within a packed sequence, which most implementations explicitly prevent via a block-diagonal attention mask or per-document position resets) — a detail adjacent to, but distinct from, the mixture-weighting question this section is about.

## 4. Automatic Mixture Optimization: DoReMi and Related Approaches

### 4.1 The problem with hand-tuning

The temperature framework in Section 3 gives you a smooth one-parameter family of mixtures, but it still leaves the *raw domain groupings and their relative priority* to human judgment — someone still has to decide that code deserves upsampling and by roughly how much, typically by running a set of training ablations (train several smaller-scale models with different candidate mixtures, evaluate downstream benchmarks, pick the mixture that looks best) and then betting that the ranking observed at small scale holds at the target scale. This is expensive in a specific way: each ablation is itself a nontrivial training run, the number of plausible mixture configurations grows combinatorially in the number of domains once you allow per-domain rather than single-temperature adjustments, and there is no guarantee that a ranking observed on a small proxy model transfers cleanly to the much larger model you actually intend to train (this transfer-fidelity question is exactly what Section 4.3 addresses for the automatic-optimization case, and it is an equally live concern for hand-tuned ablations).

### 4.2 The DoReMi idea: minimax reweighting via a proxy model

DoReMi ("Domain Reweighting with Minimax Optimization") is the best-known instance of a different approach: rather than searching over candidate fixed mixtures by trial and error, train a small, cheap **proxy model** and use signals from that training run to *algorithmically derive* a target mixture, which is then used (unchanged, or with modest adjustment) to train the actual large target model.

The mechanism, at a conceptual level appropriate for an interview answer (the exact loss weightings and optimizer details of the published method are a level of detail worth knowing exists but not worth memorizing verbatim): first train (or otherwise obtain) a **reference model** on some baseline mixture, which establishes, for each domain, a reference loss level — a proxy for "how hard is this domain intrinsically, or how much loss remains achievable on it." Then train a second, small proxy model whose domain weights are not held fixed but are instead treated as an adversarial variable, updated via a minimax / group-distributionally-robust-optimization (group-DRO) style rule: at each step, compute each domain's **excess loss** — the proxy model's current loss on that domain minus the reference model's loss on that domain — and increase the sampling weight on domains where this excess loss is largest (i.e., where the proxy model is doing *relatively* worse than the reference, controlling for how hard the domain is in absolute terms), while decreasing weight on domains that are already well-fit relative to the reference. Framed as a game: the mixture-weighting player is trying to find the domain weighting that makes the *worst-case* excess loss across domains as large as possible (pushing training attention toward the domains the model is relatively neglecting), while the model-training player is simultaneously trying to minimize loss under whatever mixture the weighting player currently proposes; the equilibrium of this game is a mixture that resists letting any single domain be badly underserved, which is a robustness property distinct from simply minimizing average loss across the raw mixture (average-loss minimization can happily sacrifice a small domain entirely if doing so improves loss on the bulk of the corpus, whereas the minimax framing explicitly resists that).

The proxy-model training run terminates with a learned domain-weight vector, and the empirical claim is that this vector — derived cheaply from a small model — can then be used as the actual sampling mixture for training the real, much larger target model, replacing (or informing) a hand-tuned mixture.

To make the "increase weight on high-excess-loss domains" instruction concrete rather than purely verbal, the group-DRO-style update this class of method uses is an **exponentiated-gradient-ascent** step on the domain-weight simplex — structurally similar to the softmax/temperature machinery in Section 3, but now the thing being exponentiated is an accumulated excess-loss signal rather than a static raw proportion, and the weights are updated online over the course of the proxy run rather than fixed in advance:

```python
def update_domain_weights(weights: dict, excess_losses: dict, eta: float) -> dict:
    """
    One step of exponentiated-gradient-ascent reweighting toward domains
    with the largest excess loss (proxy_loss - reference_loss), i.e. the
    domains the proxy model is currently doing relatively worst on.

    weights:        current domain sampling weights q_i, summing to 1.
    excess_losses:  per-domain (proxy_loss_i - reference_loss_i) for the
                    current step/batch; larger = proxy is relatively
                    further behind the reference on that domain.
    eta:            step size (a small positive learning rate on the
                    weight-update dynamics, distinct from the model's own
                    training learning rate).
    """
    unnormalized = {
        i: w * math.exp(eta * excess_losses[i])
        for i, w in weights.items()
    }
    total = sum(unnormalized.values())
    return {i: v / total for i, v in unnormalized.items()}
```

Each call multiplicatively boosts a domain's weight in proportion to `exp(eta * excess_loss_i)` and renormalizes — a domain with a large positive excess loss (proxy relatively behind the reference) gets its weight scaled up; a domain the proxy already matches or beats the reference on gets scaled down relative to the others. Run repeatedly over the course of proxy training, alongside ordinary gradient descent on the proxy model's own parameters against whatever the current weights imply, this is the minimax dynamic described qualitatively above: the weights chase whichever domains are currently worst-served, and the model parameters chase minimizing loss under the currently-proposed weights, with the two updates interleaved step by step. The domain-weight trajectory over the full proxy run is then averaged (or otherwise summarized) into the single fixed mixture handed off to the large target-model training run — the target run itself uses a static mixture, exactly as in Section 3; only the small proxy run's mixture is dynamic.

### 4.3 The surprising transfer result, and why it is plausible

The result that made DoReMi notable is that mixture weights optimized this way on a small proxy model, when transplanted onto a training run for a substantially larger model, were reported to outperform mixture weights that had been hand-tuned by domain experts working directly on the large model's own training signal — i.e., the small-model-derived automatic mixture beat a mixture tuned with the benefit of direct large-model feedback. This is worth sitting with because it is not the naively expected outcome: one might expect that a mixture tuned using the target model's own loss/eval signal would always dominate a mixture derived from an unrelated small model's training dynamics, simply because the small model's loss landscape and capacity constraints differ from the large model's.

The plausible explanation is a scale-invariance argument, and it is important to state it as a hypothesis about *why the result is plausible*, not as a proven mechanism: what the excess-loss signal is estimating is something like each domain's *relative difficulty or marginal value*, given how well it can currently be fit relative to some reference — and this relative-difficulty ranking across domains may be a comparatively scale-invariant property of the data itself (some domains are inherently more compressible, more redundant, or more informationally dense than others, largely independent of how large the model doing the fitting is), even though the *absolute* loss values obtained on each domain are emphatically not scale-invariant (a larger model achieves lower absolute loss on every domain). If domain difficulty ranking is approximately preserved across scale while absolute loss is not, then a signal that operates on relative/excess loss (as DoReMi's minimax objective does, by construction, since it compares proxy loss to a reference rather than using raw absolute loss) is exactly the kind of signal you would expect to transfer better across scale than a naive absolute-loss-based mixture choice would.

**This result should not be overstated.** It is a genuinely important empirical finding from one line of research, published at one point in time on specific model scales and specific domain taxonomies, not a universally reproduced law. Automatic mixture optimization along these lines remains an active research area rather than a settled, universally adopted industry practice — some organizations report using ablation-informed, largely hand-tuned mixtures validated against downstream eval correlation as their primary method, and full transparency about exactly which frontier labs use fully automated, DoReMi-style (or successor) mixture optimization for their actual flagship model training runs, as opposed to more manual processes only partially informed by such signals, is limited: labs are often not fully forthcoming about this specific methodological detail in public technical reports, and it would be inaccurate to claim with confidence that any specific named frontier lab's current flagship-model mixture is (or is not) produced by a fully automated pipeline of this kind. The honest position for an interview is: this is a real, influential idea with a real and surprising empirical result behind it, and it is reasonable to expect its influence (in spirit, if not in the literal cited algorithm) on how modern labs think about mixture design, but claiming certainty about its adoption at any specific lab's frontier run would be overclaiming beyond what is publicly disclosed.

### 4.4 Practical caveats worth naming unprompted

Two further caveats are worth being able to raise without being asked, since they are exactly the kind of follow-up probe a staff interviewer would reach for. First, the method's output is only as trustworthy as the **reference model** it measures excess loss against: the reference model's own training mixture and quality embed a value judgment (what "acceptable loss on this domain" even means), so the method does not remove human judgment from the pipeline so much as relocate it — from "hand-pick the final mixture" to "hand-pick the reference mixture that defines what counts as excess loss." A poorly chosen reference (e.g., one that is itself badly imbalanced across domains) can propagate its own biases into the derived mixture. Second, the domain taxonomy itself — how the corpus is partitioned into the `k` groups the method reweights over in the first place — is a prior human design decision the algorithm does not question: if two genuinely distinct sub-populations are lumped into one labeled "domain" (e.g., "web text" treated as a monolith when it actually spans wildly different quality tiers), minimax reweighting can only move mass toward or away from that domain as a whole, and cannot discover a more granular split the taxonomy did not expose to it. Both caveats point to the same broader lesson: automatic mixture optimization narrows the scope of human judgment required, but does not eliminate it — it shifts judgment upstream, to reference-model and taxonomy design, rather than removing the need for it.

## 5. Code Data and General Reasoning: A Contested Empirical Claim

### 5.1 The reported finding

A widely reported empirical observation across several pretraining efforts is that including a non-trivial fraction of source code in the pretraining mixture measurably improves model performance on tasks that are not about code at all — multi-step arithmetic and word-problem-style math, benchmarks probing logical/deductive reasoning, and even the qualitative coherence of chain-of-thought explanations on non-code prompts — relative to comparable models trained on code-poor or code-free mixtures at a matched total token count. This finding has been influential enough that essentially every major pretraining mixture for a general-purpose frontier model now includes a substantial code fraction (often in the range of several percent to over ten percent of tokens, though exact figures are inconsistently or only partially disclosed across labs) even when the model's primary target use case is not programming.

This should be stated carefully as "a commonly reported empirical pattern observed across multiple training efforts," not as a single definitively proven, universally quantified causal law with an agreed effect size — the exact magnitude of the effect, the code fraction at which it saturates or reverses, and its interaction with model scale are not matters of full public consensus, and different papers/labs reporting a version of this finding do not all use the same held-out reasoning benchmarks or code fractions, which makes cross-study comparison inexact.

### 5.2 Candidate mechanisms, presented as hypotheses

Multiple mechanistic explanations have been proposed for why this effect would occur, and the field does not have full consensus on which (if any single one) is the primary driver — plausibly several contribute simultaneously.

**(a) Code as a uniquely unambiguous, compositional, long-range-dependent domain.** Source code enforces precise variable binding (a name introduced on one line must be tracked, potentially across many subsequent lines, to be resolved correctly at its use site), explicit compositional structure (function calls, nested control flow, recursive definitions), and strict syntactic and semantic consistency requirements that natural-language prose does not enforce nearly as rigidly. Learning to predict the next token in code plausibly requires the model to maintain something functionally like a symbolic state — which variables are in scope, what a function's expected inputs/outputs are, where a control-flow branch will resume — over long token spans, and if the representations that support this kind of long-range, precise state-tracking are at least partially shared with (or transferable to) the representations useful for multi-step natural-language reasoning (which similarly requires tracking intermediate quantities, sub-conclusions, and dependencies across a chain of reasoning steps), then training on code would be expected to improve exactly the kind of reasoning benchmarks where the reported gains show up.

**(b) Code comments and docstrings as a natural bridge between informal and formal reasoning.** Real-world code corpora are not pure syntax; they are interleaved with comments, docstrings, and commit messages written in natural language that describe, at varying levels of formality, what the surrounding formal structure is doing and why. This paired data — natural-language description alongside a verifiable, executable formal structure it is describing — is a comparatively rare textual pattern outside of code (mathematical proofs paired with informal exposition are a partial analogue, but at far smaller scale in most corpora), and exposure to many examples of "informal reasoning grounded in a formal, checkable structure" is plausibly useful training signal for a model that will later be asked to produce informal chain-of-thought reasoning that should also, ideally, be internally consistent and checkable.

**(c) Code's relative freedom from the ambiguity endemic to web prose.** Ordinary web text is rife with vagueness, rhetorical indirection, sarcasm, contradictory claims across different pages, and simple factual noise. Code, by contrast, has an almost total absence of this kind of ambiguity at the syntactic level — a given program has one meaning under the language's semantics (bugs and ambiguous specifications in comments aside). One hypothesis is that heavy exposure to a domain with this property nudges the model's learned representations or "default mode" toward a more precise, literal, step-consistent style of token prediction, which transfers usefully into contexts (careful multi-step arithmetic, logical deduction) where precision matters more than the fluent-but-approximate mode that dominates casual prose completion.

**(d) A more skeptical, deflationary hypothesis worth presenting fairly.** It is not obvious that the causal driver is "code-ness" as a linguistic/structural property per se, as opposed to a correlated quality confound. Code-heavy corpora are typically sourced from places like GitHub and Stack Overflow, which — independent of the content being code — tend to be more curated, more likely to have passed some community quality filter (upvotes, code review, working/tested examples), and less likely to contain the kind of low-effort spam, SEO filler, or template-generated boilerplate that dominates large fractions of raw web crawl. If code-heavy sources are simply higher *average quality* by whatever quality metric matters for downstream reasoning performance, for reasons that have nothing intrinsically to do with the formal/compositional properties of code syntax, then some or all of the reported "code helps reasoning" effect could be a quality confound rather than a code-specific mechanism — and disentangling these two explanations cleanly would require a controlled comparison (matched-quality code vs. non-code corpora) that is difficult to construct and, to the extent it has been attempted, has not produced fully settled, universally agreed conclusions.

The appropriately calibrated position for an interview is: the empirical pattern (code-inclusive mixtures outperforming code-free ones on some non-code reasoning benchmarks, at matched token count) is reasonably well replicated across multiple training efforts and is treated as close to consensus practice, but the *causal mechanism* remains genuinely open, with (a)–(c) as the leading structural hypotheses and (d) as a legitimate confound that has not been fully ruled out. Presenting this as a single settled mechanism ("code teaches the model logical structure, full stop") would overstate what is actually known; presenting the empirical pattern itself as unreliable or unreplicated would understate it.

### 5.3 What would actually distinguish these hypotheses

It is worth being able to sketch, even briefly, what a genuinely disambiguating experiment would look like, since being able to design the test is a stronger signal than being able to recite the hypotheses. To separate hypothesis (d) (quality confound) from (a)–(c) (something specific to code's formal/compositional structure), you would want a matched-quality comparison: hold curation quality fixed and vary only "code-ness." One version of this is comparing a corpus of real, executable code against a corpus of comparably curated, similarly length-distributed *natural-language* text drawn from the same platforms (e.g., well-written GitHub README prose, or highly upvoted Stack Overflow answers with the code blocks stripped out and only the surrounding natural-language explanation retained) — if the reasoning-transfer benefit shrinks substantially once the formal code content itself is removed but the platform/curation-quality confound is held constant, that is evidence for something code-structure-specific rather than pure curation quality; if the benefit persists similarly without any code present, that favors the confound hypothesis. A second, complementary probe is varying code's *formal precision* while holding surface style constant — for instance, comparing training on real, syntactically valid and semantically consistent code against training on superficially code-shaped but semantically scrambled or corrupted code (preserving indentation, keyword frequency, and surface statistics while breaking variable-binding and control-flow consistency) — if hypothesis (a) is right, breaking the long-range compositional consistency should erode the reasoning-transfer benefit even though surface code-like statistics are preserved; if the benefit survives this corruption largely intact, that would argue against (a) specifically pointing to a shallower explanation (e.g., code's terse, symbol-dense surface form alone, rather than its enforced logical consistency, doing the work). To the author's knowledge no single published study has run precisely this full disambiguating design at frontier training scale with fully public results, which is itself part of why this remains an open question rather than a resolved one — the existing evidence base is closer to "code-inclusive vs. code-free mixtures, unmatched for these confounds" than to the fully controlled design sketched here.

## 6. The Curriculum-Ordering Debate

### 6.1 What "curriculum" means and why it is intuitively appealing

A training **curriculum**, in the pretraining sense, means deliberately controlling the *order* in which training examples are presented — for instance, showing simpler or cleaner text before more complex or noisier text, or organizing training into distinct phases by domain or difficulty — rather than drawing examples in a random, shuffled order from the full corpus (or from a fixed-mixture sampler as in Section 3) throughout the entire run. The intuition is drawn directly from human pedagogy: people are not taught advanced material before foundational material, and there is a natural appeal to the idea that a model might learn more efficiently, or reach a better final state, if simple/clean concepts are established first and complexity is layered on afterward, or if generalist competence is built before specialist competence.

This intuition also has some genuine grounding in the classical curriculum-learning literature from outside LLM pretraining specifically (Bengio et al.'s original 2009 curriculum-learning proposal, and a substantial body of subsequent work in smaller-scale supervised learning settings) demonstrating that ordering training examples from easy to hard can, in some architectures and tasks, improve convergence speed or final performance relative to random presentation order. The open empirical question this document is concerned with is narrower and more specific than that general literature: whether this effect, documented in various smaller-scale and different-task settings, holds up as a load-bearing practice for large-scale autoregressive LLM pretraining specifically, at the scale and with the largely-homogeneous next-token objective that characterizes current frontier training — and that is exactly where Sections 6.2 and 6.3's split verdict becomes relevant.

A further reason the curriculum intuition has historically felt compelling for pretraining specifically, beyond the general pedagogy analogy, is that early large-scale corpora were often assembled incrementally — a base web-scale crawl available first, with higher-quality curated sources (books, encyclopedic text, code) added or identified later in a project's data pipeline — so some early large-model training runs may have de facto exhibited staged data introduction as an artifact of when different sources became available to the data pipeline, rather than as a deliberately researched pedagogical choice. It is worth distinguishing that kind of incidental, pipeline-driven staging from the deliberate, hypothesis-driven curricula this section is actually evaluating.

### 6.2 The one well-evidenced curriculum-like practice: end-of-training annealing/cooldown

There is one specific practice in this space that does have fairly broad, documented adoption across multiple open model training reports, and it deserves to be described concretely and distinguished sharply from the more general, much less well-evidenced claim of a full easy-to-hard curriculum across an entire run. Several published pretraining recipes describe a **distinct final phase** — commonly called an annealing phase or a cooldown phase — positioned near the end of pretraining, in which two things typically change simultaneously relative to the bulk (majority) of training: the data mixture is shifted toward a smaller, more heavily curated, higher-quality subset of the corpus (upweighting sources like carefully filtered instruction-adjacent text, high-quality curated documents, or specific target-capability data, at the expense of the broader, noisier web-scale mixture used for the bulk of training), and the learning rate is decayed toward a small value (often as the terminal segment of whatever learning-rate schedule the run uses) rather than sustained at its peak/plateau value. The rationale combines two effects: a decaying learning rate naturally makes the model's parameters more sensitive to whatever data is shown late in training (since later small-learning-rate updates cannot easily be "undone" by even-later large updates the way earlier updates can), and deliberately controlling what data occupies that late, high-leverage window with a curated high-quality mixture is a way of using that leverage intentionally rather than leaving it to whatever the random draw from the full, mixed-quality corpus happens to serve up in the model's final steps. This is a genuine, real, adopted instance of "the mixture is deliberately made non-stationary over time" — it is curriculum-like in the sense of Section 6.1's definition (order/timing of exposure is being controlled on purpose), but it is a much narrower and more specific claim than "the entire pretraining run should follow an elaborate easy-to-hard ordering," and it should not be used as evidence for the broader claim.

This general pattern — a distinguishable final stage combining a decaying learning rate with an upweighted high-quality/curated mixture — has been described, in varying degrees of detail, across several openly published pretraining reports discussing multi-stage schedules (sometimes under names like "decay stage," "annealing," or as the terminal segment of a warmup-stable-decay-style learning-rate schedule). The exact curated-mixture composition, the exact fraction of total training tokens allotted to this final stage, and the exact learning-rate decay shape are details that differ across reports and are not standardized across the field — treat the specific numbers in any individual paper as belonging to that paper's own recipe, not as a universal formula — but the *structural* pattern (a late, deliberately non-stationary shift in both learning rate and data quality/composition) recurs often enough across independently published recipes that it is fair to call it a broadly adopted practice, in clear contrast to the much thinner evidentiary support for fine-grained ordering within the bulk of training discussed next.

### 6.3 The counter-position: for a fixed mixture, random shuffling is close to as good as elaborate ordering

Separately from the annealing-phase practice, there is a body of argument and evidence that, for the *bulk* of pretraining — the large majority of the run, prior to any such terminal phase — carefully hand-designed easy-to-hard or topically staged ordering of examples drawn from an otherwise fixed, stationary mixture does not reliably outperform simple random shuffling of that same fixed mixture. The claim is not that mixture composition is unimportant (Sections 2–4 establish clearly that it is very important); the claim is narrower and more surprising: once you have fixed *which* domains get *what* proportion of training steps, the further decision of *in what sequence* to present examples drawn from that fixed proportional mixture matters much less than pedagogical intuition would suggest, and a meaningful fraction of the benefit anecdotally attributed to "we used a curriculum" in various informal discussions is arguably actually attributable to the mixture-weighting decision itself (which domains, at what proportion — a decision the curriculum framing sometimes conflates with ordering) rather than to sequencing per se.

The structural argument for why this is plausible rests on properties of stochastic gradient-based optimization. Each training step computes a gradient from a mini-batch that is, under a fixed stationary mixture, an approximately i.i.d. draw from the mixture distribution `q_1..q_k` (composed with whatever within-domain sampling is used). Provided the mixture proportions are held stationary throughout the phase in question, permuting the *order* in which specific documents are drawn does not change the *marginal distribution* of what has been seen by any given point in training — at training step `t`, the *expected* composition of the roughly `t`-many examples seen so far (by domain, by rough topic, by whatever axis you would want to define a curriculum along) is the same regardless of the specific sequence in which those examples were drawn, because the sampling distribution generating each draw did not change. What ordering does change is the exact, specific sequence of gradient updates — but stochastic gradient descent is already designed to be robust to considerable per-step noise (that is the entire premise of using mini-batch SGD/Adam rather than full-batch gradient descent), and there is no strong a priori reason, and limited strong empirical evidence, that the optimizer's eventual trajectory is highly sensitive to the fine-grained order of an otherwise-i.i.d. stream, as opposed to being primarily sensitive to the marginal distribution being sampled from and the learning-rate schedule applied over time. This is precisely why Section 6.2's practice is the one place order plausibly does matter in a structurally different way: the annealing phase does not merely reorder a fixed mixture, it changes the mixture (and typically the learning rate) as a function of time, i.e., it deliberately breaks stationarity — so the "stationary mixture implies order-invariance" argument does not apply there, and that is exactly why it is the one curriculum-like practice with real evidentiary support, rather than a counterexample to the broader claim.

A second place where document *ordering* is a genuine, distinct engineering concern — worth naming because it is easy to conflate with the pedagogical-curriculum question but is actually a different problem — is long-context extension. When a model is trained or fine-tuned for extended context length, ordering documents by length (e.g., training predominantly on long documents, or concatenating/packing documents to fill long context windows in specific ways) is a real, deliberate choice made for reasons specific to teaching the model to use long-range attention effectively, not because "long documents are harder and should come after short ones" in a general pedagogical sense; this is a narrow, technically motivated exception rather than evidence for elaborate general-purpose curricula.

### 6.4 A balanced conclusion

Putting Sections 6.2 and 6.3 together: the strong, human-pedagogy-flavored version of the curriculum hypothesis — that pretraining benefits meaningfully from an elaborate, globally planned easy-to-hard (or otherwise carefully sequenced) ordering of examples drawn from an otherwise-fixed mixture — is not strongly evidenced as necessary or even reliably beneficial at scale, and the structural SGD argument in Section 6.3 gives a principled reason to expect this, distinct from simply lacking positive evidence. What is well evidenced, adopted broadly, and mechanistically distinct is a late-training *distributional shift*: reweighting the mixture (and typically decaying the learning rate) in a deliberate cooldown/annealing phase near the end of a run, which is a claim about deliberately breaking stationarity in the mixture over time, not a claim about fine-grained example-by-example ordering within a stationary phase. A staff-level answer should keep these two claims — "order within a fixed mixture" versus "deliberately time-varying mixture near the end of training" — sharply separated, since conflating them is the most common way this topic gets flattened into an overconfident, single-sided answer in either direction.

## 7. What This Topic Is Really Probing in an Interview

A staff research engineer interview question on mixture weighting is rarely testing whether you can recite that "code helps reasoning" or that "DoReMi exists" as isolated facts. It is testing two things at once. First, whether you can reason quantitatively about the actual mechanism rather than staying at the level of vague intuition — given raw domain proportions, can you write down and correctly manipulate the temperature-sampling formula, correctly derive its limiting behavior at `T=1` and `T→∞`, and correctly distinguish "sampling weight" from "total tokens that exist" when asked how a target mixture is actually realized in a training loop, since that distinction is where most informal reasoning about mixtures goes wrong. Second, and just as important, whether you can hold a genuinely nuanced, non-dogmatic position on empirical claims that the field has not fully settled — stating the code-and-reasoning finding as a replicated pattern with multiple live mechanistic hypotheses and an unresolved confound, rather than a single proven causal story, and stating the curriculum debate as two distinct claims (ordering within a stationary mixture, versus deliberate late-training non-stationarity) with different evidentiary support, rather than a single verdict of "curricula work" or "curricula don't matter." An interviewer who has actually trained models at this scale is far more interested in watching you correctly locate the boundary between what is well established, what is a plausible but unproven hypothesis, and what is simply undisclosed by labs that have not published the relevant detail, than in hearing a confident answer that erases those boundaries — and the two halves of this test are not actually separate skills: the discipline of stating a temperature exactly and deriving its limit correctly is the same intellectual habit as declining to collapse a genuinely two-sided empirical debate into a one-line verdict, which is precisely why this topic is a durable interview probe rather than a fact-recall exercise.
