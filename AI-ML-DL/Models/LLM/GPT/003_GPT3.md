# GPT-3

## GPT-3 (2020)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

GPT-3 ("Language Models are Few-Shot Learners", Brown et al., OpenAI, May 2020) is the third generation of OpenAI's decoder-only Transformer language model line. Architecturally it is a direct, largely unmodified continuation of GPT-2: same pre-norm decoder-only Transformer, same byte-pair encoding tokenizer family, same autoregressive next-token cross-entropy objective. The paper's contribution is not a new architecture — it is an empirical demonstration that scaling that architecture by roughly two orders of magnitude in parameter count (1.5B in GPT-2 to 175B in GPT-3) produces a qualitatively different capability: **in-context learning**. A sufficiently large pretrained LM can be handed a natural-language task description plus a handful of input/output demonstrations *inside its prompt*, with zero gradient updates, and perform competitively with fine-tuned models on many tasks.

Strategically, this is the paper that operationalized the scaling-law bet. Kaplan et al. 2020 ("Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," published by OpenAI a few months prior) had shown that test loss on autoregressive LMs follows smooth power laws in model size, dataset size, and compute, and argued that — under the compute budgets of that era — allocating additional compute primarily toward larger models (rather than proportionally more data or more training steps) was the compute-efficient move. GPT-3 is the direct experimental payoff of that thesis: take the recipe that worked at 1.5B and push parameter count by ~117x while increasing data by a comparatively modest ~5-8x (GPT-2 trained on ~40GB / ~10B tokens of WebText-derived data; GPT-3 trained on ~300B tokens). This ratio of parameter-scaling to data-scaling is the exact thing later work (Hoffmann et al. 2022, "Chinchilla") would revisit critically — see Sections 3 and 12.

The paper positions few-shot in-context learning as a third paradigm alongside fine-tuning and zero-shot transfer, explicitly framing GPT-3 as a step toward more general, task-agnostic language systems that do not require task-specific labeled datasets or gradient updates at deployment time. It is also, notably, the paper that made "prompting" a mainstream interface to language models and set up the commercial product (the OpenAI API, davinci) that GPT-3 shipped as.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

**Base block.** GPT-3 uses the same pre-LayerNorm decoder-only Transformer block as GPT-2: `x = x + Attn(LN(x))` followed by `x = x + FFN(LN(x))`, with a final LayerNorm before the output projection. The FFN is a 2-layer MLP with GELU activation and a 4x expansion ratio (`d_ff = 4 * d_model`). Positional information in the original GPT-3 is via learned absolute position embeddings added to token embeddings (same as GPT-2), not rotary or ALiBi (those come later, e.g., in GPT-NeoX/LLaMA-era models). The input embedding and output unembedding matrices are weight-tied, as in GPT-2.

**The distinctive mechanism: alternating dense and locally-banded sparse attention.** The paper states directly: GPT-3 "use[s] alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns in the layers of the transformer, similar to the Sparse Transformer [Child et al., 2019]." This is the one architectural deviation from vanilla GPT-2 worth dwelling on mechanistically, and it is explicitly inherited conceptually from Child et al. 2019 rather than invented by the GPT-3 authors.

- *Dense causal attention* (standard GPT-2-style): for a sequence of length `n`, every query position `i` attends to all key positions `j <= i`. This is the full causal self-attention with an `n x n` (lower-triangular) score matrix, cost `O(n^2 * d)` in both compute and memory for materializing scores.
- *Locally-banded sparse attention*: each query position `i` attends only to a restricted subset of keys — typically a local window of the most recent `W` positions (`j` such that `i - W <= j <= i`, still causal), optionally combined with a fixed strided component (attending to every `k`-th earlier token, as in Child et al.'s strided variant, so long-range signal is not lost entirely). This reduces the attention pattern per query from `O(n)` candidate keys to `O(W)` (or `O(W + n/k)` with a strided term), so a full layer's attention cost drops from `O(n^2 * d)` to `O(n * W * d)` (plus `O(n * (n/k) * d)` for the strided component if used). For GPT-3's context length of `n = 2048`, a local window `W` that is a small fraction of `n` yields a substantial reduction in the quadratic term for those layers.
- GPT-3 *alternates* these two patterns layer-by-layer (paper's phrasing: "alternating"). The dense layers preserve full-context mixing (any token can eventually attend to any other token through the network, since dense layers appear regularly), while the sparse-local layers cut the average cost per layer. The net effect is a compute/memory saving relative to an all-dense 96-layer stack at 2048 context, while — the design bet — not materially harming quality, since local structure dominates a lot of language modeling signal and the interleaved dense layers still provide full-sequence mixing every other layer.
- Motivation is squarely computational: dense self-attention is `O(n^2)` in sequence length, and at `n=2048` with `d_model=12288`, halving the layers' effective attention cost matters at this scale for both FLOPs and the activation memory that must be kept for backprop. This is precisely the problem Child et al. 2019 set out to solve for very long sequences (they targeted sequences of length in the thousands to tens of thousands); GPT-3's context of 2048 is far shorter than what motivated the Sparse Transformer originally, so this can reasonably be read as OpenAI reusing the mechanism opportunistically for efficiency at scale rather than because 2048 tokens strictly required it.
- **Caveat (flagged):** the GPT-3 paper devotes a single sentence to this and does not specify the exact window size `W`, stride `k`, which layer parity is dense vs. sparse, or give ablations isolating its effect on quality. All specifics beyond the direct quote above are not disclosed in the paper; what follows in the accompanying code is a pedagogical reconstruction of the mechanism as described, not a reproduction of OpenAI's exact configuration.

**Parameter count arithmetic for the 175B config.** Let `V` = vocab size (GPT-2/3 BPE vocab, 50257), `d` = d_model = 12288, `L` = n_layers = 96, `n` = context = 2048.

- Token embedding: `V * d` = 50257 * 12288 ≈ 617.6M. (Weight-tied with the unembedding, so counted once.)
- Learned positional embedding: `n * d` = 2048 * 12288 ≈ 25.2M.
- Per-layer attention: Q, K, V, and output projections, each `d x d` → `4 * d^2` = 4 * 12288^2 ≈ 603.98M. (96 heads * 128 dim/head = 12288 = d, consistent with the paper.)
- Per-layer FFN: up-projection `d x 4d` and down-projection `4d x d` → `2 * 4 * d^2` = `8 * d^2` ≈ 1207.96M.
- Per-layer LayerNorm params: `~4*d` (two LNs, scale+bias each), negligible (~49K).
- Per layer total: `4d^2 + 8d^2 = 12 d^2` ≈ 1811.94M ≈ 1.812B.
- All 96 layers: `96 * 12 d^2` = `1152 * d^2` ≈ 173.95B.
- Plus embeddings (~0.618B) and final LayerNorm (negligible): total ≈ **174.6B**, consistent with the paper's reported "175B" (small discrepancies from rounding, exact vocab size, and whether biases are included in every projection).

This arithmetic makes explicit why `d_model` dominates: parameter count per layer scales as `12 * d^2`, i.e., quadratically in `d_model`, while depth `L` only scales it linearly. Going from GPT-2 XL's `d=1600, L=48` (~1.5B) to GPT-3's `d=12288, L=96` — a 7.7x increase in `d` and 2x in `L` — accounts for roughly `7.7^2 * 2 ≈ 118x` parameter growth, matching the observed ~117x (1.5B → 175B).

**The GPT-3 family.** The paper trained and evaluated 8 model sizes to trace out scaling curves, not just the 175B flagship. Approximate configuration pattern (exact figures below for the smaller models are best-recollection / approximate — flagged):

| Name | Params | n_layers | d_model | n_heads |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 Small | 125M | 12 | 768 | 12 |
| GPT-3 Medium | 350M | 24 | 1024 | 16 |
| GPT-3 Large | 760M | 24 | 1536 | 16 |

Treat every row except the 125M and 175B endpoints (which are confirmed anchor points widely reported) as approximate reconstructions of the paper's Table 2.1; verify exact values against the paper if precision matters for a specific claim.

### 3. Scale -- Parameters, Data, Compute

- **Parameters:** 175,000,000,000 (175B), confirmed. 96 layers, `d_model=12288`, 96 attention heads at 128 dim/head, FFN inner dimension 49152 (4x), context window 2048 tokens. All confirmed by the paper.
- **Data:** ~300B tokens processed during training (this is the training token budget, not the size of the deduplicated raw corpus — the mixture below sums to more raw/available tokens than were actually seen, because of the deliberate up/down-weighting described in Section 5). Confirmed order of magnitude from the paper.
- **Compute:** the paper estimates the 175B training run consumed **~3.14 x 10^23 FLOPs**. This is the paper's own self-reported estimate (Table 2.2 / Appendix), not an independent measurement — confirmed as "OpenAI's own estimate," not independently verified.
- For scale context: GPT-2 (1.5B) was estimated in the same paper's comparison table at roughly 1.5 x 10^21 FLOPs, so GPT-3's flagship run represents roughly a ~200x jump in compute over GPT-2, consistent with the ~117x parameter jump plus a several-x increase in tokens processed.
- Ratio check (tokens per parameter): 300B tokens / 175B params ≈ 1.7 tokens/parameter. Under Chinchilla-optimal accounting (Hoffmann et al. 2022, ~20 tokens/parameter for compute-optimal training), GPT-3 is undertrained on data relative to its parameter count by roughly an order of magnitude — this is the well-known hindsight critique, discussed further in Sections 7 and 12.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

The GPT-3 paper is notably thin on training infrastructure detail — it states the model was trained on V100 GPUs on a "high-bandwidth cluster provided by Microsoft" (i.e., Azure), and gives the aggregate FLOPs estimate, but does not disclose GPU count, node topology, parallelism strategy, wall-clock training time, or software stack. **Everything below beyond that one sentence is systems-literacy reasoning about how one would have to architect training for a dense 175B-parameter transformer — not a disclosed OpenAI implementation detail.** This distinction is flagged throughout.

**Confirmed:** trained on NVIDIA V100 GPUs; cluster provided by Microsoft/Azure (a partnership publicly announced around this period). Nothing more specific than the ~3.14e23 FLOPs estimate is confirmed in the paper itself.

**Third-party estimate (flagged as speculative):** various outside commentators have estimated the run used on the order of several thousand V100s over multiple weeks, back-calculated from the FLOPs figure and plausible achieved hardware utilization (e.g., ~3.14e23 FLOPs / (V100 peak ~125 TFLOPs fp16 tensor-core throughput * assumed ~30-50% achieved utilization * seconds available) lands in the range of low-thousands of GPUs for on the order of a month, but this is back-of-envelope and not an OpenAI-confirmed figure).

**Why single-GPU or naive data-parallel training is infeasible at this scale (systems reasoning):**

- **Weight memory alone:** 175B params * 2 bytes (fp16/bf16) = 350GB just to hold the weights — already far beyond any single accelerator's HBM in the 2020 era (V100: 16 or 32GB).
- **Optimizer state dominates further:** Adam maintains fp32 first and second moment buffers per parameter, plus commonly an fp32 master copy of the weights when training in mixed precision. That is roughly `4 bytes (fp32 weight copy) + 4 bytes (m) + 4 bytes (v)` = 12 bytes/param of optimizer/master-weight state on top of the 2-byte fp16 working copy — i.e., ~14 bytes/param total, or **~2.45TB** for 175B params, before counting gradients or activations. This is the standard "mixed-precision Adam memory wall" that motivates ZeRO/FSDP-style sharding at this scale.
- **Activations:** with `d_model=12288`, 96 layers, sequence length 2048, and large batch sizes, unsharded activation memory for backprop would add many more terabytes; activation checkpointing (recompute-in-backward) is a near-mandatory technique at this scale to trade compute for memory.
- **Conclusion:** no single GPU, and no single 8-GPU DGX node's aggregate memory (even ~256-320GB with V100-32GB), can hold model + optimizer state + activations for 175B params. Multi-dimensional model parallelism is structurally required, not an optimization choice.

**How one would architect this (Megatron-LM / 3D-parallelism style reasoning, standard for this era and scale — not a confirmed OpenAI disclosure):**

- **Tensor parallelism** (intra-layer): shard each attention block's Q/K/V/output projections and each FFN's up/down projections column/row-wise across a group of GPUs (e.g., 8-way, matching heads divisible cleanly: 96 heads / 8-way TP = 12 heads per shard). This is the Megatron-LM approach — split the `d x d` and `d x 4d` matrices so each GPU holds a fraction of each layer, with an all-reduce after each attention/FFN sub-block to recombine partial sums. Communication-heavy, so TP groups are kept within a single high-bandwidth node (NVLink) where possible.
- **Pipeline parallelism** (inter-layer): partition the 96 transformer layers into stages assigned to different node groups, with micro-batched pipelining (GPipe/PipeDream-style) to keep pipeline bubble overhead down. This spreads depth across nodes connected by lower-bandwidth interconnect (InfiniBand across nodes vs. NVLink within a node).
- **Data parallelism** (across replicas): once a full model replica is assembled from some TP x PP grid, replicate that grid and split the batch across replicas, gradient-all-reducing (or reduce-scattering, in ZeRO terms) across data-parallel groups.
- **Optimizer state sharding (ZeRO stage 1/2/3, or equivalently FSDP):** rather than every data-parallel replica holding a full copy of Adam's moment buffers and fp32 master weights, shard those states across the data-parallel group so aggregate memory scales down by roughly the DP-degree. At 175B scale this is less an optimization than a requirement to fit the ~2.45TB of optimizer/master-weight state within cluster memory budgets. (Whether OpenAI used something ZeRO-like, a custom in-house system, or an early Megatron-only approach for GPT-3 specifically is not publicly documented; ZeRO itself was published by Microsoft Research contemporaneously with GPT-3's training window, and the Azure partnership makes some interaction plausible but this is speculation.)
- **Mixed precision:** fp16 (or bf16, though bf16 support was less mature on V100 than on later A100s) compute with fp32 master weights/optimizer state, loss scaling to avoid fp16 underflow in gradients — standard practice for this GPU generation.
- **Net picture:** a 3D-parallelism grid (TP x PP x DP) spread across a multi-hundred-to-multi-thousand-GPU V100 cluster, connected by a mix of NVLink (intra-node) and InfiniBand (inter-node), with activation checkpointing and mixed-precision Adam. This is the standard systems-interview answer for "how do you train a 175B dense transformer" circa 2020; it is presented here as reasoning, not as a description of what OpenAI actually built, because OpenAI never published this level of detail for GPT-3.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

**Objective:** standard autoregressive language modeling — maximize `sum_t log P(x_t | x_<t)` under cross-entropy loss, identical in form to GPT-1 and GPT-2. GPT-3's novelty is emphatically not the training objective; it is what emerges from applying the same objective at 175B scale over ~300B tokens, namely in-context learning (Section 7).

**Data sources and the quality-weighted mixture.** The training corpus is a filtered and deliberately reweighted mixture of five sources. The following token counts and mixture weights are the paper's Table 2.2 figures as best-recollected here — **treat all numbers in this paragraph as approximate and flag for verification against the primary source**:

| Source | Approx. tokens available | Approx. weight in training mix |
|---|---|---|
| Common Crawl (filtered) | ~410B | ~60% |
| WebText2 | ~19B | ~22% |
| Books1 | ~12B | ~8% |
| Books2 | ~55B | ~8% |
| Wikipedia (English) | ~3B | ~3% |

The pedagogically important point is not the exact percentages but the *shape* of the weighting: this is not proportional/naive sampling by raw token count. Common Crawl, despite being by far the largest available source (~410B filtered tokens, and vastly larger before filtering), was *downweighted* relative to its raw size — sampled at a rate that gives it roughly 60% of a ~300B-token training budget despite representing the large majority of raw available tokens. Conversely, the smaller but higher-quality sources — Wikipedia, Books1/Books2, and WebText2 — were *upsampled* relative to their raw size, appearing multiple times over the course of training in some cases, on the premise that quality/curation density matters and that repeated exposure to a smaller high-quality corpus is preferable to single-pass exposure to a much larger noisy one. This is a deliberate, hand-tuned curriculum-like weighting decision, not a mechanical union of sources.

**Common Crawl filtering.** Raw Common Crawl is extremely noisy. OpenAI's pipeline: (1) trained a classifier (logistic regression over features, per the paper) to distinguish high-quality reference text (WebText-like, i.e., text similar to pages linked from Reddit with sufficient karma, the same heuristic GPT-2 used to build WebText) from raw, unfiltered Common Crawl documents, then used that classifier's score to retain only a higher-quality subset of CC; (2) applied fuzzy deduplication at the document level (via something like MinHash/LSH-style near-duplicate detection) both within Common Crawl and against the other corpora, to reduce train-set redundancy and contamination; (3) added the curated WebText2, Books1, Books2, and Wikipedia sources on top.

**WebText2** is described as an expanded version of the WebText corpus originally built for GPT-2 (itself built from outbound Reddit links with karma >= 3), with additional scraping to grow it beyond the original GPT-2-era snapshot.

**Books1 and Books2** are two internet-sourced book corpora; the paper does not fully disclose their provenance/licensing in detail, and exact composition of these two corpora is not something later reproduced precisely in the open literature (their overlap with corpora later scrutinized in copyright discussions, e.g., "Books3"-style corpora used by other groups, is a downstream topic, not something the GPT-3 paper itself addresses — flagged as outside the paper's stated scope).

**Contamination note.** The paper explicitly discusses train/test contamination as a known limitation — because Common Crawl is scraped from the open web, some benchmark evaluation data can leak into the training set. The authors report attempting to filter contaminated documents and separately re-evaluating on "clean" subsets, but flag this as an imperfect, non-exhaustive mitigation. This self-reported caveat is confirmed in the paper; the practical residual contamination rate is not precisely known.

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

GPT-3 as released in the paper is the **raw pretrained language model** — there is no instruction-tuning stage, no RLHF, no supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations, and no preference modeling anywhere in this pipeline. The model is used purely via prompting: a natural-language task instruction, optionally followed by a small number (`k`) of input/output demonstration pairs placed directly in the context window, followed by a new input for the model to complete (few-shot, `k` typically up to a few dozen bounded by the 2048-token context; zero-shot is `k=0` with only an instruction; one-shot is `k=1`).

This has direct, foreseeable consequences that the paper itself discusses as limitations: the model has no training signal that teaches it to *follow instructions* as a distinct skill from *continuing plausible text*; it will often complete a prompt in a way that is textually plausible but unhelpful, evasive, repetitive, or factually wrong, because nothing in cross-entropy pretraining on internet text specifically rewards helpfulness, honesty, or harmlessness. It inherits biases and toxic associations present in Common-Crawl-heavy web text (Section 10) with no post-hoc mitigation layer. There is also no mechanism to correct a bad output at inference time beyond re-prompting — the model cannot be "told it's wrong" and update; each generation is independent conditional sampling from the fixed pretrained distribution.

This gap is exactly what OpenAI's next major step in the lineage — **InstructGPT** (Ouyang et al., 2022) — addresses directly, via supervised fine-tuning on human-written demonstrations followed by RLHF (reward modeling + PPO) against human preference judgments, explicitly aimed at making GPT-3-scale models follow instructions and align with user intent rather than just continue text plausibly. GPT-3's alignment gap is the direct motivating problem statement for that follow-up work.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

**Genuinely new in this paper:**
- Empirical demonstration that few-shot in-context learning — task specification via natural-language instructions plus a handful of demonstrations in the prompt, no gradient updates — becomes a strong, general-purpose capability at sufficient model scale, with performance on many tasks approaching or in some cases matching fine-tuned baselines from the pre-GPT-3 era.
- A clear empirical scaling curve showing that the *gap* between zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot performance is itself a function of model scale: larger models extract disproportionately more benefit from in-context demonstrations than smaller models do. This is presented as evidence that in-context learning is an emergent-with-scale phenomenon rather than a fixed capability present equally at all sizes — a qualitatively different and stronger result than GPT-2's more limited zero-shot transfer story.
- The direct, large-scale experimental validation of the Kaplan et al. 2020 scaling-law thesis: that pushing parameter count aggressively (relative to data and compute-matched alternatives of the time) was the compute-efficient path to lower loss and, it turned out, to qualitatively new capabilities not obviously predictable from loss curves alone (in-context learning is a capability result layered on top of a smooth loss-scaling result).
- Broad multi-task, multi-benchmark evaluation of a single fixed model (no per-task fine-tuning) across dozens of NLP benchmarks (translation, QA, cloze/completion, commonsense reasoning, arithmetic, novel/synthetic tasks designed specifically to rule out memorization), establishing few-shot prompting as a legitimate general evaluation paradigm.

**Explicitly inherited, not invented here:**
- The alternating dense/local-sparse attention pattern — directly attributed by the paper to Child et al. 2019's Sparse Transformer.
- The overall decoder-only, pre-norm Transformer block, learned absolute position embeddings, GELU FFN, weight-tied embeddings — all carried over from GPT-2 with no structural change.
- The autoregressive cross-entropy training objective — unchanged since GPT-1.
- BPE-style subword tokenization — continued from GPT-2's tokenizer lineage.

**Critical framing (hindsight, later work):** the compute-optimal reasoning underlying the decision to scale to 175B params came from Kaplan et al. 2020, which fit power laws using a particular parameterization and step/data budget assumptions. Hoffmann et al. 2022 ("Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models," the Chinchilla paper) revisited this with a more careful methodology (varying both model size and data size independently across a compute-matched grid, correcting for learning-rate schedule confounds in the earlier fits) and concluded that Kaplan et al.'s laws under-emphasized data scaling — for a fixed compute budget, the compute-optimal choice is a substantially smaller model trained on substantially more data than Kaplan-style extrapolation would suggest. Under Chinchilla-optimal accounting, a 175B-parameter model would want on the order of 3.5 trillion training tokens for compute-optimality, not ~300B. GPT-3 is thus a textbook example, with hindsight, of a model that is oversized relative to its training data by later standards — it is not "wrong" for its time (it was built on the best scaling-law understanding available in 2020), but it is a clear case study in how a foundational methodological assumption (Kaplan et al.'s specific power-law fits) shaped a very expensive real-world architecture decision that a follow-up paper two years later showed to be a suboptimal point on the compute frontier. This should be stated as "later work revised this," not as a contemporaneous error by the GPT-3 authors.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

The paper does not provide a detailed engineering post-mortem (unlike, e.g., later papers such as OPT-175B's logbook or BLOOM's technical report, which explicitly document instabilities). What can be stated:

- **Confirmed, paper-adjacent:** the paper reports using a cosine learning-rate decay schedule with linear warmup, weight decay, gradient clipping, and increasing batch size with model size (larger models trained with larger batches and lower learning rates) — standard large-LM training hygiene of the era, consistent with practices established in GPT-2 and contemporaneous work.
- **Systems-reasoning (not confirmed for GPT-3 specifically):** at 175B scale, the standard set of engineering problems any team would face includes: (a) numerical instability in fp16 mixed precision requiring careful loss scaling, since gradient underflow becomes more likely as depth and width grow; (b) stragglers/hardware failures over a training run spanning likely weeks across thousands of GPUs, requiring frequent checkpointing and elastic recovery; (c) communication overhead from all-reduces in tensor-parallel groups becoming a larger fraction of step time as TP degree grows, motivating keeping TP groups within NVLink domains; (d) activation memory pressure requiring selective activation checkpointing/recomputation; (e) load-balancing pipeline stages so no stage bottlenecks the others. None of this is confirmed by the GPT-3 paper as specifically encountered; it is presented as the standard challenge set for training at this scale, flagged as such.
- **Data-pipeline engineering (confirmed in spirit, not in exact detail):** building the CC-quality classifier, running fuzzy deduplication at the scale of hundreds of billions of tokens, and mixing five heterogeneous sources at controlled sampling ratios is itself a nontrivial offline data-engineering pipeline; the paper describes the outcome (Section 5) but not the pipeline's implementation.

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

- **Memory footprint:** 175B params at fp16 = 350GB of weights alone, before any KV cache or activation memory — this cannot fit on a single accelerator of the 2020 (or even mid-2020s) era, so serving requires the model to be sharded across multiple GPUs even at inference time (tensor parallelism is the natural fit for low-latency serving, since it avoids pipeline-parallel bubble latency per token).
- **KV cache:** with 96 layers, 96 heads, 128 dim/head, and context up to 2048, the KV cache per token is `2 (K and V) * 96 layers * 12288 (=96*128) * 2 bytes (fp16)` ≈ 4.7MB/token, so a full 2048-token context's KV cache is roughly 9.7GB per sequence in the batch — a meaningful serving cost that scales linearly with batch size and must be budgeted for on top of the 350GB of weights.
- **Alternating dense/sparse attention and serving:** the locally-banded sparse layers, if actually exploited at inference (rather than just masked-and-computed-densely for simplicity, which the paper's description does not rule out), would reduce KV-cache *read* and attention-score compute for those layers proportionally to `W/n`; whether OpenAI's production serving stack exploited this optimization is not disclosed.
- **Latency/throughput:** autoregressive decoding is inherently sequential (one forward pass per output token, each attending back over the growing KV cache), and at 175B this makes single-token latency dominated by cross-GPU communication in the tensor-parallel forward pass; none of the specific latency/throughput figures OpenAI achieved in the original API are publicly confirmed in the paper (the paper is a research paper, not a serving-systems paper).
- **API delivery:** GPT-3 was made available exclusively via OpenAI's hosted API (no open weight release, unlike GPT-2 which was eventually released in full), which is itself a notable shift in OpenAI's release policy and is the model the phrase "model as a service" is often associated with in this era.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

The paper evaluates the family across dozens of standard NLP benchmarks (cloze/completion tasks like LAMBADA, question answering like TriviaQA/Natural Questions, commonsense reasoning like PIQA/ARC, translation, Winograd-schema-style tasks, SuperGLUE, and purpose-built synthetic tasks such as arithmetic and word-scramble/anagram tasks designed to probe whether the model is doing something more than pattern-matching to memorized text) under zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot prompting, without any fine-tuning. Few-shot GPT-3-175B is competitive with or exceeds prior fine-tuned state-of-the-art on a subset of these tasks, and clearly worse on others.

**Known/reported limitations, several of which became widely cited critique points:**
- **Arithmetic and multi-step symbolic reasoning:** GPT-3 performs reasonably on 2-digit addition/subtraction in few-shot settings but degrades sharply on 3+ digit arithmetic and more complex symbolic manipulation — an early, clear signal that pattern-completion-style in-context learning does not straightforwardly generalize to systematic algorithmic reasoning.
- **Long-generation coherence:** longer free-form generations can become repetitive, contradictory, or drift off-topic — the model has no explicit mechanism for maintaining long-range global coherence or planning beyond what local next-token prediction induces.
- **World-model and factual-grounding limitations:** the model has no grounding in an external world model or fact-checking mechanism; it produces fluent but sometimes factually incorrect statements (a precursor to what is now commonly called "hallucination"), and can exhibit failures consistent with weak understanding of entity relationships and directionality of facts (adjacent to what later literature term the "reversal curse" — flagged as a later-named phenomenon retroactively applicable here, not terminology used in the GPT-3 paper itself).
- **Bias and toxicity:** because training data is heavily weighted toward Common Crawl web text, the model reproduces and can amplify social biases (gender, race, religion associations) and can generate toxic content; the paper includes an explicit section analyzing measured bias along some of these axes and acknowledges it as an open problem, not one solved by the released model.
- **No tool use / no retrieval:** the model's only knowledge source is what was baked into pretraining; it cannot query external tools, browse, or retrieve documents — all "knowledge" is static, frozen at the training-data cutoff, and cannot be updated without retraining.
- **No inference-time correction / alignment gap:** as covered in Section 6, there is no mechanism to steer the model toward being more honest, harmless, or instruction-following beyond prompt engineering; the model will confidently continue in unhelpful or unsafe directions if the prompt distribution leads there, since nothing in training explicitly penalizes that.
- **Train/test contamination:** self-reported by the authors as an incompletely solved problem, given that benchmark data can leak into Common-Crawl-derived training data (Section 5).

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**Confirmed by the paper (high confidence):**
- 175B parameters, 96 layers, `d_model=12288`, 96 attention heads at 128 dim/head, FFN inner dimension 49152 (4x expansion), context window 2048 tokens.
- Family of 8 model sizes from 125M to 175B trained for scaling comparisons.

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

I'd frame GPT-3 as the paper that turned a scaling-law hypothesis into a capability discovery: Kaplan et al. showed loss scales predictably with size, but nobody had strong a priori evidence that a 175B model would unlock reliable few-shot in-context learning as a qualitatively new interface, and that's the actual empirical contribution people cite this paper for. I'd be careful to note the architecture itself is almost a non-story — it's GPT-2 plus the Child et al. 2019 alternating sparse-attention trick for efficiency, so the interesting engineering conversation is scale and infrastructure, not architectural novelty. I'd bring up, unprompted, that GPT-3's ~300B tokens against 175B parameters (~1.7 tokens/param) looks clearly data-starved by Chinchilla-optimal standards (~20 tokens/param, meaning a compute-matched optimum would want on the order of 3.5T tokens) — a good interview move is stating this as a hindsight critique enabled by Hoffmann et al. 2022's more careful compute-matched methodology, not as an error the 2020 authors should have caught. I'd connect GPT-3's complete lack of instruction-tuning or RLHF directly forward to InstructGPT: GPT-3 is a raw completion engine that happens to be steerable via clever prompting, and the entire alignment gap it leaves open — unhelpful completions, no correction mechanism, inherited web-text bias/toxicity — is precisely the problem statement InstructGPT's SFT+RLHF pipeline was built to address two years later. On the systems side, I'd walk through the memory-wall arithmetic unprompted (350GB fp16 weights, ~2.45TB of Adam optimizer/master-weight state) to show why 3D parallelism (tensor + pipeline + data, plus ZeRO-style sharding) is structurally required at this scale, while being explicit that OpenAI never disclosed their actual GPT-3 training stack, so I'm reasoning from first principles, not citing a confirmed design. Finally, I'd flag the paper's own limitations section (arithmetic failures, contamination risk, bias) as evidence the authors were fairly self-aware about the gap between "impressive few-shot benchmark numbers" and "a model that reasons or is factually grounded," which is a useful thing to be able to articulate precisely rather than hand-wave as "it's an old model, it has some issues."
