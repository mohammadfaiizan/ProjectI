## InstructGPT (2022)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

InstructGPT (Ouyang, Wu, Jiang, Almeida, Wainwright, Mishkin, Zhang, Agarwal, Slama, Ray, Schulman, Hilton, Kelton, Miller, Simens, Askell, Welinder, Christiano, Leike, Lowe — "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback," OpenAI, March 2022) is the paper that operationalizes RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) as a production post-training pipeline for a general-purpose instruction-following assistant, at three GPT-3 scales: **1.3B, 6B, and 175B parameters**, each initialized from the corresponding pretrained GPT-3 checkpoint. It is not the first application of RLHF to language models — Ziegler et al. (2019, "Fine-Tuning Language Models from Human Preferences") and Stiennon et al. (2020, "Learning to Summarize from Human Feedback") establish the pairwise-preference-reward-model-plus-PPO recipe this paper reuses — but it is the first to apply that recipe at GPT-3 scale to *general instruction-following* rather than a single narrow task (summarization), and the first to make the resulting model a shipped product (it directly became the model served behind the OpenAI API, and its lineage leads directly to ChatGPT/GPT-3.5, see Section 12).

The problem InstructGPT targets is what the paper calls the **misalignment between the language modeling objective and "follow the user's instructions helpfully and safely."** Raw GPT-3 is trained with a single objective: predict the next token of internet text with high likelihood. Nothing in that objective distinguishes "a helpful, direct answer to a question" from "a plausible continuation of a question in the style of internet text" — and a huge amount of internet text is not helpful, direct, or honest. Concretely this manifests as: GPT-3 often continues a prompt rather than answering it (e.g., appending more questions to a question, because that is what a plausible internet document containing that question would statistically do); it fabricates plausible-sounding but false content with no distinction in surface fluency between confident-true and confident-false statements (nothing in pretraining rewards calibrated honesty specifically); and it can readily produce toxic, biased, or otherwise undesirable completions, because such content exists in the pretraining distribution and the objective has no mechanism to penalize it differentially from any other equally-likely continuation. The paper frames the fix as **aligning the model with user intent** — a deliberately narrower and more operational framing than "solving alignment" in the general sense.

It is worth being precise about a terminology point that is easy to conflate across labs: the paper does discuss wanting outputs to be helpful, honest/truthful, and harmless, and cites Askell et al. (2021, "A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment") for that framing — but the specific branded "HHH" framing as a first-class, load-bearing research identity is more strongly associated with Anthropic's contemporaneous and subsequent work (see `LLM/Claude/001_Claude1.md`, Section 6, on Constitutional AI). InstructGPT's own framing is centered on **aligning with the stated and implied intent of the person giving the instruction**, operationalized through a labeler rating rubric that *includes* helpfulness, truthfulness, and harm-avoidance as components, rather than treating "HHH" as the paper's central three-word thesis statement the way Anthropic's papers do.

Strategically, InstructGPT's headline empirical claim — that labelers preferred outputs from the **1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model over outputs from the 175B-parameter base GPT-3 model**, a >100x parameter gap — is a direct challenge to "scale alone is the alignment lever." It demonstrates that a comparatively cheap post-training intervention (RLHF fine-tuning, which the paper reports costs a small fraction of pretraining compute — see Section 3) can move the needle on "outputs humans actually want" more than two more orders of magnitude of raw pretraining compute. This result is arguably the single most cited empirical justification for why every subsequent frontier lab treats post-training/alignment as a first-class research investment rather than an afterthought bolted onto a pretrained checkpoint.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

**The base transformer architecture is unchanged from GPT-3.** InstructGPT's policy models (at 1.3B, 6B, 175B) are decoder-only, causally-masked transformers with learned position embeddings, initialized directly from pretrained GPT-3 checkpoints of matching size (see the GPT-3 scaling writeup for the per-layer architecture detail — 96 layers / 12,288 hidden / 96 heads at 175B, etc.). The paper introduces **zero new architectural components to the policy/generator network**. This is a deliberate and important framing: InstructGPT's contribution is entirely in the *training procedure* applied on top of an unmodified pretrained backbone, not in a new layer type, attention variant, or objective reformulation of the base model.

**The actual architectural novelty of this release is the reward model (RM).** The RM reuses the same GPT-family transformer backbone (the paper initializes RMs from the 6B and 175B SFT checkpoints), but with the final softmax language-modeling head **removed and replaced by a single linear projection to a scalar**, applied to the transformer's hidden state at the **final token of the (prompt, response) sequence** (i.e., the last non-padding token, typically an end-of-sequence marker appended after the response). Concretely:

```
h = TransformerBackbone(x, y)          # x = prompt tokens, y = response tokens, concatenated
h_last = h[:, -1, :]                    # hidden state at final token position
r_theta(x, y) = Linear(h_last) -> scalar
```

This "backbone-plus-scalar-head-on-final-token" pattern is the standard construction for turning any autoregressive transformer into a sequence-level scorer, and it recurs throughout the RLHF literature after this paper (reward models, value functions/critics, and later "outcome reward models" for reasoning-RL pipelines all use the same head-swap idea). The key design decision worth internalizing: **the RM's parameter count need not match the policy's.** The paper explicitly used a **6B reward model even when fine-tuning the 175B policy** — the paper reports that training a 175B RM was more unstable and that a 175B RM's cost as a repeatedly-queried scorer inside the PPO loop (every rollout, every token-level KL computation context) was not justified by an accuracy gain large enough to offset that instability and cost. This decoupling of RM size from policy size is a real, citable design finding: bigger is not strictly better for a reward model used as a training signal, and RM training dynamics (Section 8) can be qualitatively different from policy pretraining dynamics at the same parameter count.

There is no separate architecture for the SFT model — it is literally the pretrained GPT-3 checkpoint continuing to use the same LM head, fine-tuned via standard supervised cross-entropy (Section 6). The only genuinely new *module* introduced by this paper, architecturally, is the scalar reward head.

### 3. Scale -- Parameters, Data, Compute

- **Policy model sizes:** 1.3B, 6B, 175B — each initialized from the pretrained GPT-3 checkpoint of matching scale. These three figures are the headline, confirmed release sizes.
- **Reward model size:** 6B, used as the reward signal for PPO fine-tuning **across all three policy sizes, including the 175B policy** — this decoupling is confirmed in the paper and is one of its more quietly important infra/design facts (Section 2, Section 8). A 175B RM was also trained and evaluated but not used as the production reward signal for the reported PPO runs, for the stability/cost reasons noted above.
- **Post-training data (approximate — flagged as best-recollection figures from the paper, not re-verified against primary source in this session):**
  - **SFT dataset:** on the order of **~13,000 prompts** with human-written demonstration completions (a mix of labeler-written prompts across use-case categories — generation, open QA, closed QA, brainstorming, chat, rewriting, summarization, classification, other free-form — and prompts drawn from actual OpenAI Playground/API traffic with user consent, filtered for PII and de-duplicated).
  - **RM training dataset:** on the order of **~33,000 prompts**, each with multiple sampled completions (K = 4 to 9 per prompt, per the paper) ranked by labelers from best to worst, decomposed into pairwise comparisons for training (Section 6).
  - **PPO dataset:** on the order of **~31,000 prompts**, used purely to sample rollouts for the RL fine-tuning stage (no human labels needed at this stage — the reward model supplies the training signal).
  - These three figures (~13k / ~33k / ~31k) should be treated as **approximate**; the paper reports more granular breakdowns per labeler-written vs. customer-sourced split and per use-case category that are not being reproduced exactly here.
- **Compute:** the paper does not report FLOPs for the RLHF fine-tuning stages with the same precision pretraining papers report pretraining FLOPs, but it does make the qualitative/quantitative claim (best-recollection, flagged) that the **RLHF fine-tuning compute is a small fraction — on the order of a couple of percent — of the GPT-3 pretraining compute** for the corresponding base model. This is a materially important framing for interviews: alignment/post-training at this point in the field's history was cheap relative to pretraining, which is a large part of why "spend more on alignment" was an easy call once the 1.3B-beats-175B result was in hand.
- **Labeler pool:** the paper describes a pool of roughly **40 contractors** (hired via Upwork and ScaleAI), screened via a labeling-agreement/qualification test designed to select for sensitivity to different demographic groups' preferences and ability to identify potentially harmful outputs. This headcount figure is commonly cited alongside the paper; treat it as best-available.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

This is the section where InstructGPT is a genuinely different systems problem from pretraining, and it is worth being explicit about why, because it generalizes to essentially every RLHF/RL-from-a-learned-reward pipeline since.

Pretraining (and SFT) is a **pure gradient-descent problem**: read a batch of tokens, forward pass, compute a loss, backward pass, optimizer step, repeat. It is compute-bound in a very homogeneous, predictable way, and its infra story (data/tensor/pipeline parallelism, ZeRO-style sharding, activation checkpointing) is entirely about making that one repeated operation fast at scale.

**PPO fine-tuning is not that.** For every training step, the pipeline needs, concurrently:

1. **The policy model, in generation/sampling mode** — autoregressively decoding a completion for each sampled prompt (KV-cached autoregressive decode, one or more sampling passes), which is the "actor" producing rollouts. This is inference-shaped (sequential, latency-sensitive, benefits from KV-caching and batched decoding) rather than training-shaped, and in practice is frequently the wall-clock bottleneck of the whole loop, since it requires as many sequential forward passes as generated tokens per rollout, in contrast to the single forward/backward pass per training example in pretraining.
2. **The frozen reference model (the SFT snapshot), in forward-pass-only mode** — used to compute the KL penalty term (Section 6) by re-scoring the same rollout tokens under the reference distribution. This is a second full model's worth of forward-pass compute per training step, held perfectly fixed (no gradient updates), so it can typically run with minimal-precision inference optimizations without concern for training numerical stability.
3. **The reward model, in forward-pass-only mode** — scoring each completed (prompt, response) pair to produce the scalar reward. A third model's worth of forward-pass compute, also frozen throughout PPO (it is trained once, in Stage 2, and never updated during Stage 3).
4. **A value function/critic**, either a separate head on the policy network or a distinct model, producing the baseline used for advantage estimation (GAE) inside PPO — a fourth set of forward passes if implemented as a separate model, or folded into (1) if implemented as an auxiliary head sharing the policy's backbone (this is the more common and more compute-efficient choice, and is what the accompanying code in this writeup's Python file does).
5. **The policy model again, in training/backward mode** — recomputing log-probabilities of the already-generated rollout tokens under the current (and, across PPO epochs, changing) policy parameters, and taking gradient steps against the PPO clipped objective (Section 6).

The net infra shape is closer to an **actor-learner reinforcement learning system** (in the Rllib/IMPALA/SEED-RL sense) than to a pretraining data-parallel job: a generation/rollout-collection phase that is inference-bound and needs efficient batched sampling infrastructure, interleaved with a policy-update phase that is standard backward-pass training, with two additional frozen models (reference, reward) that must be kept resident and queryable throughout. The paper itself does not disclose cluster topology or parallelism strategy for this stage (this is not disclosed with the precision of, e.g., Megatron-LM's or GPT-3's own infra descriptions), so the specific engineering choices (how rollout generation is batched/distributed relative to the training step, whether reference/reward model inference is co-located with policy training or served separately) are **inferred from general RLHF systems practice of the era rather than confirmed from the paper**, and should be flagged as such. What is confirmed is the *shape* of the problem: at minimum 3 models' worth of concurrent forward-pass compute (policy generation, frozen reference, frozen reward model), plus the policy's own backward pass — a fundamentally more heterogeneous and more inference-heavy systems problem than gradient-descent-only pretraining.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

The base-model pretraining data and objective are **entirely unchanged from GPT-3** — the same web-crawl-heavy mixture (Common Crawl, WebText2, Books1/Books2, Wikipedia) and the same causal next-token-prediction cross-entropy objective (see the GPT-3 scaling writeup for the mixture breakdown). InstructGPT does not re-pretrain or modify this stage in any way; it starts from an already-pretrained GPT-3 checkpoint and only touches post-training.

The genuinely **new data introduced by this paper is entirely human-generated post-training data** — demonstrations for SFT and pairwise/ranked preference comparisons for the reward model — and the full mechanics of how that data is collected, structured, and used are the actual subject of this paper. Rather than duplicate that material here, this section exists to make explicit that **Section 6 is where the real "data & objective" story for InstructGPT lives**: the SFT demonstration dataset, the RM comparison dataset, and the PPO prompt dataset (all summarized quantitatively in Section 3) are the paper's actual data contribution, collected through a purpose-built labeling interface with a screened contractor pool, using prompts sourced both from labelers themselves (writing prompts across explicit use-case categories to ensure coverage) and from real OpenAI Playground/API traffic (filtered for personally identifiable information and de-duplicated per user to avoid over-representing any single customer's use case).

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

This is the paper's core contribution: a three-stage pipeline, run sequentially, each stage's model output feeding the next stage.

**Stage 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).** Collect a dataset of human-written demonstrations: for a distribution of prompts (Section 3/5), a labeler writes the response they would want the model to give. Fine-tune the pretrained GPT-3 checkpoint on these (prompt, demonstration) pairs using standard autoregressive cross-entropy, with the loss **masked on the prompt tokens and computed only over the completion/response tokens** (the prompt is context the model conditions on, not a target to be predicted). This produces the **SFT model**, which serves two roles downstream: it is the **initial policy** for RL fine-tuning, and it is the **frozen reference model** the KL penalty in Stage 3 measures divergence against. The paper trains for a small number of epochs (this stage overfits quickly on ~13k examples relative to the pretraining corpus size, consistent with it being a light-touch adaptation rather than a from-scratch training run) and finds SFT alone already substantially improves instruction-following over the raw pretrained model, though not as much as the subsequent RM+RL stages.

**Stage 2 — Reward Model (RM) training.** For a set of prompts, sample **multiple completions** from one or more policy snapshots (the SFT model and/or earlier PPO checkpoints), typically **K = 4 to 9 completions per prompt**. A labeler then ranks all K completions for that prompt from best to worst (a full ordering, which is more label-efficient per labeler-hour than binary A/B comparisons alone, since a single ranking of K items yields `C(K,2)` pairwise comparisons). Train a reward model — a transformer backbone plus scalar head (Section 2) — via the **Bradley-Terry pairwise preference loss**. For a prompt `x` with a preferred completion `y_w` ("winner") and a less-preferred completion `y_l` ("loser") drawn from the same ranking:

```
loss(theta) = -E_{(x, y_w, y_l) ~ D} [ log( sigmoid( r_theta(x, y_w) - r_theta(x, y_l) ) ) ]
```

This is exactly maximum-likelihood estimation under the **Bradley-Terry model of pairwise choice**: it posits `P(y_w > y_l | x) = sigmoid(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l))`, i.e., the probability that a human prefers `y_w` over `y_l` is a logistic function of the *difference* in the two completions' latent scalar "goodness" scores, and training minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the observed preference labels under that model — mechanically identical to training a logistic regression on score differences, except the scores are the outputs of a shared neural network conditioned on `(x, y)`.

Two efficiency/overfitting details the paper is explicit about: (1) with `K` completions per prompt, all `C(K,2)` pairs are used, but because all `C(K,2)` pairs for a given prompt are highly correlated (they all condition on the same prompt and largely the same handful of completions), naively treating each pair as an independent training example both **overweights prompts with larger K** (a prompt with 9 ranked completions contributes 36 pairs, versus 6 for a prompt with 4) and **risks overfitting**, since the effective number of independent data points per prompt is closer to K than to `C(K,2)`. The paper's mitigation is to **normalize the loss by the number of comparisons per prompt** and, for training efficiency, to place **all `C(K,2)` comparisons from a single prompt into one forward-pass batch element** (i.e., compute all K completions' hidden states once, then form all pairwise loss terms from those K cached scalar outputs) rather than re-running the transformer forward pass redundantly for the same completion every time it appears in a different pair.

**Stage 3 — RL fine-tuning via PPO.** Use the trained RM as the reward signal to fine-tune the SFT policy with reinforcement learning (Proximal Policy Optimization, Schulman et al., 2017). Mechanically, for each sampled prompt `x`, the policy generates a completion `y ~ pi^RL_phi(. | x)`, and the **KL-penalized reward** given to the RL algorithm is:

```
R(x, y) = r_theta(x, y) - beta * KL( pi^RL_phi(. | x) || pi^SFT(. | x) )
```

approximated in practice per-token via the log-probability ratio, `beta * (log pi^RL_phi(y_t | ...) - log pi^SFT(y_t | ...))`, summed (or averaged) over the completion's tokens — the standard practical estimator for a KL divergence between two autoregressive distributions evaluated on a sample from one of them.

**Why the KL penalty term exists — this is a mechanism worth being able to explain cold in an interview.** Without it, the policy is free to move arbitrarily far from the SFT distribution in whatever direction maximizes `r_theta`. Because `r_theta` is a *learned, imperfect proxy* for true human preference (trained on a finite, noisy comparison dataset), a policy with no constraint will discover and exploit whatever quirks or blind spots the RM has — producing text that scores highly under `r_theta` but is not actually good text by the standard the RM was meant to approximate. This is **reward hacking / Goodhart's Law** ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") applied to a learned reward model, and in this literature is usually called **RM overoptimization**: the further the policy drifts from the RM's training distribution, the less reliable the RM's scores become as a proxy for real human judgment, yet the RL objective keeps climbing those (increasingly unreliable) scores regardless. The KL penalty anchors the policy near the SFT distribution — the region where the RM was actually trained and validated against real human comparisons — while still allowing it to climb reward within that trust region. A secondary, related benefit: the KL term also discourages the policy's output distribution from collapsing into a narrow, low-entropy mode purely because that mode scores well under the RM (mode collapse/loss of diversity), since collapsing sharply away from the SFT distribution's entropy is itself penalized by the KL term.

**The PPO clipped surrogate objective, mechanically.** Let `r_t(theta) = pi_theta(a_t | s_t) / pi_theta_old(a_t | s_t)` be the probability ratio between the current policy being optimized and the policy that generated the rollout (the "old" policy, fixed for the duration of the current PPO update). Given an advantage estimate `A_t` (Section on GAE below), the clipped surrogate objective is:

```
L^CLIP(theta) = E_t[ min( r_t(theta) * A_t, clip(r_t(theta), 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * A_t ) ]
```

with `eps` typically ~0.2. The `min` combined with clipping the ratio means: if the advantage is positive (the action was better than the baseline), the objective still rewards increasing `r_t(theta)` (making that action more likely) but the *gain* from doing so is capped once `r_t(theta) > 1 + eps` — there is no incentive to move the policy arbitrarily far from the old policy just because a single advantage estimate was very positive. Symmetrically, for negative advantage, the penalty for decreasing probability is capped once `r_t(theta) < 1 - eps`. This is PPO's mechanism for approximating a trust-region constraint (à la TRPO) without TRPO's expensive second-order/conjugate-gradient machinery — a much cheaper first-order surrogate that empirically behaves similarly. The advantage `A_t` itself is estimated with a **value-function baseline via GAE (Generalized Advantage Estimation)**, requiring the value head/model mentioned in Section 2/4.

**Mapping this onto language generation specifically:** in the LM-RLHF setting, the natural "action" is generating one token conditioned on the sequence so far, and an "episode" is a full completion (prompt through end-of-sequence). The paper's formulation gives a reward at the *sequence* level (the RM scores a completed response, not each partial prefix) with the KL term applied *per token* (since the KL-divergence log-ratio is naturally decomposable token-by-token for an autoregressive model) — so in practice the reward signal driving PPO is a mix of a sparse terminal reward (the RM score, received once the full sequence is generated) and a dense per-token KL penalty (accrued at every generation step). This is a meaningfully different credit-assignment problem than PPO's original robotics/games settings, where reward is often available at every environment step — the sparsity of the RM signal is part of why GAE's bias/variance tradeoff (via its `lambda` parameter) and the value function's quality both matter more here than they might in a denser-reward RL setting.

**PPO-ptx: mitigating the alignment tax.** The paper observes that pure RL fine-tuning against the RM causes measurable regressions on public NLP benchmarks (SQuAD, DROP, HellaSwag, WMT translation, and others) relative to the base GPT-3 model — a phenomenon it calls the **alignment tax**: optimizing for "what human labelers in this specific rating process prefer" is not the same objective as "perform well on academic NLP benchmarks," and pure PPO fine-tuning trades some of the latter for the former. The paper's mitigation is to mix in an **additional pretraining log-likelihood loss term** during the PPO updates, giving the combined objective (per the paper, as a maximization target):

```
objective(phi) = E_{(x,y) ~ D_pi^RL_phi} [ r_theta(x, y) - beta * log( pi^RL_phi(y|x) / pi^SFT(y|x) ) ]
                 + gamma * E_{x ~ D_pretrain} [ log( pi^RL_phi(x) ) ]
```

The first line is exactly the KL-shaped PPO reward objective already described. The second term adds back a coefficient-`gamma`-weighted log-likelihood loss on samples from the **original pretraining distribution** — i.e., during RL fine-tuning, the model is simultaneously asked not to forget how to be a good general-purpose language model on ordinary web text. When `gamma > 0` this variant is called **PPO-ptx** ("ptx" for "pretraining mix"); `gamma = 0` recovers plain PPO. This is mechanically a cousin of GPT-1's auxiliary-LM-loss-during-fine-tuning trick (see `001_GPT1.md`, Section 6) and conceptually a cousin of the KL-to-reference term itself — both are instances of "constrain the fine-tuned model to not drift too far from a distribution we trust," just anchored to different reference distributions (the SFT policy for the KL term, the raw pretraining corpus for the ptx term). PPO-ptx is reported to substantially close (though not fully eliminate) the alignment-tax regressions observed with plain PPO, at some cost in how strongly the model optimizes pure human preference relative to plain PPO — i.e., it is itself a tradeoff knob, not a free lunch.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

**Genuinely new in this paper:**
- Demonstrating the full **SFT -> RM (Bradley-Terry) -> PPO** RLHF pipeline works at GPT-3 scale (up to 175B) for **general instruction-following**, rather than a single narrow task — this is the first time this recipe is shown to generalize across the open-ended distribution of "whatever a user might ask an assistant," as opposed to Stiennon et al.'s single-task summarization setting.
- The empirical finding that **RM size can be decoupled from policy size** (a 6B RM sufficing, and outperforming a 175B RM in practice, as the reward signal for a 175B policy) — a real, non-obvious systems/research finding about what reward models actually need to be good at versus what policies need.
- **PPO-ptx**, the mixed RL-plus-pretraining-log-likelihood objective, as a specific, reusable technique for reducing the alignment tax — introducing the idea that "anchor to the pretraining distribution, not just the SFT distribution" is a separate, additional lever beyond the KL-to-SFT-reference term.
- The **1.3B-beats-175B preference result** itself is an empirical contribution as much as a training-technique contribution: it is the paper's central piece of evidence that alignment interventions can dominate raw scale on the specific axis of "humans prefer this output," reframing how the field allocates research investment between capability-scaling and post-training.

**Inherited / building on prior work (be precise about lineage in an interview):**
- **PPO itself** is Schulman et al., 2017 ("Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms") — a general-purpose RL algorithm with no LM-specific content; InstructGPT applies it, does not invent it.
- **Bradley-Terry pairwise preference modeling** predates this paper by decades (the Bradley-Terry model is from 1952, originally for paired-comparison experiments in statistics) and had already been used for reward modeling from human preferences in RL contexts prior to this paper (e.g., Christiano et al., 2017, "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences," applied it to Atari/robotics preference learning, with several of the same authors carrying that lineage forward into the LM setting).
- **RLHF applied to language models specifically** has direct precedent in Ziegler et al. (2019) and, most directly, **Stiennon et al. (2020, "Learning to Summarize from Human Feedback")** — the same reward-model-plus-PPO structure, the same organization, largely overlapping authorship, applied to abstractive summarization rather than general instruction-following. InstructGPT is best understood as "the Stiennon et al. recipe, scaled up and generalized from one task to the full distribution of user intents," not as a from-scratch invention of RLHF-for-LMs.
- The **helpful/honest/harmless evaluative framing** draws on Askell et al. (2021), as noted in Section 1.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

- **RM overfitting from correlated within-prompt comparisons** (Section 6): mitigated by normalizing the loss per prompt by the number of comparisons and batching all `C(K,2)` pairs from one prompt as a single unit of computation, rather than treating each pairwise comparison as an i.i.d. training example.
- **RM overoptimization / reward hacking during PPO** (Section 6): the central, unresolved-in-general failure mode of this whole paradigm. The KL penalty coefficient `beta` is the primary lever; too small and the policy overoptimizes the (imperfect) RM and drifts into text that scores well but reads poorly to actual humans (verbose, hedgy, or superficially "matches labeler preferences" in ways that are not genuinely higher quality — an early, paper-adjacent manifestation of what the field now broadly calls **sycophancy risk**, discussed further in Section 10); too large and the policy barely moves from the SFT model, forfeiting most of the benefit of RL fine-tuning. Later work (studied more rigorously in follow-up scaling-law-style papers on reward model overoptimization, not this paper itself) shows the gap between true reward and proxy (RM) reward grows roughly predictably as the policy is optimized further against a fixed RM — this paper's KL-constraint design is exactly the practical mitigation that later work quantifies more precisely.
- **175B RM training instability** (Section 2/3): the paper's own stated reason for preferring a 6B RM as the production reward signal even for the 175B policy — a concrete instance of "bigger is not automatically better/more stable" for a reward model specifically, distinct from the policy-scaling story where bigger reliably helps.
- **Alignment tax on public NLP benchmarks from pure PPO** (Section 6): mitigated via PPO-ptx's mixed pretraining-log-likelihood loss term, at the cost of somewhat diluting the pure preference-optimization signal — an explicit, acknowledged tradeoff rather than a fully solved problem.
- **Labeler agreement, consistency, and bias.** Human preference labels are noisy and demographically/instructionally biased by the specific labeler pool used (Section 3's ~40-contractor pool, screened for a particular notion of sensitivity to harm). The paper is explicit that the resulting model's notion of "aligned" is aligned to *this labeler population's* preferences and the *specific instructions given to labelers*, not to some demographically neutral or universal human preference — a limitation carried forward into essentially every RLHF-trained model since, and worth naming directly in an interview as a genuine, unresolved sourcing problem rather than a solved one.
- **Sample efficiency of human labels.** Ranking K completions per prompt rather than doing only pairwise A/B comparisons is itself an engineering choice to extract more supervision signal (`C(K,2)` comparisons) per unit of expensive human labeler time — a direct response to human feedback being the scarce, costly resource in this pipeline (unlike pretraining, where the scarce resource is compute against an effectively unlimited raw-text supply).

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

Once trained, the **served model is just the final PPO policy** — a standard decoder-only transformer, generation-time behavior identical in kind to serving the base GPT-3 model: autoregressive causal decoding, benefiting from KV-caching exactly as any GPT-family model does (see `001_GPT1.md`, Section 9, and the GPT-3 writeup for the general mechanics), with the usual sampling controls (temperature, top-k/top-p/nucleus sampling) exposed to the caller. Nothing about RLHF fine-tuning changes the *computational* profile of serving relative to serving the SFT or base model at the same parameter count — the reward model, reference model, and value function are all **training-time-only artifacts**; none of them are queried at inference/serving time once the policy is finalized. This is an important point to make explicit: the "3-4 models concurrently" infra story of Section 4 is exclusively a training-time phenomenon, and it fully collapses back down to "one model, standard autoregressive decoding" for serving.

One serving-relevant behavioral consequence of RLHF worth naming: because the policy has been trained to stop generating and produce a clean, complete response for a given prompt (rather than continuing indefinitely in the style of a raw internet document, GPT-3's default behavior), InstructGPT-style models are much more usable behind a simple "prompt in, complete response out" API without the caller needing to hand-engineer few-shot examples or stop sequences to coax well-formed completions out of the base model — a real, product-relevant simplification of the serving/prompting surface, distinct from any change in the raw compute cost of inference.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

**Headline result:** in blind human preference comparisons, labelers preferred outputs from the **1.3B InstructGPT model over outputs from the 175B base GPT-3 model**, and this preference held (with the gap widening) across all three released scales — i.e., InstructGPT models at every size were preferred to the base GPT-3 model of the *same* size by an even larger margin, but the cross-scale 1.3B-vs-175B result is the more striking and more frequently cited figure, since it isolates "alignment intervention" from "scale" as competing levers on the same evaluation axis.

**Truthfulness:** InstructGPT shows measurable improvement on **TruthfulQA** relative to base GPT-3, i.e., a reduction in confidently-stated false answers to questions specifically designed to elicit common misconceptions — a direct piece of evidence that the training pipeline nudges the model toward calibrated honesty on at least this benchmark, though this should not be read as "hallucination solved" (see limitations below).

**Toxicity:** on toxicity-eliciting prompt sets (e.g., RealToxicityPrompts-style evaluations), InstructGPT reduces but **does not eliminate** toxic output generation relative to the base model, and the paper notes the reduction is more pronounced when the model is explicitly instructed to be respectful than under adversarial or unprompted conditions — toxicity reduction is real but conditional, not a hard behavioral guarantee.

**Alignment tax:** as discussed in Section 6/8, pure PPO fine-tuning produces measurable regressions on public NLP benchmarks (translation, reading comprehension, commonsense-QA-style tasks) relative to base GPT-3 — a real cost of the RLHF procedure, substantially though not fully mitigated by PPO-ptx.

**Known limitations (some explicitly acknowledged in the paper, some more visible in retrospect):**
- **Hallucination is not solved.** RLHF-tuned models can still generate confident, fluent, false content; TruthfulQA improvement is a benchmark-specific, partial result, not evidence of general factual reliability.
- **Reward-model exploitation / sycophancy risk.** Because the RM is trained on labeler preferences, and labelers (like humans generally) can be swayed by surface features — length, apparent thoroughness, hedging, agreeableness — that correlate with but are not identical to genuine response quality, a policy optimized against the RM has a structural incentive to produce outputs that are *specifically* well-tuned to satisfy those surface features rather than to be maximally correct or useful. This is a real, structural risk inherent to optimizing any fixed learned proxy for human judgment, not a bug specific to this paper's implementation.
- **Labeler-pool bias gets baked into the model.** The specific demographic composition, instructions, and judgment calls of the ~40-person labeler pool (Section 3) directly shape what "aligned" means for the resulting model; this is a sourcing/representativeness limitation, not merely a data-quantity limitation.
- **RM overoptimization remains a live failure mode** if the KL constraint is too loose or PPO training runs too long against a fixed RM checkpoint — the RM is a static, increasingly-unreliable proxy for true human judgment the further the policy's output distribution moves from the RM's training distribution, and nothing in the paper's method fully closes this gap; it is managed (via `beta`) rather than eliminated. This exact failure mode is studied more rigorously, with scaling-law-style curves relating policy-RM KL divergence to true-vs-proxy reward gap, in follow-up research building directly on this paper's setup.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**Confirmed (stated directly in the paper / robustly and consistently reported across secondary sources describing the paper):**
- Three released model scales: 1.3B, 6B, 175B, each initialized from a matching-scale pretrained GPT-3 checkpoint.
- Three-stage pipeline: SFT on human demonstrations -> reward model trained on human-ranked comparisons via a Bradley-Terry-style pairwise loss -> PPO RL fine-tuning of the SFT policy against the reward model, with a KL penalty to the SFT reference.
- Comparisons per prompt for RM training: K = 4 to 9 ranked completions per prompt, decomposed into pairwise comparisons for training, with loss normalization to avoid overweighting prompts with more comparisons.
- Reward model size used for PPO: 6B, used even for the 175B policy; a 175B RM was also trained but not used as the production PPO reward signal, citing training instability.
- PPO-ptx: an additional pretraining-data log-likelihood loss term mixed into the PPO objective (coefficient `gamma`), introduced specifically to reduce alignment-tax regressions on public NLP benchmarks.
- Headline result: labelers preferred 1.3B InstructGPT outputs over 175B GPT-3 outputs in blind comparisons.
- Directional results: improved TruthfulQA performance; reduced (not eliminated) toxic-output generation; measurable alignment-tax regressions from pure PPO, mitigated (not eliminated) by PPO-ptx.
- Labeler pool: contractors hired via Upwork and ScaleAI, screened via a qualification process.

**Best-available / approximate / not independently re-verified in this session (explicitly flagged as such):**
- **Exact dataset sizes** — SFT ~13k prompts, RM training ~33k prompts, PPO ~31k prompts. These are commonly-cited approximate figures for the paper; treat as best-recollection order-of-magnitude figures, not exact reproductions of the paper's detailed per-split tables.
- **Labeler headcount (~40 contractors)** — widely cited alongside the paper; not independently re-verified against primary source text in this session.
- **RLHF fine-tuning compute as "a small fraction (order of a couple percent) of GPT-3 pretraining compute"** — a qualitative/approximate cost-comparison claim; the precise figure and its exact denominator (which GPT-3 pretraining run, exactly which fine-tuning stages are included) are not being re-derived here from primary source with FLOP-level precision.
- **Specific RM instability failure mode at 175B** — the qualitative finding (6B RM preferred/used in practice, 175B RM trained but not used as the production signal) is treated as confirmed; the precise mechanistic description of *why* the 175B RM was unstable is paraphrased/best-effort rather than a verbatim quotation of the paper's own explanation.
- **Infrastructure/parallelism details for the PPO training runs** (Section 4) — the *shape* of the problem (concurrent policy-generation, frozen-reference, and frozen-reward-model forward passes, plus policy backward passes) is a structural, near-certain inference from how PPO-based RLHF must work mechanically; the *specific* cluster topology, framework, and parallelism strategy used for InstructGPT's training runs are not disclosed in the paper with the precision of, e.g., a dedicated systems paper, and are not claimed as confirmed here.

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

I'd lead with the observation that InstructGPT's real contribution is procedural, not architectural — the transformer doesn't change at all; what changes is a three-stage data-and-optimization pipeline bolted onto an unmodified pretrained backbone, and that pipeline is now the default template ("pretrain, then SFT, then preference-model-plus-RL") for essentially every subsequent frontier assistant model, including OpenAI's own later GPT-3.5/ChatGPT (which is InstructGPT's direct RLHF recipe re-applied to multi-turn dialogue-formatted data rather than single-turn instruction-response pairs) and, independently, Anthropic's Claude line (with Constitutional AI as a variant that substitutes AI feedback for a large share of the harmlessness-preference labeling, see `LLM/Claude/001_Claude1.md`). I'd flag the KL-penalty-to-reference-policy mechanism as the single most important piece of the RL stage to understand cold, because it is a direct, mechanical embodiment of Goodhart's Law applied to a learned reward model: `r_theta` is always an approximation of true human preference fit on a finite, noisy comparison dataset, and any RL process that climbs `r_theta` without a distributional anchor will find and exploit the gap between the proxy and the target — the KL term doesn't eliminate that gap, it just bounds how far the policy is allowed to travel while exploiting it, which is a risk-management choice, not a solution. I'd also point out the RM-size-decoupling finding (6B RM for a 175B policy) as an underrated result worth citing specifically, because it's a concrete counterexample to "just make everything bigger" inside an RLHF pipeline, and it says something real about the RM's job being narrower and more brittle than the policy's job. On the results side, I'd treat the 1.3B-beats-175B headline number as the paper's most durable contribution to how the field allocates research investment — it's the empirical argument that made "spend serious research effort on alignment/post-training, not just scale" legible and fundable at the major labs, well before "scaling laws for RLHF" existed as a subfield in its own right. I'd critique the approach honestly on two axes: first, it fundamentally optimizes a fixed labeler population's revealed preferences under a specific rating rubric, which is a real, non-universal target dressed up as "aligning with user intent" — a limitation that's easy to gloss over and worth naming unprompted in an interview; second, reward-model exploitation and its softer cousin, sycophancy (the policy learning to produce outputs that are specifically well-tuned to what raters reward rather than what's genuinely most useful/correct), are structural risks of optimizing any fixed learned proxy for human judgment, and this paper's KL constraint manages that risk rather than closing it, which is exactly the gap that later reward-overoptimization scaling-law work goes on to characterize quantitatively. Finally, I'd connect this forward explicitly: everything interesting about ChatGPT's training relative to InstructGPT's is "the same three-stage pipeline, reformatted for multi-turn conversation and a broader, more product-driven prompt distribution," not a new alignment technique — which is itself evidence of how quickly this particular recipe became the field's default rather than one option among many.
