## PaLM (2022)

### 1. Overview & Strategic Context

PaLM (Pathways Language Model), introduced by Chowdhery et al. (Google Research, April 2022), is a 540-billion-parameter dense decoder-only Transformer. It sits at a specific inflection point in the GPT-3-era scaling race: it was Google's answer to the question "what happens if you take the GPT-3 recipe and push parameter count, data quality, and hardware scale further, while also building the systems software needed to actually orchestrate a training run that large across more accelerators than fit in a single physical pod?" The paper is as much a systems paper as a modeling paper -- the title explicitly foregrounds "Pathways," the distributed execution system used to train it, rather than a purely architectural claim.

Strategically, PaLM served three purposes for Google. First, it was a capability demonstration: at 540B parameters it was, at release, one of the largest dense language models trained, and it set new state-of-the-art few-shot results on a large fraction of the ~150 language understanding and generation benchmarks it was evaluated on, including outperforming average human performance on BIG-bench. Second, it was a scaling-law data point: Google released three sizes (8B, 62B, 540B) trained on identical data and procedure, which let researchers study scaling behavior cleanly and directly motivated the "emergent abilities" literature. Third, it validated Pathways -- Google's next-generation ML orchestration system meant to succeed the single-pod-bound training setups used for prior large models -- as viable for production-scale frontier training, which mattered strategically because it de-risked Google's internal infrastructure bet for everything that followed (PaLM 2, Gemini).

PaLM is a pretrained, few-shot-prompted model in its original paper; it is not RLHF-tuned or instruction-tuned in the base release (instruction-tuned descendants like Flan-PaLM came later as separate work). It should be read as a scaling and systems paper first, an alignment paper not at all.

### 2. Architecture Deep Dive

PaLM is a standard decoder-only, causal, autoregressive Transformer with several deliberate deviations from the vanilla design, each chosen for a specific efficiency or stability reason at 540B scale:

- **Parallel attention + FFN sublayers.** Instead of the standard sequential arrangement -- `x = x + Attn(LN(x))` then `x = x + FFN(LN(x))` -- PaLM computes both sublayers from the *same* normalized input and sums their outputs: `x = x + Attn(LN(x)) + FFN(LN(x))`. This is a formulation used earlier in GPT-J-6B and popularized further via PaLM's use at scale. The practical motivation is throughput: the two large matrix multiplications (the attention input projections and the FFN's first projection) can be fused into a single larger matmul, and likewise the output projections can be fused, which improves MFU on TPU MXUs by presenting fewer, larger operations to the hardware. The paper reports this gives roughly a 15% training speedup at large scale, with the paper explicitly noting the quality delta from this change is close to neutral at 62B/540B scale (small ablations showed a marginally larger negative effect at 8B, i.e. the parallel formulation trades a little quality for throughput, and that trade gets more favorable as scale increases).
- **Multi-query attention (MQA).** Standard multi-head attention gives every head its own key and value projection. PaLM shares a single key/value head across all query heads while keeping per-head queries. This has ~no effect on training speed (training is already parallel across the sequence) but is a large win for autoregressive decoding: the KV cache shrinks by a factor of the number of heads, and decoding becomes far less memory-bandwidth-bound, which matters enormously at 540B where the KV cache would otherwise dominate inference cost.
- **SwiGLU activation** in the MLP block, in place of ReLU/GeLU, following the by-then-established finding (Shazeer, 2020) that gated linear units with the Swish nonlinearity improve quality per FLOP.
- **RoPE (Rotary Position Embeddings)** rather than learned absolute or relative position embeddings, chosen for better extrapolation to longer sequences than seen during training.
- **Shared input/output embeddings** (the unembedding matrix is the transpose of the token embedding matrix), a standard parameter-saving and regularizing choice.
- **No biases** in any dense layer or layer norm. The paper reports this increases training stability at large scale, at negligible cost.
- **A 256k-token SentencePiece vocabulary** trained directly on the training corpus (rather than reusing an existing vocabulary), chosen to losslessly represent the multilingual and code content in the corpus, including preserving whitespace for code.

Model depth/width follow standard Transformer scaling for the three released sizes (8B, 62B, 540B); the 540B configuration uses 118 layers, a model dimension around 18,432, and 48 attention heads, though the paper's headline architectural claims are the parallel-block formulation and MQA above rather than any change to the layer-stacking pattern itself.

### 3. Scale -- Parameters, Data, Compute

PaLM's 540B parameter count is fully confirmed and precise -- it is a paper with complete disclosure, not a technical-report-with-redactions like the later Gemini family. Three dense (non-MoE) variants were trained end-to-end on identical data and hyperparameter recipe, differing only in width/depth: 8B, 62B, and 540B parameters. Training used 780 billion tokens for all three sizes (i.e., token count was held fixed across the scaling comparison, which is exactly what makes the emergent-abilities observations at 540B a clean scaling-law data point rather than a confound of also changing the data budget).

Training compute for the 540B run is reported at roughly 2.56 x 10^24 FLOPs, computed as the standard 6ND (6 x parameters x tokens) approximation the paper itself uses to report hardware FLOPs utilization. The training run used 6144 TPU v4 chips total, split as two TPU v4 Pods of 3072 chips each -- at the time, the largest TPU configuration used for a single training run, and specifically larger than fits inside one physical pod, which is precisely the constraint Pathways was built to remove.

### 4. Training Infrastructure & Distributed Training

This is PaLM's genuine systems-research contribution and the part of the paper that most differentiates it from a pure scaling exercise. Prior large models (GPT-3, Megatron-Turing NLG, Gopher) were trained within the bounds of a single pod or a single large contiguous accelerator island, because cross-pod (cross-island) communication bandwidth is dramatically lower than intra-pod bandwidth, and naive data-parallel or model-parallel training across pods historically caused throughput to collapse -- the cross-pod links become the bottleneck and utilization craters.

**Pathways** (Barham et al., 2022) is a new distributed ML system designed specifically to solve this. It introduces a single-controller architecture for orchestrating computation across thousands of accelerator chips spanning multiple pods, expressed as a dataflow graph of components, with the Pathways runtime scheduling and dispatching that graph efficiently rather than relying on the SPMD-everywhere model that prior systems (and most of PaLM's contemporaries) used exclusively.

For PaLM specifically, Pathways enabled a two-level parallelism strategy that maps cleanly onto the pod boundary:
- **Within each pod** (3072 TPU v4 chips): standard model/tensor parallelism via GSPMD (the SPMD partitioner Google uses for sharding large models across chips), combined with data parallelism across the chips within that pod.
- **Across the two pods**: pure data parallelism at the pod level. Each pod computes gradients on its own data shard; Pathways coordinates a two-way gradient transfer between the pods over the datacenter network (DCN, not the much faster intra-pod interconnect), and this transfer is overlapped with the backward pass compute on the *next* step so the cross-pod communication is largely hidden rather than sitting on the critical path.

The result the paper reports is a hardware FLOPs utilization (as opposed to model FLOPs utilization, but the paper's key claim is on **model FLOPs utilization, MFU**) of 46.2% for the 540B run -- notably higher than the roughly 20s-percent MFU reported for comparable-era large models (GPT-3, Gopher, MT-NLG), and the paper attributes this directly to the parallel-sublayer formulation (fewer, larger fused matmuls) plus Pathways' ability to keep both pods' accelerators busy despite the run spanning two pods. This MFU figure is one of the most-cited numbers from the paper precisely because it's a rare, concrete, apples-to-comparable-scale efficiency claim in an era where most labs disclosed nothing about training efficiency.

Additional engineering details disclosed: the paper describes using a redefined "loss spike" recovery procedure -- periodically checkpointing, and when a training instability (loss spike) was detected, rolling back roughly 100 steps before the spike and skipping ahead through a few hundred data batches before resuming, rather than tuning around every instability live. This is disclosed as an ad hoc but effective mitigation, in contrast to more principled instability-avoidance work in later models.

### 5. Pretraining Data & Objective

The training objective is standard autoregressive language modeling (next-token cross-entropy). The 780B-token training mixture is a high-quality, curated blend spanning: filtered webpages, English and multilingual Wikipedia, books (English), news articles, source code (~24 programming languages, scraped from GitHub, filtered by license/repo criteria), and social-media conversations (used specifically to inject informal, conversational register that formal corpora like Wikipedia/books/news lack). The vocabulary (256k SentencePiece tokens, trained on this corpus) preserves whitespace for code and does not perform lossy normalization, which the paper notes matters for code fidelity. No dropout was used during pretraining (dropout of 0.1 is applied only when fine-tuning on smaller downstream datasets), consistent with the single-epoch, data-rich pretraining regime typical of this generation of models.

### 6. Post-Training / Alignment Approach

The PaLM paper itself does no RLHF or instruction-tuning -- the base model is evaluated purely via few-shot (and some zero-shot) prompting, plus chain-of-thought prompting for reasoning tasks. The paper does include a fine-tuning study (PaLM fine-tuned on a code-heavy mixture, referred to informally as PaLM-Coder, and other task-specific fine-tunes) to show the base model is a good fine-tuning starting point, but there is no safety-tuning or preference-optimization stage in the original release. Instruction-tuned and RLHF-adjacent descendants (Flan-PaLM, and later PaLM's role as a component in more aligned successor systems) are separate follow-on work, not part of the PaLM paper itself.

### 7. Key Research Contributions & Novel Techniques

Two contributions define PaLM's legacy:

1. **Pathways as a validated cross-pod training system.** PaLM is the proof point that a single training run can span multiple physical TPU pods without the cross-pod communication collapse that had previously kept large model training confined to single-pod boundaries, via the single-controller/dataflow-graph design plus overlapped cross-pod gradient transfer described in Section 4. This is a genuine systems contribution independent of the model's quality, and it is the piece of PaLM's legacy that most directly enabled subsequent even-larger Google training runs.
2. **Discontinuous, "emergent" task improvements at 540B.** Across the three same-data, same-recipe sizes (8B/62B/540B), PaLM's paper documents several benchmark tasks (e.g., certain BIG-bench tasks) where performance is roughly flat (near chance) from 8B to 62B and then jumps sharply at 540B -- a discontinuity not predicted by extrapolating the 8B-to-62B trend. The paper explicitly frames this as evidence of emergent capabilities that only manifest past some scale threshold. This observation, alongside similar findings from other models, fed directly into Wei et al.'s "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models" (2022) and became a central (and later contested) data point in the broader emergent-abilities debate -- subsequent work (e.g., Schaeffer et al., 2023, "Are Emergent Abilities a Mirage?") argued some of these discontinuities are artifacts of using non-linear/discontinuous evaluation metrics (like exact-match accuracy) rather than evidence of a genuine phase transition in the underlying model. PaLM's data is frequently cited on both sides of that argument.

Additional smaller contributions: strong chain-of-thought + self-consistency results showing that reasoning-style prompting compounds with scale (CoT gains are themselves larger at 540B than at smaller sizes on multi-step arithmetic/commonsense reasoning); and the MFU efficiency result itself, which was, at the time, an unusually transparent and citable efficiency benchmark.

### 8. Training Challenges & Engineering Solutions

- **Loss spikes / instability at scale.** Roughly 20 loss spikes occurred over training that were not fully explained by any single root cause the team could isolate (the paper is candid about this). The mitigation used was operationally pragmatic rather than principled: checkpoint frequently, and on detecting a spike, restart training from a checkpoint ~100 steps earlier while skipping the batches that immediately preceded the spike, rather than diagnosing and fixing the root cause live.
- **Cross-pod communication as a throughput risk.** Addressed structurally by Pathways' design (see Section 4) rather than by a training-time workaround -- overlapping the two-way gradient transfer between pods with compute, so the slower DCN link is hidden behind useful work rather than sitting on the critical path.
- **Fused matmuls for MXU efficiency.** The parallel attention+FFN formulation was explicitly chosen as an engineering lever to convert several smaller matmuls into fewer, larger ones, which TPU matrix units (MXUs) execute much more efficiently -- an example of an architectural choice being made primarily for systems efficiency rather than modeling quality.
- **Vocabulary/tokenizer fidelity for code and multilingual text.** Training a dedicated 256k SentencePiece vocabulary directly on the target corpus (rather than reusing GPT-2/GPT-3-style byte-pair vocabularies) was an engineering choice to avoid excess fragmentation on code whitespace and non-English scripts.

### 9. Inference & Serving Considerations

The paper is pretraining/systems-focused and says comparatively little about production serving, but two architectural choices are directly inference-motivated: multi-query attention (shrinks the KV cache by the head-count factor, easing memory-bandwidth pressure during autoregressive decoding at 540B) and shared input/output embeddings (parameter savings that also slightly reduce the model's memory footprint at serve time). Beyond these, PaLM at 540B dense is, by construction, an expensive model to serve -- every token activates all 540B parameters, unlike the sparse MoE architectures Google would adopt later specifically to decouple total capacity from per-token compute cost. This dense-and-expensive-to-serve profile is part of why PaLM 2's smaller-but-better positioning (see the next file) was such a significant strategic shift.

### 10. Evaluation, Benchmarks & Known Limitations

PaLM 540B set new few-shot state-of-the-art results on a majority of the ~150 tasks it was evaluated against at release, and exceeded average human rater performance on the aggregate BIG-bench suite. It showed strong results on multi-step reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K-style arithmetic word problems) when combined with chain-of-thought prompting, and on code generation/translation/bug-fixing after fine-tuning. Known limitations disclosed or later identified: no RLHF/instruction-tuning in the base model means it is a comparatively weak conversational assistant out of the box relative to instruction-tuned contemporaries; dense 540B parameters make it costly to serve; the unexplained loss spikes indicate training-stability understanding at this scale was still immature; and the "emergent abilities" claims drawn from PaLM's benchmark discontinuities were later partially challenged as measurement artifacts rather than genuine capability phase transitions.

### 11. Confirmed Facts vs. Speculation

**Confirmed (directly stated in the paper):** 540B (and 8B, 62B) dense parameter counts; 780B training tokens for all three sizes; two TPU v4 Pods of 3072 chips each (6144 chips total); parallel attention+FFN sublayer formulation with reported ~15% training speedup; multi-query attention; SwiGLU; RoPE; no biases; shared embeddings; 256k SentencePiece vocabulary; 46.2% model FLOPs utilization; ~20 unexplained loss spikes and the checkpoint-rollback mitigation; no RLHF in the base model.

**Speculation / not fully specified:** the paper does not give an exhaustive per-layer breakdown suitable for exact reproduction (e.g., some hyperparameter and initialization details require cross-referencing the appendix and are occasionally under-specified relative to a full reproducibility bar); the exact composition percentages of the 780B-token mixture by source are given only approximately; and any claims about how directly PaLM's Pathways implementation maps onto Gemini's later training stack are reasonable inference from Google's own statements about infrastructure continuity, not something either paper states explicitly.

### 12. Staff/Research Interview Talking Points

- Be able to explain *why* naive multi-pod training collapses in throughput (cross-pod link bandwidth is far below intra-pod interconnect bandwidth, so any parallelism strategy that puts synchronous, latency-sensitive communication on that link stalls the whole run) and *specifically* how Pathways avoids it (single-controller dataflow scheduling + confining cheap-bandwidth-tolerant communication, i.e. periodic gradient exchange, to the cross-pod link, while keeping bandwidth-hungry tensor-parallel communication intra-pod).
- Be able to state the parallel-sublayer trick precisely: same LayerNorm output feeds both attention and FFN, outputs summed, motivated by fusing matmuls for MXU efficiency, with an approximately neutral-to-slightly-negative quality tradeoff that improves with scale.
- Know the MFU number (46.2%) and be able to explain what model FLOPs utilization measures and why it's a more meaningful cross-model efficiency comparison than raw hardware utilization or wall-clock time alone.
- Be ready to discuss the emergent-abilities controversy from both directions: PaLM's discontinuous benchmark jumps as originally reported, and the later "mirage" critique (metric non-linearity/discontinuity as an alternative explanation to a genuine capability phase transition) -- this is a favorite probing question because it tests whether a candidate treats "emergence" as settled science or as an ongoing empirical debate.
- Understand why multi-query attention was included for inference reasons despite having no training-time benefit -- a good example of an architecture decision driven by a downstream systems concern rather than pretraining loss.
