# Market Microstructure Interview

## Q1: What are the components of the bid-ask spread?

**A1:** The bid-ask spread has three main components: (1) Order processing costs: the cost of operating the market (technology, clearing, settlement). This is typically small and relatively constant. (2) Inventory risk: market makers hold inventory and face price risk. They widen spreads to compensate for holding positions, especially in volatile or illiquid securities. The spread increases with inventory level and volatility. (3) Adverse selection: informed traders exploit market makers who don't know fundamental value. When a market maker trades with an informed trader, they lose money on average. To break even, they widen spreads to offset losses to informed traders with profits from uninformed traders. The adverse selection component is often the largest and varies with information asymmetry. In practice, spreads also reflect competition (more market makers narrow spreads), tick size constraints (minimum price increments), and market conditions (wider in stress). The spread decomposition helps understand market quality and can guide market making strategies.

## Q2: Explain Kyle's lambda and its interpretation.

**A2:** Kyle's lambda (λ) measures price impact: the change in price per unit of order flow. In Kyle's model, informed traders submit orders to maximize profit, and market makers set prices based on order flow. The equilibrium price impact is λ = σ_v / (2σ_u), where σ_v is volatility of fundamental value and σ_u is volatility of noise trading. Interpretation: λ measures market depth (inverse of liquidity). High λ means small orders move prices a lot (illiquid). Low λ means large orders have little impact (liquid). Lambda is estimated empirically as the regression coefficient: ΔP = λ * Q + ε, where Q is signed order flow (buy volume - sell volume). In practice, lambda varies by: order size (larger orders have higher impact, often non-linear), time of day, volatility, and market conditions. Traders use lambda to estimate execution costs and optimize order sizing. Market makers monitor lambda to assess adverse selection risk. High lambda suggests informed trading or low liquidity, requiring wider spreads or more cautious quoting.

## Q3: How can order book imbalance be used as a trading signal?

**A3:** Order book imbalance measures the relative pressure from buy vs sell orders. Common metrics: (1) Volume imbalance: (Bid volume - Ask volume) / (Bid volume + Ask volume), ranges from -1 to +1. Positive means more buy pressure. (2) Price-level imbalance: weighted by price levels, giving more weight to orders closer to mid-price. (3) Order flow imbalance: signed volume at best bid/ask over time. (4) Microprice: (Bid*Ask_size + Ask*Bid_size) / (Bid_size + Ask_size), a volume-weighted mid-price that tilts toward the side with less volume. Intuition: if there are more buy orders than sell orders, price should rise as those orders execute. Empirical evidence shows short-term predictability: high imbalance predicts positive returns over seconds to minutes. However, the signal decays quickly and transaction costs can erode profits. Uses: (1) Market making: adjust quotes based on imbalance to manage inventory. (2) Execution: trade on the light side (fewer orders) to reduce impact. (3) Short-term alpha: predict price moves, though capacity is limited. (4) Optimal execution: time trades when imbalance is favorable. The signal is strongest in liquid markets and during normal trading hours. It's a high-frequency signal requiring low latency infrastructure.

## Q4: How do market makers manage inventory risk?

**A4:** Market makers quote bid and ask prices, accumulating inventory as they trade. Inventory risk is price risk from holding positions. Management strategies: (1) Inventory-based quoting: widen spreads and skew quotes (move bid/ask away from mid-price) when inventory is large. For example, if long inventory, lower the bid more than raising the ask to discourage buys and encourage sells. (2) Dynamic hedging: hedge inventory with futures, ETFs, or correlated securities to reduce exposure. (3) Position limits: stop quoting or reduce size when inventory exceeds thresholds. (4) Mean reversion: if prices are mean-reverting, holding inventory can be profitable, but if trending, it's costly. (5) Information: monitor order flow—if trades are one-sided, it may signal informed trading, requiring inventory reduction. (6) Time-based: reduce inventory before market close or news events. The optimal strategy balances: profit from spreads, cost of inventory risk, and adverse selection. In practice, market makers use models (e.g., Avellaneda-Stoikov) that optimize quotes based on inventory, time to expiration, and volatility. The goal is to maximize expected profit while controlling inventory risk, often targeting zero inventory by end of day.

## Q5: Compare VWAP and arrival price as execution benchmarks.

**A5:** VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price) is the average execution price weighted by volume: VWAP = Σ(P_i * V_i) / ΣV_i over the benchmark period (e.g., full day). It's a passive benchmark: if you trade at VWAP, you match average market performance. Advantages: widely used, easy to compute, not affected by your own trading. Disadvantages: doesn't account for market impact of your order, can be gamed (trade when volume is low), and doesn't measure opportunity cost. Arrival price is the mid-price at order arrival time. It measures: did you improve or worsen from the starting point? Advantages: measures true execution quality relative to decision price, accounts for opportunity cost (what if you didn't trade), and is harder to game. Disadvantages: sensitive to timing (arrival price may be noisy), and doesn't account for market conditions. Choice depends on context: VWAP for passive execution over a period, arrival price for measuring trader skill or opportunity cost. Many funds use arrival price for internal evaluation and VWAP for client reporting. Both should be adjusted for market movement (market-adjusted) to isolate execution skill.

## Q6: Explain the Almgren-Chriss optimal execution model.

**A6:** Almgren-Chriss minimizes expected execution cost plus risk aversion. The model assumes: price follows arithmetic Brownian motion dS = σ dW, temporary impact is linear h(n) = ηn (where n is trading rate), and permanent impact is linear g(x) = γx (where x is remaining shares). The cost function is: C = Σ[x_k * (S_k - S_0)] + Σ[n_k * h(n_k)] + λ * Var[cost], where λ is risk aversion. The first term is opportunity cost, the second is temporary impact cost, and the third penalizes execution risk. The optimal trading trajectory (how many shares to trade each period) balances: trading faster reduces opportunity cost but increases impact cost and risk. Solution: the optimal schedule is exponential: n(t) = (X/T) * [1 - e^(-κ(T-t))] / [1 - e^(-κT)], where κ depends on risk aversion and impact parameters. High risk aversion → trade faster initially (front-loading). Low risk aversion → trade more evenly. The model provides a framework for optimal execution but has limitations: assumes linear impact (often non-linear in practice), constant volatility (volatility clustering), and doesn't account for alpha decay or information leakage. Extensions add stochastic volatility, alpha signals, and adaptive strategies.

## Q7: What is the difference between temporary and permanent market impact?

**A7:** Market impact is the price change caused by trading. Temporary impact is transient: price moves during trading but reverts afterward. It's caused by: order book depletion (consuming liquidity), short-term supply/demand imbalance, and transitory liquidity effects. Temporary impact decays quickly (seconds to minutes) as new orders arrive. Permanent impact persists: price doesn't fully revert. It's caused by: information revelation (your trade signals information), inventory effects on other market participants, and fundamental supply/demand changes. In practice, impact is measured by: I = (P_exec - P_before) / P_before, and decomposed into temporary (reverts) and permanent (doesn't revert) components. Permanent impact is typically smaller but more costly long-term. For large orders, temporary impact dominates initially, but permanent impact accumulates. Models like Almgren-Chriss separate them: temporary impact affects execution cost, permanent impact affects final price. Understanding the split helps: (1) Execution: can you wait for temporary impact to decay? (2) Cost attribution: how much is truly "cost" vs "alpha"? (3) Strategy: if impact is mostly temporary, splitting orders helps; if permanent, it's unavoidable cost. Empirical measurement uses price reversion analysis or compares to unaffected prices.

## Q8: What are dark pools and what are their pros and cons?

**A8:** Dark pools are private trading venues where orders are not displayed publicly. Orders are matched internally or with other dark pools. Pros: (1) Reduced market impact: large orders don't signal intentions to the broader market. (2) Lower adverse selection: less informed trading than lit markets. (3) Price improvement: can get better prices than public quotes. (4) Anonymity: don't reveal trading interest. Cons: (1) Lack of transparency: can't see order book, making price discovery harder. (2) Information leakage: participation may signal large orders exist. (3) Fragmentation: liquidity is split across venues, reducing depth. (4) Adverse selection: some dark pools attract informed traders or toxic flow. (5) Execution uncertainty: orders may not fill, or fills may be delayed. (6) Regulatory concerns: potential conflicts of interest (broker-owned pools), and questions about best execution. Dark pools work best for: large institutional orders, when market impact is a primary concern, and when you have time (not urgent). They're less suitable for: small orders (use lit markets), urgent trades, or when you need guaranteed execution. The rise of dark pools has fragmented markets, creating challenges for price discovery and best execution.

## Q9: Give an overview of high-frequency trading (HFT) strategies.

**A9:** HFT uses algorithms and low-latency infrastructure to trade at millisecond or microsecond speeds. Main strategies: (1) Market making: provide liquidity by quoting bid/ask, profit from spread while managing inventory. Requires fast risk management and quote updates. (2) Arbitrage: exploit price differences across venues (statistical arbitrage, cross-exchange arbitrage) or related securities (ETF vs underlying, futures vs spot). Profits are small but frequent. (3) Latency arbitrage: use speed advantage to trade on stale quotes or public information before others react. (4) Order flow prediction: use order book signals, news, or other data to predict short-term price moves. (5) Liquidity detection: identify large hidden orders and trade ahead or provide liquidity. (6) Market microstructure strategies: exploit tick size rules, order types, or exchange mechanics. Key requirements: co-location (servers near exchange), fast data feeds, low-latency execution, and sophisticated risk systems. HFT is controversial: proponents argue it provides liquidity and tightens spreads; critics argue it creates instability, advantages insiders, and doesn't add value. HFT profits have declined as competition increased and markets became more efficient.

## Q10: Explain co-location and why latency matters in trading.

**A10:** Co-location means placing trading servers physically near exchange matching engines, minimizing network latency. Latency is the time from signal (e.g., price update) to action (e.g., order submission). Why it matters: (1) Speed advantage: being first to react to information (news, order flow, arbitrage opportunities) captures profits before others. (2) Market making: faster quote updates reduce adverse selection risk and improve inventory management. (3) Arbitrage: price differences exist briefly; faster execution captures more opportunities. (4) Order flow: seeing orders first allows front-running or better positioning. Latency components: (1) Network: distance × speed of light (~5 microseconds per km in fiber), plus switching delays. (2) Processing: computation time for strategies, risk checks, order routing. (3) Exchange: matching engine processing time. Co-location reduces network latency from milliseconds to microseconds. The cost is high (rental fees, infrastructure), but for HFT, microseconds matter. A 10-microsecond advantage can be worth millions annually for high-volume strategies. However, as more firms co-locate, the advantage diminishes, and the focus shifts to algorithms and data processing speed rather than just physical proximity.

## Q11: How does tick size affect market liquidity?

**A11:** Tick size is the minimum price increment (e.g., $0.01 for stocks, $0.25 for some futures). Effects: (1) Spreads: smaller tick size allows tighter spreads, improving liquidity for small orders. However, if tick size is too small, spreads may be one tick, reducing price improvement opportunities. (2) Depth: larger tick size concentrates orders at fewer price levels, increasing depth at those levels but reducing granularity. (3) Market making profitability: larger tick size increases minimum spread, making market making more profitable and attracting more liquidity providers. (4) Price discovery: smaller tick size allows finer price discovery but may increase noise. (5) Order types: tick size affects the value of limit orders vs market orders. (6) Fragmentation: different tick sizes across venues can fragment liquidity. The optimal tick size balances: tight spreads (smaller is better) vs market maker incentives (larger is better). Too small: market makers can't profit, reducing liquidity. Too large: spreads are wide, increasing costs. Regulators have experimented with tick size pilots (e.g., wider ticks for small stocks to improve liquidity). In practice, tick size is often binding for liquid securities (spreads are one tick) but not for illiquid ones.

## Q12: What caused the 2010 Flash Crash and what lessons were learned?

**A12:** On May 6, 2010, U.S. markets dropped ~9% in minutes then recovered. Causes: (1) Large sell order: a single large sell order (E-mini futures) executed via algorithm without proper safeguards. (2) Liquidity evaporation: as prices fell, market makers widened spreads and pulled quotes, reducing liquidity. (3) Feedback loops: falling prices triggered stop-losses and margin calls, causing more selling. (4) Cross-market contagion: selling in futures spilled to equities via arbitrage. (5) HFT behavior: some HFT firms pulled liquidity during stress, exacerbating the crash. (6) Fragmentation: orders routed across venues, making it hard to see full liquidity. Lessons: (1) Circuit breakers: implemented market-wide halts (e.g., 7% drop triggers 15-minute halt). (2) Order size limits: prevent extremely large orders. (3) Market maker obligations: some venues require market makers to provide liquidity during stress. (4) Better risk controls: algorithms need kill switches and position limits. (5) Coordination: exchanges need better coordination during stress. (6) Monitoring: real-time surveillance to detect anomalies. The flash crash highlighted market fragility and the role of HFT and algorithms. It led to regulatory changes and improved risk management practices, though similar events (e.g., 2020 COVID crash) show markets remain vulnerable to liquidity crises.

## Q13: What is market fragmentation and how does it affect trading?

**A13:** Market fragmentation means trading occurs across multiple venues (exchanges, dark pools, ECNs) rather than a single central market. Causes: (1) Regulation: Regulation NMS in the U.S. requires routing to best prices across venues. (2) Technology: electronic trading enabled new venues. (3) Competition: new venues compete on fees, speed, or features. Effects: (1) Liquidity splitting: orders are spread across venues, reducing visible depth at any single venue. (2) Price discovery: harder to see true supply/demand when liquidity is fragmented. (3) Execution complexity: need smart order routing to access all venues, increasing complexity and latency. (4) Adverse selection: some venues may have worse flow (toxic order flow). (5) Costs: need connections to multiple venues, data feeds from each. (6) Best execution: harder to ensure best execution when checking multiple venues. Benefits: (1) Competition: venues compete on fees and service. (2) Innovation: new venues introduce new features. (3) Choice: traders can choose venues that fit their needs. Fragmentation is managed via: consolidated tape (aggregated quotes), smart order routing, and regulations ensuring fair access. The trade-off is complexity vs competition. In practice, fragmentation has increased, requiring sophisticated technology to navigate effectively.

## Q14: Explain payment for order flow (PFOF) and its implications.

**A14:** PFOF is when market makers pay brokers for routing retail orders to them. The market maker profits from the spread (buying at bid, selling at ask) and shares some profit with the broker. Implications: (1) Zero-commission trading: brokers can offer "free" trading because they're paid by market makers. (2) Price improvement: market makers may offer better prices than public quotes to attract flow. (3) Conflict of interest: brokers may route to highest-paying market makers rather than best execution. (4) Market structure: concentrates retail flow with a few market makers (e.g., Citadel, Virtu). (5) Transparency: retail traders may not know their orders are being sold. (6) Execution quality: debate over whether PFOF improves or worsens execution for retail. Proponents argue: retail gets price improvement and zero commissions. Critics argue: brokers prioritize payment over best execution, and market makers profit at retail's expense. Regulators require disclosure and best execution obligations, but enforcement is challenging. PFOF is controversial and has been scrutinized, especially after events like GameStop, where questions arose about order routing and execution quality. The practice exists in equities and options but is less common in other asset classes.

## Q15: How do you determine optimal order sizing in execution?

**A15:** Optimal order sizing balances execution cost (market impact) vs opportunity cost (price moves away). Factors: (1) Urgency: urgent orders trade faster (larger sizes per period), accepting higher impact. Patient orders can split into smaller pieces. (2) Alpha decay: if you have alpha (expected return), it may decay over time, favoring faster execution. (3) Volatility: high volatility increases both impact and opportunity cost, requiring careful balancing. (4) Liquidity: more liquid securities allow larger sizes with less impact. (5) Order book: check available liquidity at each price level to determine feasible sizes. (6) Risk limits: position limits, VaR constraints may cap sizes. Methods: (1) TWAP: trade equal amounts each period. Simple but ignores market conditions. (2) VWAP: trade proportional to expected volume. Matches market participation. (3) Implementation shortfall: minimize expected cost using models like Almgren-Chriss. (4) Adaptive: adjust sizes based on realized impact, volatility, and order book. (5) IS strategy: trade faster when you have alpha, slower when impact is high. In practice, traders use a combination: start with a model (e.g., Almgren-Chriss), then adapt based on market conditions, realized impact, and changing information. The goal is to minimize total cost: impact + opportunity cost + risk.

## Q16: What is Transaction Cost Analysis (TCA) and how is it performed?

**A16:** TCA measures and analyzes execution costs to evaluate trading performance and improve strategies. Components: (1) Execution cost: difference between execution price and benchmark (arrival price, VWAP, etc.). (2) Market impact: temporary and permanent price impact from trading. (3) Timing cost: opportunity cost from delayed execution. (4) Spread cost: crossing the bid-ask spread. (5) Market movement: cost from adverse price moves during execution. Methodology: (1) Data collection: execution prices, sizes, timestamps, benchmarks, market data. (2) Cost calculation: compare execution to benchmark, decompose into components. (3) Attribution: attribute costs to factors (venue, algorithm, market conditions, order characteristics). (4) Benchmarking: compare to peer performance, historical averages, or theoretical models. (5) Reporting: provide reports to traders and management. Advanced TCA uses: regression analysis to identify cost drivers, pre-trade models to predict costs, and post-trade analysis to validate models. Uses: (1) Performance evaluation: did traders/algorithms perform well? (2) Strategy improvement: identify what works and what doesn't. (3) Venue selection: which venues provide best execution? (4) Algorithm selection: which algorithms are best for which order types? (5) Cost budgeting: estimate costs for portfolio construction. Good TCA requires clean data, appropriate benchmarks, and statistical rigor to separate skill from luck.

## Q17: How can information leakage occur in trading and how is it managed?

**A17:** Information leakage is when trading intentions or information become known to others before execution, causing adverse price moves. Sources: (1) Order routing: brokers or venues may signal large orders exist. (2) Market impact: large orders move prices, revealing information. (3) Pattern recognition: repeated patterns (e.g., same time each day) can be detected. (4) Data sales: order flow data sold to market makers may reveal information. (5) Leaks: internal information accidentally or intentionally leaked. (6) Correlation: trading correlated securities reveals information about others. Effects: prices move against you before you finish trading, increasing costs. Management: (1) Order splitting: break large orders into smaller pieces to reduce visibility. (2) Timing randomization: vary execution times to avoid patterns. (3) Venue selection: use dark pools or venues with less information leakage. (4) Algorithm selection: use algorithms designed to minimize information leakage (e.g., stealth algorithms). (5) Cross-asset: trade in less correlated markets or use derivatives. (6) Speed: execute quickly before information spreads, though this increases impact. (7) Monitoring: track whether prices move before your orders execute. The trade-off is between information leakage (favoring fast execution) and market impact (favoring slow execution). Optimal strategies balance both, often using adaptive algorithms that adjust based on observed leakage and impact.

## Q18: Decompose market maker P&L into its components.

**A18:** Market maker P&L comes from: (1) Spread capture: profit from buying at bid and selling at ask. If you trade Q shares with spread s, P&L = Q * s/2 (assuming balanced flow). This is the primary revenue source. (2) Inventory P&L: profit/loss from price moves on inventory. If you're long I shares and price moves by ΔP, P&L = I * ΔP. This can be positive (favorable moves) or negative (adverse moves). (3) Adverse selection: losses from trading with informed traders. When you trade with informed flow, you lose on average. This is a cost, not revenue. (4) Order flow imbalance: if buy and sell flow are imbalanced, you accumulate inventory, creating risk. (5) Competition: other market makers may quote better prices, reducing your fill rate and spread capture. (6) Operational costs: technology, data, co-location, personnel. Net P&L = Spread revenue - Adverse selection costs - Inventory losses - Operational costs. Successful market makers: (1) Capture spreads efficiently (high fill rates, tight quotes). (2) Minimize adverse selection (avoid toxic flow, use information). (3) Manage inventory (hedge, skew quotes, limit positions). (4) Control costs (efficient operations, technology). The business is high-volume, low-margin: small profits per trade, but many trades. Risk management is crucial: a few bad trades can wipe out many good ones.

## Q19: What is realized spread and how does it differ from quoted spread?

**A19:** Quoted spread is the difference between best bid and best ask: S_quoted = Ask - Bid. It's what you see in the order book. Realized spread is the effective spread after accounting for price moves: S_realized = 2 * |P_exec - P_mid|, where P_exec is execution price and P_mid is mid-price at trade time. Alternatively, for a market maker: S_realized = 2 * Q * (P_after - P_exec), where Q is signed trade size (+1 for buy, -1 for sell) and P_after is price after some time horizon (e.g., 5 minutes). This measures: did the market maker profit after the trade? If price moves against them (adverse selection), realized spread is lower than quoted spread. Difference: (1) Quoted spread is ex-ante (before trade), realized spread is ex-post (after trade). (2) Realized spread accounts for adverse selection: if you buy and price falls, you didn't capture the full quoted spread. (3) Realized spread measures true profitability for market makers. (4) Effective spread (similar to realized) is what traders actually pay: 2 * |P_exec - P_mid|. In practice, realized spread is often lower than quoted spread due to adverse selection, especially in markets with informed trading. Market makers need realized spread > 0 to be profitable. The difference (quoted - realized) measures adverse selection costs.

## Q20: How does price impact decay over time and why does it matter?

**A20:** Price impact from trading decays over time as new information arrives and liquidity replenishes. Temporary impact (from order book depletion) decays quickly (seconds to minutes) as new orders fill the book. Permanent impact (from information) may persist longer or permanently. Empirical studies show impact decays roughly as: I(t) = I₀ * t^(-α), where α is the decay exponent (typically 0.5-1.0). Why it matters: (1) Execution strategy: if impact decays quickly, you can split orders and wait between trades to reduce total cost. If it decays slowly, splitting helps less. (2) Cost measurement: need to measure impact over appropriate horizons. Too short: miss temporary impact. Too long: include unrelated price moves. (3) Optimal execution: models like Obizhaeva-Wang incorporate impact decay, showing optimal schedules depend on decay rate. Fast decay → can trade more slowly. Slow decay → trade faster (impact won't decay much anyway). (4) Market making: understanding decay helps market makers manage inventory—if impact is temporary, holding inventory is less risky. (5) Post-trade analysis: attribute costs correctly by accounting for decay. Measurement: compare prices before, during, and after trading to separate temporary vs permanent impact. The decay rate varies by: security (more liquid = faster decay), market conditions (stress = slower decay), and order size (larger = slower decay). Understanding decay is crucial for execution optimization and cost management.
