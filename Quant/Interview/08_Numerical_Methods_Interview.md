# Numerical Methods Interview

## Q1: What is the convergence rate of Monte Carlo simulation?

**A1:** Monte Carlo has convergence rate O(1/√N), where N is the number of simulations. This follows from the central limit theorem: the error in the sample mean is approximately normally distributed with standard deviation σ/√N, where σ is the standard deviation of the payoff. To halve the error, you need to quadruple simulations (4× more work for 2× improvement). This is slow compared to deterministic methods (finite difference converges as O(h²) where h is grid spacing). However, MC's convergence rate is independent of problem dimension, making it superior for high-dimensional problems (e.g., basket options with many underlyings). The error is probabilistic: with 95% confidence, error is within ±1.96σ/√N. To achieve error ε, need N ≈ (1.96σ/ε)² simulations. Variance reduction techniques (antithetic variates, control variates) reduce σ, effectively improving convergence without increasing N.

## Q2: Compare variance reduction techniques: antithetic variates, control variates, and importance sampling.

**A2:** Antithetic variates: generate pairs (Z, -Z) where Z ~ N(0,1). If payoff is monotonic in Z, f(Z) and f(-Z) are negatively correlated, reducing variance. Variance reduction factor: if correlation is -1, variance halves (need half the simulations). Works well for options (payoffs are often monotonic). Control variates: use a correlated variable with known expectation. If Y is control with E[Y] known, estimate E[X] using X - c(Y - E[Y]) where c is chosen to minimize variance. Optimal c = Cov(X,Y)/Var(Y). If |correlation| > 0.7, significant reduction. Common controls: underlying asset (for options), discounted payoff of simpler option. Importance sampling: change probability measure to sample more from important regions (e.g., deep ITM for options). If f is payoff and g is density, E[f] = ∫ f(x)g(x)dx = ∫ [f(x)g(x)/h(x)]h(x)dx, where h is new density. Choose h to put mass where f(x)g(x) is large. Can reduce variance dramatically for tail events but requires knowing good h. Best use: antithetic for monotonic payoffs, control variates when good control exists, importance sampling for rare events.

## Q3: When should you use quasi-Monte Carlo instead of standard Monte Carlo?

**A3:** Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) uses deterministic low-discrepancy sequences (Sobol, Halton) instead of random numbers. Advantages: (1) Faster convergence: error is O((log N)^d/N) for d dimensions, better than O(1/√N) for MC when d is moderate. (2) Better coverage: low-discrepancy sequences fill space more evenly than random numbers, reducing clustering. (3) Deterministic: same sequence gives same result (useful for debugging). Disadvantages: (1) Deteriorates with dimension: (log N)^d factor grows, so QMC is worse than MC for very high d (typically d > 20-30). (2) Error bounds are deterministic but conservative (hard to get confidence intervals). (3) Less flexible: harder to adapt sequences for importance sampling. Use QMC when: (1) Moderate dimensions (d < 20-30). (2) Smooth payoffs (QMC works better for smooth functions). (3) Need faster convergence than MC. (4) Deterministic results are valuable. For high-dimensional problems (basket options with many assets) or when you need confidence intervals, standard MC may be better. In practice, QMC is often combined with randomization (randomized QMC) to get both faster convergence and error estimates.

## Q4: When is finite difference better than Monte Carlo, and vice versa?

**A4:** Finite difference (FD) solves PDEs on a grid. Better than MC when: (1) Low dimensions (1-3): FD has O(h²) convergence, much faster than MC's O(1/√N) for low d. (2) Need Greeks: FD computes all Greeks simultaneously by solving PDE once. MC requires separate simulations or finite differences of MC estimates. (3) Early exercise: American options require solving PDE with free boundary, which FD handles naturally. MC needs special methods (LSM). (4) Smooth payoffs: FD assumes smoothness (differentiability), works well for vanilla options. (5) Deterministic: FD gives exact solution (to discretization error), no Monte Carlo noise. MC is better when: (1) High dimensions: FD requires O((1/h)^d) grid points, exponential in d. MC is O(N) regardless of d. (2) Path-dependent options: Asian, lookback options need full paths, MC is natural. (3) Complex payoffs: discontinuous or highly nonlinear payoffs are easier in MC. (4) Multiple assets: basket options with many underlyings favor MC. (5) Stochastic volatility: 2D+ PDEs are complex; MC is simpler. Rule of thumb: use FD for 1-2D problems, MC for 3+D or path-dependent.

## Q5: Explain Crank-Nicolson stability and when to use it.

**A5:** Crank-Nicolson (CN) is an implicit finite difference scheme: it discretizes the PDE using average of forward and backward differences in time, giving second-order accuracy in both time and space. Stability: CN is unconditionally stable (no time step restriction), unlike explicit schemes (Euler) which require Δt < (Δx)²/(2σ²) for stability. This allows larger time steps, reducing computation. However, CN can have oscillations if initial conditions are discontinuous (e.g., option payoffs have kinks). The scheme: (V^{n+1} - V^n)/Δt = (1/2)[L(V^{n+1}) + L(V^n)], where L is spatial discretization. This creates a system of linear equations to solve at each step (tridiagonal for 1D, sparse for higher D). Use CN when: (1) Need accuracy: second-order in time is better than first-order (Euler). (2) Stability matters: unconditional stability allows larger steps. (3) Smooth solutions: works well for smooth payoffs. Alternatives: (1) Fully implicit: more stable but first-order in time. (2) Explicit: simpler but requires small time steps. (3) ADI (Alternating Direction Implicit): for 2D+, combines implicit/explicit. CN is standard for Black-Scholes PDE due to good balance of accuracy and stability.

## Q6: Compare methods for pricing American options: LSM vs PDE approaches.

**A6:** Longstaff-Schwartz Method (LSM): (1) Simulate paths forward using Monte Carlo. (2) At each exercise date, use regression (on basis functions of state) to estimate continuation value. (3) Exercise if immediate payoff > continuation value. (4) Average discounted payoffs. Advantages: handles high dimensions, path-dependent features, multiple assets. Disadvantages: low bias (underestimates value, as regression is approximate), computationally expensive (many paths needed), hard to get Greeks. PDE methods: (1) Solve free boundary problem: V(S,t) = max(payoff(S), continuation value). (2) Use finite difference with early exercise constraint enforced at each time step. Advantages: high accuracy, computes all Greeks, faster for low dimensions. Disadvantages: limited to low dimensions (typically 1-2D), harder for path-dependent. Comparison: LSM is standard for high-dimensional or path-dependent American options (basket options, Bermudan swaptions). PDE is preferred for single-asset American options (stocks) where accuracy and Greeks matter. LSM bias can be reduced using more basis functions or dual methods (upper bound). For 1D problems, PDE is typically faster and more accurate. For 3+D, LSM is only practical option.

## Q7: How do you estimate Greeks via Monte Carlo: pathwise vs likelihood ratio method?

**A7:** Greeks are derivatives of option price with respect to parameters. Pathwise method (also called infinitesimal perturbation): differentiate the payoff along each path. For delta: Δ = ∂V/∂S = E[e^(-rT) ∂payoff/∂S]. If payoff = max(S_T - K, 0), then ∂payoff/∂S = (S_T/S_0) × I(S_T > K) for calls (since S_T = S_0 exp(...), derivative is S_T/S_0). Estimate: average e^(-rT) × (S_T/S_0) × I(S_T > K) over paths. Advantages: unbiased, low variance (uses same paths as price). Works when payoff is differentiable (calls, puts). Likelihood ratio method: differentiate the probability density. Delta: Δ = E[e^(-rT) payoff × (∂ln f/∂S_0)], where f is density of S_T. For log-normal: ∂ln f/∂S_0 = (ln(S_T/S_0) - (r-σ²/2)T) / (S_0 σ² T). Estimate: average e^(-rT) × payoff × (score function). Advantages: works for discontinuous payoffs (digital options). Disadvantages: higher variance (multiplies by score, which can be large). Use pathwise when payoff is smooth (most options), likelihood ratio for digitals or barriers. For vega, similar: pathwise differentiates w.r.t. σ in the path, likelihood ratio uses density derivative.

## Q8: What optimization methods are used for model calibration?

**A8:** Calibration finds parameters θ to match market prices: min Σ(C_model(θ) - C_market)². Methods: (1) Levenberg-Marquardt: hybrid of gradient descent and Gauss-Newton, robust for nonlinear least squares. Good default choice. (2) Nelder-Mead (simplex): derivative-free, works when gradients are expensive or noisy. Slow but robust. (3) Genetic algorithms: population-based, good for global optimization (avoid local minima). Slow, use when local optimizers fail. (4) Particle swarm: similar to genetic, swarm intelligence. (5) Simulated annealing: probabilistic, can escape local minima. (6) Quasi-Newton (BFGS, L-BFGS): approximate Hessian, faster than Newton when Hessian is expensive. Good for smooth objectives. (7) Gradient descent: simple but slow, may get stuck in local minima. Considerations: (1) Multiple minima: calibration often has many local minima. Use multiple starts or global optimizers. (2) Regularization: add penalty terms to prevent overfitting (smooth parameters). (3) Constraints: parameters must satisfy bounds (e.g., volatility > 0). Use constrained optimizers. (4) Speed: each function evaluation requires option pricing (expensive). Use fast pricers or approximate models. (5) Stability: parameters shouldn't change dramatically day-to-day. Regularization helps. In practice, Levenberg-Marquardt or BFGS with multiple starts is common.

## Q9: Compare Newton's method versus quasi-Newton methods.

**A9:** Newton's method: uses exact Hessian H, update: x_{n+1} = x_n - H^{-1}∇f. Convergence: quadratic (very fast) near optimum, but requires computing and inverting Hessian (expensive, O(d³) for d dimensions). Quasi-Newton (BFGS, L-BFGS): approximates Hessian using gradient information, update: x_{n+1} = x_n - B_n^{-1}∇f, where B_n approximates H and is updated using secant condition: B_{n+1}(x_{n+1} - x_n) = ∇f_{n+1} - ∇f_n. BFGS stores full approximation (O(d²) memory), L-BFGS stores only recent updates (O(md) memory, m ~ 5-20). Convergence: superlinear (faster than linear, slower than quadratic). Advantages of quasi-Newton: (1) No Hessian computation: only need gradients. (2) Less memory for L-BFGS. (3) Often faster in practice (Hessian computation is expensive). (4) More robust (exact Hessian may be ill-conditioned). Use Newton when: (1) Hessian is cheap to compute (e.g., analytical). (2) Need fastest convergence. (3) Problem is well-conditioned. Use quasi-Newton when: (1) Hessian is expensive (common in calibration, each evaluation requires many option pricings). (2) Problem is large-scale. (3) Gradients are available but Hessian is not. In finance, quasi-Newton (especially L-BFGS) is standard for calibration due to efficiency.

## Q10: When do you need global versus local optimization?

**A10:** Local optimization finds a minimum in a neighborhood (may be local, not global). Global optimization finds the true global minimum. Need global when: (1) Multiple local minima: objective function has many valleys. Calibration often has this (different parameter sets can fit data similarly). (2) Non-convex: objective is not convex, so local methods may get stuck. (3) Initial guess is poor: if you don't know where to start, local methods may converge to wrong minimum. (4) Robustness: want solution that's best overall, not just locally optimal. Methods: (1) Multiple starts: run local optimizer from many random starting points, take best result. Simple, often effective. (2) Genetic algorithms: population-based, explore space broadly. (3) Simulated annealing: probabilistic, can escape local minima. (4) Particle swarm: swarm intelligence. (5) Branch-and-bound: systematic search (for discrete problems). Use local when: (1) Convex or near-convex: local minimum is global. (2) Good initial guess: know roughly where optimum is (e.g., previous day's parameters). (3) Speed matters: global methods are slower. (4) Objective is smooth: local methods work well. In practice, start with local optimization from reasonable initial guess. If results are poor or unstable, use global methods or multiple starts. For calibration, multiple starts of local optimizer is often sufficient.

## Q11: Why is the Mersenne Twister used for random number generation?

**A11:** Mersenne Twister (MT) is a pseudorandom number generator with period 2^19937 - 1 (extremely long). Advantages: (1) Long period: won't repeat for practical purposes (important for long simulations). (2) Good statistical properties: passes many randomness tests (uniform distribution, independence). (3) Fast: efficient implementation. (4) Reproducible: given seed, produces same sequence (useful for debugging, comparing strategies). (5) Widely available: standard in many languages (Python numpy, C++, etc.). Alternatives: (1) Linear congruential generators: shorter period, poorer quality. (2) Cryptographic generators: slower, overkill for simulations. (3) Physical randomness: true random but not reproducible. Limitations: (1) Not cryptographically secure (predictable if you know algorithm and recent outputs). (2) State is large (624 words), but manageable. (3) Some subtle statistical issues in high dimensions, but fine for most applications. For Monte Carlo in finance, MT is standard because it balances speed, quality, and reproducibility. For very high-dimensional problems, consider generators with better high-dimensional properties (Sobol for QMC, or newer generators like PCG).

## Q12: Derive the optimal coefficient for control variates.

**A12:** Control variate method estimates E[X] using Y with known E[Y]. Estimator: X̂_cv = X̄ - c(Ȳ - E[Y]), where c is coefficient. Variance: Var(X̂_cv) = Var(X̄) + c²Var(Ȳ) - 2cCov(X̄, Ȳ) = (1/N)[Var(X) + c²Var(Y) - 2cCov(X,Y)]. Minimize w.r.t. c: differentiate, set to zero: 2cVar(Y) - 2Cov(X,Y) = 0, so c* = Cov(X,Y)/Var(Y) = ρ_{XY} × (σ_X/σ_Y), where ρ is correlation. Optimal variance reduction: Var(X̂_cv) = Var(X̄)(1 - ρ²_{XY}). If |ρ| = 1, variance is zero (perfect control). If |ρ| = 0, no reduction (uncorrelated). If |ρ| = 0.7, variance reduces by 49%. In practice, c* is unknown, so estimate: ĉ = sample Cov(X,Y) / sample Var(Y). This introduces bias (slight), but variance reduction usually outweighs it. For multiple controls, use multivariate regression: X̂ = X̄ - Σc_i(Ȳ_i - E[Y_i]), where c is vector from regression of X on Y's. The key is finding controls highly correlated with payoff but with known expectation.

## Q13: Explain importance sampling for estimating tail probabilities.

**A13:** Importance sampling changes the sampling distribution to focus on rare events. Problem: estimate P(X > a) where a is large (tail probability). Standard MC: generate X ~ f, estimate as fraction with X > a. If P(X > a) is small (e.g., 0.01), need many simulations. Importance sampling: sample from g (different distribution), then reweight: P(X > a) = ∫_{x>a} f(x)dx = ∫_{x>a} [f(x)/g(x)]g(x)dx = E_g[I(X>a) × f(X)/g(X)]. Generate X ~ g, estimate as average of I(X>a) × f(X)/g(X). If g puts more mass near a, variance reduces dramatically. Optimal g (minimizing variance) is g*(x) ∝ I(x>a)f(x) (proportional to f in tail, zero elsewhere), but this requires knowing the answer. Practical approach: (1) Shift mean: if X ~ N(μ, σ²) and a >> μ, use g = N(a, σ²) (center at threshold). (2) Exponential tilting: use g(x) ∝ f(x)e^{θx} and choose θ to center g at a. (3) Asymptotic: use extreme value distribution as g. Variance reduction can be orders of magnitude: if P = 10^-4, standard MC needs 10^6 simulations for one hit. With good importance sampling, may need only 10^3-10^4. Critical: choose g that doesn't make f/g too large (variance can explode if g has thin tails where f is thick).

## Q14: Provide intuition for FFT-based option pricing.

**A14:** FFT pricing uses the fact that option price can be written as convolution: C(k) = e^{-rT}∫_{-∞}^{∞} e^{izk}φ(z-i)dz, where k = ln(K), z is integration variable, φ is characteristic function of log-price. The integral is a Fourier transform. FFT computes this efficiently: (1) Discretize strike grid: k_j = k_0 + jΔk. (2) Use FFT to compute C(k_j) for all strikes simultaneously (O(N log N) instead of O(N²)). (3) Characteristic function φ is known analytically for many models (Black-Scholes, Heston, etc.). Advantages: (1) Fast: price many strikes at once. (2) Works for complex models: any model with known characteristic function (Lévy processes, stochastic volatility). (3) Greeks: can compute by differentiating in Fourier space. Challenges: (1) Discretization: need fine grid for accuracy, but FFT is periodic (aliasing if grid too coarse). (2) Damping: integrand may not decay, need damping factor (multiply by exponential). (3) Strike spacing: FFT gives prices on fixed grid, may need interpolation for specific strikes. Use FFT when: pricing many options (calibration, risk management), or when characteristic function is available but PDF is not. For single option, other methods may be simpler.

## Q15: What are the advantages of the COS method for option pricing?

**A15:** The COS (Cosine) method expands the probability density in cosine series, then uses characteristic function to compute coefficients. Method: (1) Expand density f(x) = Σ_{k=0}^{N} A_k cos(kπ(x-a)/(b-a)) on [a,b]. (2) Coefficients A_k = (2/(b-a))Re[φ(kπ/(b-a))e^{-ikπa/(b-a)}], where φ is characteristic function. (3) Option price = discounted expectation, computed using series. Advantages: (1) Fast convergence: exponential convergence for smooth densities (faster than FFT for smooth cases). (2) Works with characteristic functions: any model with known φ (Heston, Lévy, etc.). (3) Accurate: high precision with few terms (N ~ 50-200 often sufficient). (4) Computes many strikes: once coefficients computed, price all strikes by evaluating series (faster than FFT for multiple strikes). (5) Greeks: easy to compute (differentiate series). (6) Early exercise: can extend to American options. Compared to FFT: COS is often faster and more accurate for smooth densities, but FFT is more general (works for rough densities). COS is preferred for: European options with smooth models (Heston, VG), calibration (need many prices), or when high accuracy is needed. Implementation requires choosing truncation range [a,b] appropriately (based on cumulants).

## Q16: What are the properties of Sobol sequences for quasi-Monte Carlo?

**A16:** Sobol sequences are low-discrepancy sequences (deterministic but fill space evenly). Properties: (1) Low discrepancy: discrepancy is O((log N)^d/N), much better than random O(1/√N) for moderate d. (2) Dimension: each dimension uses different generator (base-2 digital nets). Quality varies by dimension (some dimensions are better than others). (3) N = power of 2: works best when N is power of 2 (e.g., 1024, 2048). For other N, can use first N points (slight degradation). (4) Uniform coverage: points are more evenly distributed than random, reducing clustering. (5) Deterministic: same seed gives same sequence (reproducible). (6) Fast generation: efficient algorithms exist. (7) Scrambling: can randomize Sobol (scrambled Sobol) to get error estimates while keeping low discrepancy. Limitations: (1) Deteriorates with dimension: (log N)^d factor grows, so for d > 20-30, may be worse than MC. (2) Anisotropic: different dimensions have different quality (first dimensions are best). (3) Correlation: points are deterministic, so no statistical independence (but this is fine for QMC). Use Sobol for: moderate-dimensional integrals (d < 20-30), smooth integrands, when faster convergence than MC is needed. Often combined with randomization to get both benefits.

## Q17: How do you design a finite difference grid for option pricing?

**A17:** Grid design affects accuracy and efficiency. Considerations: (1) Domain: S ∈ [S_min, S_max]. For calls, S_min ≈ 0, S_max ≈ 3-5× current price (capture tail). For puts, may need larger S_max. (2) Grid spacing: uniform vs non-uniform. Non-uniform (more points near strike, current price) improves accuracy where it matters. Common: S_i = S_0 exp(x_i) where x_i is uniform (log-space), or use transformation. (3) Number of points: typically 100-500 in S-direction. More points = more accurate but slower. (4) Time steps: for explicit schemes, need Δt < (ΔS)²/(2σ²S²_max) for stability. For implicit (Crank-Nicolson), can use larger steps. Typically 50-200 time steps. (5) Boundary conditions: at S = 0, V = 0 for calls, V = K for puts. At S = S_max, V ≈ S - Ke^{-rT} for calls (deep ITM), V ≈ 0 for puts. For Greeks, use one-sided differences at boundaries. (6) Strike alignment: if possible, place strike on grid point (avoids interpolation). (7) Adaptive: refine grid near discontinuities (payoff kinks). For Black-Scholes, uniform log-grid with ~200 S-points and ~100 time steps is often sufficient. For complex models (stochastic vol), may need finer grids or adaptive methods.

## Q18: What boundary conditions are used in PDE methods for options?

**A18:** Boundary conditions depend on option type and domain. For calls on domain S ∈ [0, S_max]: (1) At S = 0: V(0,t) = 0 (stock worthless, call worthless). (2) At S = S_max: V(S_max,t) ≈ S_max - Ke^{-r(T-t)} (deep ITM, behaves like forward). More precisely: ∂V/∂S = 1 (delta = 1) or V = S - Ke^{-r(T-t)}. (3) At t = T: V(S,T) = max(S-K, 0) (payoff). For puts: (1) At S = 0: V(0,t) = Ke^{-r(T-t)} (stock worthless, put worth K). (2) At S = S_max: V(S_max,t) = 0 (deep OTM). (3) At t = T: V(S,T) = max(K-S, 0). For barriers: at barrier level H, V(H,t) = rebate (if any) for knock-out, or continuity for knock-in. For American options: free boundary (optimal exercise boundary) is part of solution. At each time, V(S,t) = max(payoff(S), continuation value). The boundary is where these are equal. For stochastic volatility models (2D PDE): boundaries in volatility direction: at σ = 0, use limiting behavior; at σ = σ_max, use asymptotic (volatility doesn't affect much when very high). Boundary conditions ensure solution is well-posed and match financial intuition.

## Q19: How do you test convergence of numerical methods?

**A19:** Convergence testing ensures solutions are accurate. Methods: (1) Grid refinement: solve with grid spacing h, then h/2, h/4, etc. Plot error vs h. Should see expected rate (e.g., O(h²) for second-order method). Error = |V_h - V_{h/2}| or |V_h - analytical| if known. (2) Richardson extrapolation: if error is O(h^p), combine solutions at h and h/2 to get higher-order estimate: V_extrapolated = (2^p V_{h/2} - V_h)/(2^p - 1). Compare extrapolated to fine-grid solution. (3) Analytical benchmarks: compare to known solutions (Black-Scholes for European options, etc.). (4) Monte Carlo comparison: for complex payoffs, compare to MC with many simulations (treat MC as "truth" with confidence intervals). (5) Greeks: check if Greeks satisfy relationships (e.g., put-call parity, delta-gamma relationship). (6) Stability: check if small parameter changes cause small solution changes (smoothness). (7) Conservation: for some problems, check if quantities are conserved (e.g., portfolio value). (8) Asymptotic: check behavior in limits (deep ITM/OTM should match analytical). In practice, refine grid until solution changes by less than tolerance (e.g., 0.1% relative error), or until computational cost becomes prohibitive. Document convergence studies for validation.

## Q20: What are common pitfalls in implementing numerical methods for finance?

**A20:** Common pitfalls: (1) Look-ahead bias: using future information (e.g., future volatility) in current step. Ensure all information is from past or current time only. (2) Boundary conditions: wrong boundary conditions cause errors, especially at S = 0 or large S. Check asymptotic behavior. (3) Grid alignment: if strike is between grid points, need interpolation. Better to align strike on grid. (4) Time stepping: explicit schemes require small time steps for stability. Check stability condition. Implicit schemes are safer but more complex. (5) Discretization errors: first-order methods (Euler) have large errors. Use second-order (Crank-Nicolson) when possible. (6) Non-smooth payoffs: kinks (at strike) cause reduced convergence rates. May need local refinement or smoothing. (7) Dimensionality: high-dimensional PDEs (3+) are expensive. Consider MC instead. (8) Calibration: fitting to noisy market data can overfit. Use regularization, out-of-sample testing. (9) Numerical stability: avoid dividing by small numbers, use log-space for very small/large values, check for NaN/Inf. (10) Reproducibility: use fixed seeds, document all parameters, version control code. (11) Greeks: computing Greeks via finite differences of noisy MC estimates amplifies noise. Use pathwise or likelihood ratio methods. (12) Testing: always test on known cases (Black-Scholes) before applying to complex models. The key is understanding method limitations and validating thoroughly.
